# Abstract

Off-policy learning enables a reinforcement learning (RL) agent to reason counterfactually about policies that are not executed and is one of the most important ideas in RL. It, however, can lead to instability when combined with function approximation and bootstrapping, two arguably indispensable ingredients for large-scale reinforcement learning. This is the notorious deadly triad. Gradient Temporal Difference (GTD) is one powerful tool to solve the deadly triad. Its success results from solving a doubling sampling issue indirectly with weight duplication or Fenchel duality. In this paper, we instead propose a direct method to solve the double sampling issue by simply using two samples in a Markovian data stream with an increasing gap. The resulting algorithm is as computationally efficient as GTD but gets rid of GTD's extra weights. The only price we pay is a logarithmically increasing memory as time progresses. We provide both asymptotic and finite sample analysis, where the convergence rate is on-par with the canonical on-policy temporal difference learning. Key to our analysis is a novel refined discretization of limiting ODEs.

Keywords: off-policy reinforcement learning, gradient temporal difference learning, double sampling

# 1 Introduction

Off-policy learning (Watkins, 1989; Precup et al., 2000; Maei, 2011; Sutton et al., 2016; Li, 2019) is arguably one of the most important ideas in reinforcement learning (RL, Sutton and Barto (2018)). Different from on-policy learning (Sutton, 1988), where an RL agent learns quantities of interest of a policy by executing the policy itself, an off-policy RL agent learns quantities of interest of a policy by executing a different policy. Here the policy of interest is called the target policy. The policy that is actually executed is called the behavior policy. This flexibility offers additional safety (Dulac-Arnold et al., 2019) and data efficiency (Lin, 1992; Sutton et al., 2011).

Off-policy learning, however, can lead to instability if combined with function approximation and bootstrapping, two other arguably indispensable techniques for any large-scale RL applications. The idea of function approximation (Sutton, 1988) is to represent quantities of interest with parameterized functions instead of look-up tables. The idea of bootstrapping (Sutton, 1988) is to construct update targets for an estimator by using the

estimator itself recursively. This instability results from off-policy learning, function approximation, and bootstrapping is called the deadly triad (Baird, 1995; Sutton and Barto, 2018; Zhang, 2022).

Gradient Temporal Difference learning (GTD, Sutton et al. (2008, 2009); Maei (2011); Zhang et al. (2021a)) is a family of algorithms that address the deadly triad via minimizing some objectives with stochastic gradient descent. Provided that a linear architecture is used for approximation, the objectives are convex and the analytical expression of the gradient can be easily computed, which typically involves a matrix product in the form of  $\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{A}_t^\top\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{A}_t\right]$ . Notably, the expectation here is independent of  $t$ . At time step  $t$ , we do have access to  $\hat{A}_t$ . However, we cannot use  $\hat{A}_t^\top\hat{A}_t$  as an estimator for  $\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{A}_t^\top\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{A}_t\right]$ , since, in general cases,

$$
\mathbb {E} \left[ \hat {A} _ {t} ^ {\top} \hat {A} _ {t} \right] \neq \mathbb {E} \left[ \hat {A} _ {t} ^ {\top} \right] \mathbb {E} \left[ \hat {A} _ {t} \right].
$$

This is the notorious double sampling issue (Baird, 1995; Sutton et al., 2008), i.e., we cannot use  $\hat{A}_t$  as samples of both expectations. GTD algorithms address this double sampling issue indirectly and can be derived from two different perspectives. The first is to approximate one expectation with an additional estimator and use  $\hat{A}_t$  as a sample of the other expectation (Sutton et al., 2008, 2009). The second is to transform the original convex optimization problem into a convex-concave saddle-point problem via Fenchel's duality (Liu et al., 2015). Primal-dual methods are then applied to solve this convex-concave saddle-point problem, which does not involve any double sampling issue. Those two perspectives result in exactly the same algorithm. The asymptotic and finite sample analyses of GTD algorithms have been well established (Sutton et al., 2008, 2009; Liu et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2017; Yu, 2017). Both perspectives, however, result in an extra weight vector to learn, making GTD algorithms hard to use in practice (Sutton et al., 2008).

In this paper, we propose a new direct method to address the double sampling issue. The fundamental cause of the double sampling issue is that

$$
\mathbb {E} \left[ X Y \right] = \mathbb {E} \left[ X \right] \mathbb {E} \left[ Y \right]
$$

typically does not hold if  $X$  and  $Y$  are not independent - clearly  $\hat{A}_t$  is not independent with itself. However, suppose that  $\left\{\hat{A}_t\right\}$  is generated by an underlying Markov Decision Process, the correlation between  $\hat{A}_t$  and  $\hat{A}_{t + \tau}$  diminishes to zero geometrically fast with the increase of  $\tau$  under some mild conditions (see, e.g., Vempala (2005)). Motivated by this, we propose to use  $\hat{A}_{t + \tau}^{\top}\hat{A}_t$  as an estimator for  $\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{A}_t^\top\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{A}_t\right]$ . Of course, we need to keep  $\tau$  increasing because the correlation diminishes only asymptotically. But we prove that  $\tau$  does not need to increase so fast. In fact, setting  $\tau$  to  $\Omega(\ln^2(t))$  is sufficient to guarantee convergence. We term our new method Direct GTD. Different from GTD, Direct GTD does not need the additional weight. The price we pay is a logarithmically increasing memory as time progresses, which we argue is a reasonably small overhead. Our asymptotic analysis of Direct GTD is inspired by the proof structure of Proposition 4.7 in Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis (1996) but is much more involved to work with the increasing gap (i.e.,  $\tau$ ). Key to our analysis is a novel refined discretization of limiting ODEs. We also provide a finite sample

analysis of a variant of Direct GTD, which involves an additional projection operator at every step like Bhandari et al. (2018); Zou et al. (2019). Our convergence rate is on par with the canonical convergence rate of the on-policy TD with linear function approximation established in Bhandari et al. (2018), up to a logarithmic factor.

# 2 Background

In this paper, all vectors are columns. A matrix  $M$  (not necessarily symmetric) is said to be positive definite (p.d.) if there exists a constant  $\lambda > 0$  such that  $x^{\top}Mx \geq \lambda x^{\top}x$  holds for any  $x$ .  $M$  is negative definite (n.d.) if and only if  $-M$  is p.d. For a vector  $x$  and a p.d. matrix  $M$ , we use  $\| x\|_M \doteq \sqrt{x^{\top}Mx}$  to denote the vector norm induced by  $M$ . We also use  $\| \cdot \|_M$  to denote the corresponding induced matrix norm. We use  $\| \cdot \|$  as shorthand for  $\| \cdot \|_I$  where  $I$  is the identity matrix, i.e.,  $\| \cdot \|$  is the standard  $\ell_2$ -norm. We use  $diag(x)$  to denote a diagonal matrix whose diagonal entry is  $x$  and write  $\| \cdot \|_x$  as shorthand for  $\| \cdot \|_{diag(x)}$  when  $diag(x)$  is p.d. We use functions and vectors interchangeably when it does not confuse, e.g., if  $f$  is a function from  $S$  to  $\mathbb{R}$ , we also use  $f$  to denote a vector in  $\mathbb{R}^{|S|}$ , whose  $s$ -th element is  $f(s)$ .

We consider an infinite horizon Markov Decision Process (MDP, Puterman (2014)) with a finite state space  $S$ , a finite action space  $A$ , a reward function  $r: S \times A \to \mathbb{R}$ , a transition function  $p: S \times S \times A \to [0,1]$ , and a discount factor  $\gamma \in [0,1)$ . At time step 0, a state  $S_0$  is sampled from some initial distribution  $p_0$ . At time step  $t$ , an agent at a state  $S_t$  takes an action  $A_t \sim \pi(\cdot | S_t)$ . Here  $\pi: A \times S \to [0,1]$  is the policy being followed. A reward  $R_{t+1} \doteq r(S_t, A_t)$  is then emitted, and a successor state  $S_{t+1}$  is sampled from  $p(\cdot | S_t, A_t)$ .

The return at time step  $t$  is defined as

$$
G _ {t} \doteq \sum_ {i = 0} ^ {\infty} \gamma^ {i} R _ {t + i + 1},
$$

which allows us to define the state value function as

$$
v _ {\pi} (s) \doteq \mathbb {E} _ {\pi , p} \left[ G _ {t} | S _ {t} = s \right].
$$

The value function  $v_{\pi}$  is the unique fixed point of the Bellman operator

$$
\mathcal {T} _ {\pi} v \doteq r _ {\pi} + \gamma P _ {\pi} v.
$$

Here  $r_{\pi} \in \mathbb{R}^{|S|}$  is the reward vector induced by  $\pi$ , defined as  $r_{\pi}(s) \doteq \sum_{a} \pi(a|s)r(s,a)$ .  $P_{\pi} \in \mathbb{R}^{|S| \times |S|}$  is the transition matrix induced by  $\pi$ , i.e.,  $P_{\pi}(s,s') \doteq \sum_{a} \pi(a|s)p(s'|s,a)$ .

Estimating  $v_{\pi}$  is one of the most important tasks in RL and is called policy evaluation. Temporal Difference learning (TD, Sutton (1988)) is one of the most powerful tools for policy evaluation. Let  $V$  be our estimate for  $v_{\pi}$ , then TD updates  $V$  iteratively as

$$
V (S _ {t}) \leftarrow V (S _ {t}) + \alpha_ {t} \left(R _ {t + 1} + \gamma V (S _ {t + 1}) - V (S _ {t})\right), \tag {1}
$$

where  $\{\alpha_t\}$  is a sequence of learning rate. Under mild conditions, the estimate  $V$  converges to  $v_{\pi}$  almost surely (Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis, 1996). In TD, the update target for  $V(S_{t})$

is constructed as  $R_{t + 1} + \gamma V(S_{t + 1})$ , which includes the estimate  $V$  itself. This is called bootstrapping.

When the state space  $\mathcal{S}$  is too large, maintaining a look-up table  $V$  is impractical, and one thus resorts to approximate methods. In particular, consider a feature function  $x: \mathcal{S} \to \mathbb{R}^K$  that maps a state  $s$  to a  $K$ -dimensional feature  $x(s)$ . We then use  $x(s)^{\top} w$  to approximate  $v_{\pi}(s)$ . Here  $w \in \mathbb{R}^K$  is the learnable weight, and typically  $K$  is much smaller than  $|S|$ . Let  $X \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}| \times K}$  be the feature matrix, whose  $s$ -th row is  $x(s)^{\top}$ . The goal is then to adapt  $w$  such that  $Xw \approx v_{\pi}$ . Such approximation architecture is called linear function approximation. Linear TD (Sutton, 1988) updates  $w$  iteratively as

$$
w _ {t + 1} \dot {=} w _ {t} + \alpha_ {t} \left(R _ {t + 1} + \gamma x _ {t + 1} ^ {\top} w _ {t} - x _ {t} ^ {\top} w _ {t}\right) x _ {t}. \tag {2}
$$

Here we use  $x_{t} \doteq x(S_{t})$  as shorthand. Under mild conditions, the iterates  $\{w_{t}\}$  in (2) converge to  $w_{\infty}$  (Tsitsiklis and Roy, 1996), the minimizer of the Mean Squared Projected Bellman Error (MSPBE) defined as

$$
\mathrm {M S P B E} (w) \doteq \| \Pi_ {d _ {\pi}} (\mathcal {T} _ {\pi} X w - X w) \| _ {d _ {\pi}} ^ {2}.
$$

Here  $d_{\pi}$  denotes the stationary state distribution of the Markov chain induced by  $\pi$ .  $\Pi_{d_{\pi}}$  is a projection operator that projects any vector in  $\mathbb{R}^{|S|}$  to  $\operatorname{col}(X)$ , the column space of  $X$ , i.e.,

$$
\Pi_ {d _ {\pi}} v \doteq \arg \min _ {v ^ {\prime} \in \operatorname {c o l} (X)} \left\| v ^ {\prime} - v \right\| _ {d _ {\pi}} ^ {2}.
$$

The update in (2) can be rearranged as

$$
w _ {t + 1} = w _ {t} + \alpha_ {t} \left(x _ {t} \left(\gamma x _ {t + 1} ^ {\top} - x _ {t} ^ {\top}\right) w _ {t} + x _ {t} R _ {t + 1}\right).
$$

If  $\{w_t\}$  converges, the expected update must diminish. The limiting point  $w_{\infty}$  therefore, must satisfy

$$
\mathbb {E} _ {s \sim d _ {\pi (\cdot)}, a \sim \pi (\cdot | s), s ^ {\prime} \sim p (\cdot | s, a)} \left[ x (s) \left(\gamma x (s ^ {\prime}) - x (s)\right) ^ {\top} \right] w _ {\infty} + \mathbb {E} _ {s \sim d _ {\pi (\cdot)}, a \sim \pi (\cdot | s)} \left[ r (s, a) x (s) \right] = 0. (3)
$$

See Tsitsiklis and Roy (1996) for formal proof.

It is common the case that we want to estimate  $v_{\pi}$  without actually sampling actions from  $\pi$ , due to various concerns, e.g., safety (Dulac-Arnold et al., 2019), data efficiency (Lin, 1992; Sutton et al., 2011). Off-policy learning makes this possible. In particular, instead of sampling  $A_{t}$  according  $\pi(\cdot|S_{t})$ , off-policy method samples  $A_{t}$  according to another policy  $\mu$ . Here, the policy  $\pi$  is called the target policy and the policy  $\mu$  is called the behavior policy. For the rest of the paper, we always consider the off-policy setting, i.e.,

$$
A _ {t} \sim \mu (\cdot | S _ {t}), R _ {t + 1} = r (S _ {t}, A _ {t}), S _ {t + 1} \sim p (\cdot | S _ {t}, A _ {t}). \tag {4}
$$

Since the behavior policy  $\mu$  is different from the target policy  $\pi$ , importance sampling ratio is used to account for this discrepancy, which is defined as

$$
\rho (s, a) \doteq \frac {\pi (a | s)}{\mu (a | s)}.
$$

In particular, we use as shorthand  $\rho_t \doteq \rho(S_t, A_t)$ . In the off-policy setting, the TD update (1) becomes

$$
V (S _ {t}) \gets V (S _ {t}) + \alpha_ {t} \rho_ {t} \left(R _ {t + 1} + \gamma V (S _ {t + 1}) - V (S _ {t})\right).
$$

Under mild conditions, the  $V$  in off-policy TD converges to  $v_{\pi}$  almost surely (Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis, 1996). Correspondingly, linear TD (2) in the off-policy setting becomes

$$
w _ {t + 1} \dot {=} w _ {t} + \alpha_ {t} \rho_ {t} \left(R _ {t + 1} + \gamma x _ {t + 1} ^ {\top} w _ {t} - x _ {t} ^ {\top} w _ {t}\right) x _ {t}. \tag {5}
$$

Unfortunately, the possible divergence of off-policy linear TD (5) is well documented (Baird, 1995; Sutton et al., 2016; Sutton and Barto, 2018). This divergence exercises the deadly triad.

Similar to (3), if off-policy linear TD (5) converged, it would converge to a  $w_{*}$  (Sutton et al., 2008) satisfying

$$
A w _ {*} + b = 0, \tag {6}
$$

where

$$
A \dot {=} \mathbb {E} _ {s \sim d _ {\mu} (\cdot), a \sim \pi (\cdot | s), s ^ {\prime} \sim p (\cdot | s, a)} \left[ x (s) \left(\gamma x (s ^ {\prime}) - x (s)\right) ^ {\top} \right] = X ^ {\top} D _ {\mu} (\gamma P _ {\pi} - I) X \tag {7}
$$

$$
b \dot {=} \mathbb {E} _ {s \sim d _ {\mu} (\cdot), a \sim \pi (\cdot | s)} [ r (s, a) x (s) ] = X ^ {\top} D _ {\mu} r _ {\pi}.
$$

Here  $d_{\mu}$  is the stationary distribution of the Markov chain induced by the behavior policy  $\mu$ , and  $D_{\mu} \doteq diag(d_{\mu})$ .

Instead of using off-policy linear TD (5), one natural approach for policy evaluation in the off-policy setting is then to solve

$$
A w + b = 0
$$

directly, probably with stochastic gradient descent on the objective

$$
L (w) \doteq \| A w + b \| ^ {2}.
$$

The objective  $L(w)$  dates back to Yao and Liu (2008) and is termed as the norm of the expected  $TD$  update (NEU) in Sutton et al. (2009). The gradient of  $L(w)$  can be easily computed (Yao and Liu, 2008; Sutton et al., 2009) as

$$
\nabla L (w) = 2 A ^ {\top} (A w + b).
$$

One can, therefore, update  $w$  as

$$
w _ {t + 1} \doteq w _ {t} - \alpha_ {t} A ^ {\top} (A w _ {t} + b). \tag {8}
$$

Since we do not know  $A$  and  $b$ , we need to estimate  $A^{\top}(Aw_t + b)$  with samples. According to the convergence theorem (Levin and Peres, 2017) or the strong law of large number on stationary processes (Doob, 1953),

$$
\hat {A} _ {t} \doteq \rho_ {t} x _ {t} \left(\gamma x _ {t + 1} - x _ {t}\right) ^ {\top},
$$

$$
\hat {b} _ {t} \doteq \rho_ {t} R _ {t + 1} x _ {t}
$$

are good estimates of  $A$  and  $b$  respectively since they are unbiased asymptotically. However,  $\hat{A}_t^\top \hat{A}_t$  is not a good estimator for  $A^\top A$  due to the aforementioned double sampling issue.

Sutton et al. (2008) attack this issue by introducing a second approximator. Namely, since

$$
\nabla L (w) = A ^ {\top} (A w + b) = A ^ {\top} X ^ {\top} D _ {\mu} \left(\mathcal {T} _ {\pi} (X w) - X w\right),
$$

Sutton et al. (2008) use  $\nu \in \mathbb{R}^{K}$  to approximate  $X^{\top}D_{\mu}(\mathcal{T}_{\pi}(Xw) - Xw)$ . For a given  $w$ , the learning of  $\nu$  reduces to a weighted least square. Sutton et al. (2008) then use  $\hat{A}_t^\top \nu_t$  as an estimate of  $\nabla L(w)$ . The resulting GTD algorithm makes the following iterative updates in the off-policy setting with the sequential data  $\{S_0, A_0, R_1, S_1, \ldots\}$ :

$$
\delta_ {t} \doteq R _ {t + 1} + \gamma x _ {t + 1} ^ {\top} w _ {t} - x _ {t} ^ {\top} w _ {t}, \tag {9}
$$

$$
\nu_ {t + 1} \dot {=} \nu_ {t} + \alpha_ {t} (\rho_ {t} \delta_ {t} x _ {t} - \nu_ {t}),
$$

$$
w _ {t + 1} \dot {=} w _ {t} + \alpha_ {t} \rho_ {t} (x _ {t} - \gamma x _ {t + 1}) x _ {t} ^ {\top} \nu_ {t}.
$$

Following Liu et al. (2015), we can also apply Fenchel's duality

$$
u ^ {\top} u = \max _ {\nu} 2 u ^ {\top} \nu - \nu^ {\top} \nu
$$

to  $L(w)$  and get

$$
L (w) = \max _ {\nu} 2 (A w + b) ^ {\top} \nu - \nu^ {\top} \nu .
$$

The original convex optimization problem of minimizing  $L(w)$  now reduces to finding the saddle-point of the convex-concave function

$$
L (w, \nu) \doteq 2 (A w + b) ^ {\top} \nu - \nu^ {\top} \nu .
$$

Applying primal-dual methods (Nemirovski et al., 2009) then leads to the same GTD update (9). The convergence and finite sample analysis of GTD in (9) is well established (Sutton et al., 2008, 2009; Liu et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2017; Yu, 2017).

# 3 Direct Gradient Temporal Difference Learning

GTD algorithms address the double sampling issue indirectly by employing an additional weight vector. We instead use two samples directly. As discussed in Section 1, we use  $\hat{A}_{t + f(t)}^{\top}\left(\hat{A}_t w_t + \hat{b}_t\right)$  as an estimate for  $A^{\top}(Aw + b)$ , yielding the Direct GTD algorithm:

$$
w _ {t + 1} \doteq w _ {t} + \alpha_ {t} \rho_ {t + f (t)} \left(x _ {t + f (t)} - \gamma x _ {t + f (t) + 1}\right) x _ {t + f (t)} ^ {\top} \rho_ {t} \left(R _ {t + 1} + \gamma x _ {t + 1} ^ {\top} w _ {t} - x _ {t} ^ {\top} w _ {t}\right) x _ {t}, (1 0)
$$

where the data stream (10) operates on is described in (4) and  $f: \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}$  is the gap function. We require  $f$  being monotonically increasing. In Direct GTD (10), the term  $\rho_{t + f(t)}\rho_t\left(R_{t + 1} + \gamma x_{t + 1}^\top w_t - x_t^\top w_t\right)$  is a scalar, the computational complexity of which is

only  $\mathcal{O}(K)$ . If we compute the remaining term  $(x_{t + f(t)} - \gamma x_{t + f(t) + 1})x_{t + f(t)}^{\top}x_{t}$  from right to left, the computational complexity is still  $\mathcal{O}(K)$ . In other words, the computational complexity of Direct GTD is the same as existing GTD methods. The price we pay here is that we cannot start Direct GTD until the  $(f(0) + 1)$ -th step, and we need to maintain a memory storing

$$
x _ {t}, \rho_ {t}, x _ {t + 1}, \rho_ {t + 1}, \dots x _ {f (t)}, \rho_ {f (t)}, x _ {f (t) + 1}. \tag {11}
$$

The size of this memory is  $\mathcal{O}(f(t))$ . We will soon prove that the memory can be as small as  $\Omega(\ln^2(t))$ . We argue that this memory overhead is reasonably small. The gain is that we now do not need an additional weight vector, making the algorithm easy to use.

# 4 Asymptotic Convergence Analysis

In this section, we provide an asymptotic convergence analysis of Direct GTD (10). The major technical challenge lies in the increasing gap function. If  $f(t)$  was a constant function, say  $f(t) \equiv t_0$ , then one would be able to easily analyze Direct GTD via constructing an augmented Markov chain with states

$$
Y _ {t} \doteq \left\{S _ {t}, A _ {t}, \dots , S _ {t + t _ {0}}, A _ {t + t _ {0}}, S _ {t + t _ {0} + 1} \right\}
$$

evolving in a finite space  $(\mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{A})^{t_0 + 1} \times \mathcal{S}$ . Suppose the origin Markov chain  $\{S_t\}$  is ergodic, this new chain  $\{Y_t\}$  would also be ergodic. Classical convergence results (e.g., Proposition 4.7 of Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis (1996)) can then take over. When  $f(t)$  is increasing, the augmented chain, however, would be

$$
Y _ {t} \doteq \left\{S _ {t}, A _ {t}, \dots , S _ {t + f (t)}, A _ {t + f (t)}, S _ {t + f (t) + 1} \right\} \tag {12}
$$

which evolves in an infinite space

$$
\cup_ {i = 1} ^ {\infty} (\mathcal {S} \times \mathcal {A}) ^ {i} \times \mathcal {S}. \tag {13}
$$

Even if the origin chain  $\{S_t\}$  is ergodic, the new chain  $\{Y_t\}$  still behaves poorly in that it never visits the same augmented state twice. This rules out the possibility of applying most, if not all, existing convergence results in the stochastic approximation community (e.g., Benveniste et al. (1990); Kushner and Yin (2003); Borkar (2009)). Our main technical novelty lies in a refined discretization of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) to deal with this increasing gap.

To better understand our technical novelty, we first give a holistic view of standard ODE-based convergence analysis, following Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis (1996). Consider a general stochastic approximation algorithm

$$
w _ {t + 1} = w _ {t} + \alpha_ {t} h \left(w _ {t}, Y _ {t}\right). \tag {14}
$$

Consider a sequence  $\{t_m\}_{m = 0,1,\ldots}$  of increasing time steps. Expanding the right-hand side of (14) yields

$$
w _ {t _ {m + 1} + 1} = w _ {t _ {m}} + \sum_ {i = t _ {m}} ^ {t _ {m + 1}} \alpha_ {i} h (w _ {i}, Y _ {i}).
$$

Assuming the learning rates  $\{\alpha_{i}\}$  are small enough, then  $w_{i}$  changes little for  $i\in [t_m,t_{m + 1}]$  and the randomness in  $\{Y_i\}$  is averaged out. This leads to the following approximation

$$
h (w _ {i}, Y _ {i}) \approx \bar {h} (w _ {t _ {m}}),
$$

where  $\bar{h}$  is the expectation of  $h$  w.r.t. some proper distribution. Consequently,

$$
w _ {t _ {m + 1} + 1} \approx w _ {t _ {m}} + h (w _ {t _ {m}}) \sum_ {i = t _ {m}} ^ {t _ {m + 1}} \alpha_ {i}.
$$

In the standard analysis (e.g., Benveniste et al. (1990); Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis (1996); Kushner and Yin (2003); Borkar (2009)), the sequence  $\{t_m\}$  is selected such that  $\forall m$

$$
\sum_ {i = t _ {m}} ^ {t _ {m + 1}} \alpha_ {i} \approx T, \tag {15}
$$

where  $T$  is some fixed constant. So we have

$$
w _ {t _ {m + 1} + 1} \approx w _ {t _ {m}} + h (w _ {t _ {m}}) T.
$$

This corresponds to Euler's discretization of the ODE

$$
\frac {\mathrm {d} w (t)}{\mathrm {d} t} = h (w (t)) \tag {16}
$$

with an interval  $T$ . The behavior of the stochastic and discrete iterates  $\{w_t\}$  generated by (14) can then be associated with a deterministic and continuous trajectory of (16). This approach has dominated existing ODE-based convergence analysis for more than twenty years (see, e.g., Benveniste et al. (1990); Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis (1996); Kushner and Yin (2003); Borkar (2009)). However, such a discretization cannot help analyze our Direct GTD, mostly because we have an increasing gap function and the augmented Markov chain (cf. (13)) is much more complicated than standard ones (cf. (12)). We address this challenge by conducting a refined discretization. Instead of performing discretization w.r.t. a fixed interval  $T$  (cf. (15)), we perform a refined discretization w.r.t. a sequence of intervals  $\{T_m\}$ , namely,

$$
\sum_ {i = t _ {m}} ^ {t _ {m + 1}} \alpha_ {i} \approx T _ {m}.
$$

This is the most important ingredient in our convergence analysis. Designing a proper  $\{T_m\}$  according to  $f(t)$  and  $\{\alpha_t\}$  is the major challenge here. On the one hand,  $\{T_m\}$  has to diminish fast enough such that the noise can average out properly. On the other hand,  $\{T_m\}$  has to diminish slowly enough such that the gap between  $t_m$  and  $t_{m+1}$  actually increases to account for the increase of  $f(t)$ . Carefully balancing the learning rates  $\{\alpha_t\}$ , the endpoints  $\{t_m\}$ , and the intervals  $\{T_m\}$  is the major technical challenge.

Besides the refined discretization, another important ingredient is the mixing of joint state distributions in Markov chains. Consider a general Markov chain  $\{Y_t\}$ . Assume the

chain is ergodic and let  $d_{\mathcal{V}}$  denote its invariant distribution. Then the convergence theorem (see, e.g., Levin and Peres (2017)) yields

$$
\lim  _ {t \to \infty} \Pr (Y _ {t} = y) \to d _ {\mathcal {Y}} (y).
$$

This convergence is uniform in  $y$  and is geometrically fast. Exploiting this convergence, we are able to prove the convergence of joint state distributions, i.e.,

$$
\lim _ {t \to \infty} \operatorname * {P r} \big (Y _ {t} = y, Y _ {t + f (t)} = y ^ {\prime} \big) \to d _ {\mathcal {Y}} (y) d _ {\mathcal {Y}} (y ^ {\prime}).
$$

Intuitively, this means the dependence between  $Y_{t}$  and  $Y_{t + f(t)}$  diminishes as  $t$  goes to infinity (cf. Lemma 7.1 in Vempala (2005)). In Direct GTD, this means the bias resulting from estimating  $A^{\top}A$  with  $\hat{A}_{t + f(t)}^{\top}\hat{A}_t$  diminishes gradually.

Having introduced the two main ingredients in our analysis, we are now ready to formally describe our main results. We start with (standard) assumptions we make.

Assumption 4.1 The Markov chain induced by the behavior policy  $\mu$  is finite, irreducible, and aperiodic. The behavior policy  $\mu$  covers the target policy  $\pi$ , i.e.,  $\forall (s,a), \pi (a|s) > 0 \Rightarrow \mu (a|s) > 0$ .

Assumption 4.2 The feature matrix  $X$  has a full column rank. The matrix  $A$  defined in (7) is nonsingular.

Assumptions 4.1 and 4.2 are standard in the analysis of linear TD methods (see, e.g., Tsitsiklis and Roy (1996); Wang et al. (2017)).

Assumption 4.3 The learning rates  $\{\alpha_t\}$  have the form of

$$
\alpha_ {t} = \frac {C _ {\alpha}}{(t + 1) ^ {- \nu}}
$$

for some  $\nu \in (\frac{2}{3},1]$

Assumption 4.3 considers learning rates of a specific form. This is mostly for ease of presentation.

Assumption 4.4 The gap function  $f(t): \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}$  is monotonically increasing and satisfies  $\forall \chi \in [0,1)$ ,

$$
\sum_ {t = 0} ^ {\infty} \chi^ {f (t)} <   \infty .
$$

Moreover, there exist constants  $\tau \in (0,\frac{3\nu - 2}{2\nu})$  and  $C_{\tau} > 0$  such that  $\forall t$

$$
f (t) \leq C _ {\tau} \alpha_ {t} ^ {- \tau}.
$$

Assumption 4.4 is the most "unnatural" assumption we make and prescribes how the gap function should be chosen. Intuitively, those conditions prevent the gap function from growing too fast. Despite seemingly complicated, Lemma 15 confirms that simply setting

$$
f (t) = \lfloor h (t) \ln (t + 1) \rfloor \tag {17}
$$

with some non-negative increasing function  $h(\cdot)$  converging to  $\infty$  as  $t \to \infty$  fulfills the first condition of Assumption 4.4. Here  $\lfloor x \rfloor$  is the floor function denoting the largest integer that is smaller than  $x$ . A concrete example satisfying Assumption 4.3 and 4.4 is

$$
\nu = 1, h (t) = \ln (t + 1), f (t) = \left\lfloor \ln^ {2} (t + 1) \right\rfloor , \tau = 0. 1. \tag {18}
$$

We are now ready to present our main results.

Theorem 1 Let Assumptions 4.1, 4.2, 4.3, & 4.4 hold. Then the iterates  $\{w_t\}$  generated by Direct GTD (10) satisfies

$$
\lim  _ {t \to \infty} w _ {t} = w _ {*} \quad a l m o s t s u r e l y,
$$

where  $w_{*}$  is the TD fixed point defined in (6).

The proof of Theorem 1 is provided in Section A.2. Theorem 1 confirms that asymptotically, Direct GTD finds the same solution as the canonical GTD methods.

# 5 Finite Sample Analysis

Theorem 1 confirms the asymptotic convergence of Direct GTD. The price we pay is a memory of size  $\Omega (\ln t)$  (cf. (11)). If the convergence is fast, e.g.,  $\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{t}\right)$ , the memory increases reasonably slowly, and we argue that the memory overhead is acceptable. If, however, the convergence is too slow, the memory may still become too large. To make sure that Direct GTD is a practical algorithm, therefore, requires performing a finite sample analysis. To this end, we, in this section, perform finite sample analysis of a variant of Direct GTD, which adopts an additional projection operator and updates  $\{w_t\}$  iteratively as

$$
w _ {t + 1} \doteq \Gamma \left(w _ {t} + \alpha_ {t} \rho_ {t + f (t)} \left(x _ {t + f (t)} - \gamma x _ {t + f (t) + 1}\right) x _ {t + f (t)} ^ {\top} \rho_ {t} \left(R _ {t + 1} + \gamma x _ {t + 1} ^ {\top} w _ {t} - x _ {t} ^ {\top} w _ {t}\right) x _ {t}\right), \tag {19}
$$

where  $\Gamma :\mathbb{R}^K\to \mathbb{R}^K$  is a projection operator onto a ball of a radius  $B$ , i.e.,

$$
\Gamma (w) \doteq \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} w, & \| w \| \leq B \\ \frac {B}{\| w \|} w, & \| w \| > B \end{array} . \right. \tag {20}
$$

The update (19) differs from Direct GTD (10) only in that it adopts an additional projection operator  $\Gamma$ . We, therefore, call it Projected Direct GTD. We show that the convergence rate of our off-policy Projected Direct GTD is on-par with the convergence rate of the canonical on-policy linear TD in Bhandari et al. (2018), up to a few logarithm terms.

Notably, adding a projection operator is a common practice in finite sample analysis of TD algorithms (see, e.g., Liu et al. (2015); Wang et al. (2017); Bhandari et al. (2018); Zou

et al. (2019)) to simplify the presentation. Techniques from Srikant and Ying (2019) can indeed be used to perform finite sample analysis of the original Direct GTD directly. This, however, complicates the presentation, and we, therefore, leave it for future work. We now present our main results.

Theorem 2 Let  $B$  in (20) be large enough such that  $\| w_{*} \| \leq B$ . Consider learning rates in the form of

$$
\alpha_ {t} = \frac {C _ {\alpha}}{t + 1}.
$$

Let Assumptions 4.1, 4.2, & 4.4 hold. Then there exists a constant  $C_0$  such that as long as  $C_{\alpha} \geq C_0$ , the iterates  $\{w_t\}$  generated by Projected Direct GTD (19) satisfy

$$
\mathbb {E} \left[ \| w _ {t} - w _ {*} \| ^ {2} \right] = \mathcal {O} \left(\frac {f (t) \ln (t)}{t}\right).
$$

The proof of Theorem 2 is provided in Section A.3 and is a refined analysis of Bhandari et al. (2018). Theorem 2, together with (17), together confirms that the convergence rate of Projected Direct GTD is reasonably fast. In particular, if the configuration in (18) is used, the convergence rate of Direct GTD is on-par with the on-policy linear TD (Bhandari et al., 2018) up to logarithmic factors.

# 6 Direct GTD with I.I.D. Data

So far, we consider only Markovian data (4). Most GTD works, however, start with i.i.d. data (Sutton et al., 2008, 2009). In other words, we are presented with a data stream

$$
\left\{(S _ {k}, A _ {k}, R _ {k}, S _ {k} ^ {\prime}) \right\} _ {k = 0, 1, \ldots},
$$

where

$$
S _ {k} \sim d _ {\mu}, A _ {k} \sim \mu (\cdot | S _ {k}), R _ {k} \doteq r (S _ {k}, A _ {k}), S _ {k} ^ {\prime} \sim p (\cdot | S _ {k}, A _ {k}).
$$

Direct GTD with i.i.d. data is trivial. Starting from (8), one can use  $\iota_k \doteq (S_k, A_k, R_k, S'_k)$  to estimate  $A^\top$  and use  $\iota_{k+1} \doteq (S_{k+1}, A_{k+1}, R_{k+1}, S'_{k+1})$  to estimate  $A$ . The product of the two estimates is an unbiased estimation of  $A^\top A$  since  $\iota_k$  and  $\iota_{k+1}$  are independent of each other. In particular, this would lead to the following update:

$$
k = 2 t, \tag {21}
$$

$$
\Delta_ {t, 1} = \frac {\pi (A _ {k} | S _ {k})}{\mu (A _ {k} | S _ {k})} \left(x (S _ {k}) - \gamma x (S _ {k} ^ {\prime})\right) x ^ {\top} (S _ {k}),
$$

$$
\Delta_ {t, 2} = \frac {\pi (A _ {k + 1} | S _ {k + 1})}{\mu (A _ {k + 1} | S _ {k + 1})} \left(R _ {k + 1} + \gamma x ^ {\top} (S _ {k + 1} ^ {\prime}) w _ {t} - x ^ {\top} (S _ {k + 1}) w _ {t}\right) x (S _ {k + 1}),
$$

$$
w _ {t + 1} = w _ {t} + \alpha_ {t} \Delta_ {t, 1} \Delta_ {t, 2}.
$$

This idea here has also been explored in Zhang et al. (2021a) to address a similar double sampling issue. Thanks to the i.i.d. nature of the data, the asymptotic convergence and

finite sample analysis of (21) is a straightforward copy of those in Liu et al. (2015); Sutton et al. (2008, 2009); Zhang et al. (2021a). We, therefore, omit them to avoid verbatim repetition.

The most common criticism is that it is not clear where to get those i.i.d. data since, after all, the canonical RL framework is Markovian (Sutton and Barto, 2018). We, therefore, mainly focus on the Markovian setting in this work, which is much more practical than the i.i.d. setting. Notably, if the i.i.d. data is indeed available, the Direct GTD algorithm can be easily extended to the more broad Preconditioned TD (Yao and Liu, 2008) algorithms. Namely, we can add a preconditioner  $M$  in the update (8), yielding

$$
w _ {t + 1} \doteq w _ {t} - \alpha_ {t} M A ^ {\top} (A w _ {t} + b).
$$

There are multiple choices for this  $M$ . We refer the reader to Yao and Liu (2008) for more details. Estimating  $MA^{\top}A$  is now a triple sampling problem, solving, which remains trivial if i.i.d. data is available. Different  $M$  may result in different performances, which is out of the scope of this work and is left for future work.

# 8 Related Work

In this paper, we propose a new method to implement the idea of GTD. The family of GTD algorithms has been widely studied, including Sutton et al. (2008, 2009); Maei et al. (2009); Maei and Sutton (2010); Maei et al. (2010); Maei (2011); Liu et al. (2015); Du et al. (2017); Wang et al. (2017); Yu (2017); Xu et al. (2019); Wang and Zou (2020); Wai et al. (2020); Zhang et al. (2021a). Those works cover both prediction and control problems, both asymptotic and finite sample analysis, both discounted and average rewards, and both linear and nonlinear function approximation. All existing works are indirect in the sense that they introduce an additional weight vector to solve the double sampling issue. By contrast, this work is the first in the GTD family that does not require an additional weight vector. This work considers only the prediction problem with the discounted total rewards. The idea in this work can be easily extended to control problems, to average rewards, to nonlinear function approximation, and to eligibility traces, all of which are, however, saved for future work.

GTD is originally invented to solve the deadly triad. Besides GTD, other possible solutions include emphatic weighting (Yu, 2015; Zhang and Whiteson, 2022), density ratio learning (Nachum et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2020a), and target networks (Zhang et al., 2021b). Those different solutions have their own pros and cons. There is not a constant winner, and the best-performing one is usually problem-dependent. We refer readers to Zhang (2022) for a more detailed discussion about all those different solutions to the deadly triad issue. Here we only briefly discuss emphatic weighting in Emphatic Temporal Difference learning (ETD) to future elaborate on our technical novelty. To address the possible

divergence of (5), ETD, in its simplest form, re-weights the incremental update in (5) with a scalar  $F_{t}$  called the follow-on trace. Specifically, ETD makes the following iterative update

$$
F _ {t} \dot {=} 1 + \gamma \rho_ {t - 1} F _ {t - 1}, \tag {23}
$$

$$
w _ {t + 1} \doteq w _ {t} + \alpha_ {t} F _ {t} \rho_ {t} \left(R _ {t + 1} + \gamma x _ {t + 1} ^ {\top} w _ {t} - x _ {t} ^ {\top} w _ {t}\right) x _ {t}.
$$

This followon trace  $F_{t}$  is recursively defined, and it can be easily seen that  $F_{t}$  is a function of  $S_{0}, A_{0}, \ldots, S_{t-1}, A_{t-1}$ . To analyze ETD, one naive way is then to consider an augmented chain  $\{Y_{t}\}$  defined as

$$
Y _ {t} \doteq (S _ {0}, A _ {0}, \dots , S _ {t}, A _ {t}).
$$

This chain evolves in an increasing space

$$
\cup_ {i = 1} ^ {\infty} \left(\mathcal {S} \times \mathcal {A}\right) ^ {i}
$$

and is, in general, very hard to analyze. One common practice for analyzing TD with traces is to include the trace itself into states, see, e.g., Tsitsiklis and Roy (1996). In particular, Yu (2015) considers an augmented chain  $\{F_t, S_t, A_t\}$  evolving in  $\mathbb{R} \times \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{A}$  for analyzing ETD and confirms its asymptotic convergence. If we compare ETD (23) with Direct GTD (10), it can be seen that we essentially replace the random scalar  $F_t$  with a random matrix  $\rho_{t + f(t)}(x_{t + f(t)} - \gamma x_{t + f(t) + 1})x_{t + f(t)}^{\top}$ . So in principle, it might also be possible to analyze Direct GTD on an augmented chain in  $\mathbb{R}^{K \times K} \times \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{A}$ . This, however, turns out to be very hard since analyzing the transition function of the augmented chain for Direct GTD is very challenging. This work, instead, uses a refined discretization of ODE to account for the increasing state space, which appears to be novel. Another approach to working with the increasing state space in ETD is truncation (Zhang and Whiteson, 2022; Guan et al., 2022). Instead of computing  $F_t$  using all previous records, Guan et al. (2022) compute  $F_t$  using only a finite history of length  $f(t)$ , with  $f(t)$  being an increasing function. In other words, instead of computing  $F_t$  using  $S_0, A_0, \ldots, S_{t-1}, A_{t-1}$ , Guan et al. (2022) compute a truncated version of  $F_t$  using only  $S_{t-f(t)}, A_{t-f(t)}, \ldots, S_{t-1}, A_{t-1}$ . This is the closest work to our method. We, however, want to highlight three key differences. First, though sharing some technical similarity, this work and Guan et al. (2022) solve two different problems. This work aims to eliminate the additional weight vector in GTD, while Guan et al. (2022) aims to reduce the variance of ETD. Second, all the analysis in Guan et al. (2022) relies on the presence of the projection operator. And Guan et al. (2022) establish convergence only in expectation. By contrast, our asymptotic analysis does not assume the presence of a projection operator and establishes the stability with the supermartingale convergence theorem. And our asymptotic convergence is almost sure convergence. As remarked in Borkar (2009), establishing stability is usually the most challenging part of analyzing a stochastic approximation algorithm. Third, our Direct GTD is more data efficient. Though both this work and Guan et al. (2022) consider a sliding window of an increasing size  $f(t)$ , how the sliding window moves are different. In this work, our sliding window always moves by 1 step. In other words, Direct GTD makes updates every step. By contrast, the sliding windows in Guan et al. (2022) never overlap. In other words, Guan et al. (2022) makes updates every  $f(t)$  steps. To summarize, from the technical perspective, both

our finite sample analysis and Guan et al. (2022) use a projection operator, so they have similar difficulty. Our asymptotic analysis does not have a projection operator and is much more challenging. It is possible that Guan et al. (2022) could benefit from our refined discretization of ODEs to establish asymptotic convergence without projection, which we, however, save for future work.

# 9 Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a new method to solve the double sampling issue in implementing the idea of Gradient TD, setting up a new agenda for defeating the notorious deadly triad. The key advantage of our approach is that we do not introduce additional weights as existing GTD methods do. Key to our convergence analysis is a novel refined discretization of the limiting ODE. Though we do not abstract our analysis into a new convergence theorem for general stochastic approximation algorithms, we do believe the insights therein can be easily transferred to analyzing other stochastic approximation algorithms with a similar structure.

# A.1 Markovian Setting and Shorthands

We first recall our Markovian setting (4) and define a few shorthands. We work on an infinite trajectory

$$
S _ {0}, A _ {0}, R _ {1}, S _ {1}, A _ {1}, R _ {2}, \dots
$$

where

$$
S _ {0} \sim p _ {0}, A _ {t} \sim \mu (\cdot | S _ {t}), R _ {t + 1} = r (S _ {t}, A _ {t}), S _ {t + 1} \sim p (\cdot | S _ {t}, A _ {t}).
$$

We use  $\hat{A}_t$  to denote the stochastic estimate of the matrix  $A$  (7) at time  $t$ , i.e.,

$$
\hat {A} _ {t} \doteq \rho_ {t} x _ {t} (\gamma x _ {t + 1} - x _ {t}) ^ {\top}.
$$

Similarly, we define

$$
\hat {b} _ {t} \doteq \rho_ {t} R _ {t + 1} x _ {t}.
$$

The finiteness of the MDP and Assumptions 4.1 & 4.2 ensure the existence of a constant  $H < \infty$  such that

$$
\sup _ {t} \max \left\{\left\| \hat {A} _ {t} \right\|, \left\| \hat {b} _ {t} \right\|, \| A \|, \| b \|, \left\| \hat {A} _ {t} A ^ {- 1} b \right\| \right\} \leq H.
$$

Let  $\mathcal{F}_t$  denote the  $\sigma$ -algebra until time  $t$ , i.e.,

$$
\mathcal {F} _ {t} \doteq \sigma \left(w _ {0}, S _ {0}, A _ {0}, \dots , S _ {t - 1}, A _ {t - 1}, S _ {t}\right).
$$

The stochastic approximates then have the following property.

Lemma 3 Let Assumption 4.1 hold. Then there exists a constant  $C_M > 0$  and  $\chi \in [0,1)$  such that

$$
\left\| \mathbb {E} \left[ \hat {A} _ {t} \right] - A \right\| \leq C _ {M} \chi^ {t}, \tag {24}
$$

$$
\left\| \mathbb {E} \left[ \hat {A} _ {t + k} ^ {\top} \hat {A} _ {t} \right] - \mathbb {E} \left[ \hat {A} _ {t + k} \right] ^ {\top} \mathbb {E} \left[ \hat {A} _ {t} \right] \right\| \leq C _ {M} \chi^ {k}, \tag {25}
$$

$$
\left\| \mathbb {E} \left[ \hat {A} _ {t + k} ^ {\top} \hat {A} _ {t} \mid \mathcal {F} _ {l} \right] - A ^ {\top} A \right\| \leq C _ {M} \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 1 & t <   l \\ \chi^ {k} + \chi^ {t - l} & t \geq l \end{array} . \right. \tag {26}
$$

Similarly,

$$
\left\| \mathbb {E} \left[ \hat {b} _ {t} \right] - A \right\| \leq C _ {M} \chi^ {t},
$$

$$
\left\| \mathbb {E} \left[ \hat {A} _ {t + k} ^ {\top} \hat {b} _ {t} \right] - \mathbb {E} \left[ \hat {A} _ {t + k} \right] ^ {\top} \mathbb {E} \left[ \hat {b} _ {t} \right] \right\| \leq C _ {M} \chi^ {k},
$$

$$
\left\| \mathbb {E} \left[ \hat {A} _ {t + k} ^ {\top} \hat {b} _ {t} | \mathcal {F} _ {l} \right] - A ^ {\top} b \right\| \leq C _ {M} \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 1 & t <   l \\ \chi^ {k} + \chi^ {t - l} & t \geq l \end{array} \right..
$$

The proof is provided in Section A.5.1.

For discretizing the ODE, we define a sequence  $\{T_m\}_{m=0,1,\ldots}$  as

$$

where  $C_{\alpha}$  is defined in Assumption 4.3 and  $\eta$  is some constant such that

$$

# A.2 Proof of Theorem 1

which completes the proof.

Lemma 15 If the gap function  $f(t) = \lfloor h(t)\ln (t)\rfloor$  where  $h$  is a non-negative, increasing function tending to infinity, then  $\sum_{t = 0}^{\infty}\chi^{f(t)}$  for all  $\chi \in (0,1)$ .

Thus, by comparison test and p-test, we can conclude that  $\sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \chi^{f(t)} \leq \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} t^{-2} < \infty$ .

\mathbb {E} \left[ \Lambda_ {t} \left(w _ {t - k}\right) \right] \leq 2 B (B + 1) C _ {M} \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 1 & k <   f (t - k) \\ \chi^ {f (t)} + \chi^ {t - (t - k + f (t - k))} & k \geq f (t - k) \end{array} . \right. \tag {41}
$$

Plugging (41) into the expectation of (40) yields

$$
\mathbb {E} [ \Lambda_ {t} (w _ {t}) ] \leq C _ {g} C _ {\alpha} C _ {L i p} \ln \left(\frac {t}{t - k}\right) + 2 B (B + 1) C _ {M} \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 1 & k <   f (t - k) \\ \chi^ {f (t)} + \chi^ {t - (t - k + f (t - k))} & k \geq f (t - k) \end{array} , \right.
$$

which completes the proof.
