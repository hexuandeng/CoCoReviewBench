Multi-armed bandit (MAB) problems are mainly studied under two extreme settings known as stochastic and adversarial. These two settings, however, do not capture realistic environments such as search engines and marketing and advertising, in which rewards stochastically change in time. Motivated by that, we introduce and study a dynamic MAB problem with stochastic temporal structure, where the expected reward of each arm is governed by an auto-regressive (AR) model. Due to the dynamic nature of the rewards, simple "explore and commit" policies fail, as all arms have to be explored continuously over time. We formalize this by characterizing a per-round regret lower bound, where the regret is measured against a strong (dynamic) benchmark. We then present an algorithm whose per-round regret almost matches our regret lower bound. Our algorithm relies on two mechanisms: (i) alternating between recently pulled arms and unpulled arms with potential, and (ii) restarting. These mechanisms enable the algorithm to dynamically adapt to changes and discard irrelevant past information at a suitable rate. In numerical studies, we further demonstrate the strength of our algorithm under non-stationary settings.

Key words: dynamic bandits, temporal structures, low-regret policy, online learning algorithms

# 1. Introduction

The multi-armed bandit (MAB) (Thompson 1933, Robbins 1952) is a framework for studying online decision-making under uncertainty. In the basic stochastic formulation of the MAB problem, there are  $k$  arms, each having a fixed, unknown and independent reward distribution, and the goal is to identify the arm with the highest expected reward while experimenting with the arms over the course of  $T$  rounds; see, e.g., Thompson (1933), Auer et al. (2002b). Another line of work that is widely studied in the MAB literature focuses on the adversarial setting, which, unlike the basic stochastic formulation, allows the reward distributions to be determined by an adversary and change arbitrarily in time (Auer et al. 2002a, Auer 2003, Kleinberg 2004, Dani and Hayes 2006). Due to the enormous unpredictability of the adversary,

their goal is to approach the performance of the best static benchmark, which is the single best arm that one can pull in hindsight.

The assumptions in either stochastic or adversarial setting, however, are often deemed too stringent and no longer valid in most real-world applications, such as online advertising (Pandey et al. 2007), online auctions (Kleinberg and Leighton 2003), and dynamic pricing (Keskin and Zeevi 2017), where the reward distributions in fact evolve with time following some intrinsic temporal structure. Consider, for example, a web search engine whose goal is to maximize its revenue by showing users ads with the highest click through rate (CTR). Here, the CTR of ads keep evolving over time, where the amount of changes can be linear in  $T$ , and can be modeled using certain temporal structure (e.g., a time series process) (Agarwal et al. 2009). Another example is marketing and advertising, where stores would like to display products with the highest demand. To do so, they need to experiment with various products to learn their demand, where product demand may vary over time following some time series models (Fattah et al. 2018).

Motivated by these, in this work, we relax the previously restrictive assumptions on the reward distributions by studying a dynamic bandit problem, where the reward of each arm has a temporal structure and the amount of changes is linear in  $T$ . To impose temporal structure, we consider the simple yet popular auto-regressive (AR) model. The AR model is one of the most commonly studied time series models and is widely adopted in real-world applications that involve non-stationary environments, including prediction of sales (Shumway and Stoffer 2017), advertising (Aaker et al. 1982) and marketing (Dekimpe and Hanssens 1995).

Our work, which focuses on MAB with an AR temporal structure, contributes to the non-stationary MAB literature that draws attention in recent years. Most related works focused on two types of non-stationary bandits, namely the rested bandits (Gittins and Jones 1974) and restless bandits (Whittle 1988). In rested bandits, only the expected reward of the arm that is being pulled will change, while in restless bandits, the expected reward of each arm changes at each round according to a known yet arbitrary stochastic process, regardless of whether the arm is pulled. Recently, there has also been a third stream of literature that studies non-stationary bandits where the changes in reward distributions depend on both which arm is pulled and the time period that passes by since its last pull (see, e.g., Kleinberg and Immorlica (2018), Cella and Cesa-Bianchi (2020)). The dynamic MAB that we study belongs to the restless setting, since the reward distributions change at each round based on

AR processes, independent of our actions. Restless bandits, however, is known to be difficult and intractable (Papadimitriou and Tsitsiklis 1999), and many work thus focus on studying its approximation (Guha and Munagala 2007) or relaxation (Bertsimas and Niño-Mora 2000).

One line of closely related work on restless bandits studies non-stationary MAB problems with limited amount of changes. These works either assume piecewise stationary rewards (see Garivier and Moulines (2011), Liu et al. (2018), Cao et al. (2019)), or impose a variation budget on the total amount of changes (see Besbes et al. (2014)). They differ from our setting in that they rely on the amount of changes in the environment to be sublinear in  $T$ , while in AR processes, changes are more rapid and linear in  $T$ . Another line of relevant research on restless bandits assumes different types of temporal structures for the reward distributions. See, e.g., Liu and Zhao (2010), Ortner et al. (2012), Tekin and Liu (2012), Mate et al. (2020) for finite-state Markovian processes, Grünewälder and Khaleghi (2019) for stationary  $\phi$ -mixing processes and Slivkins and Upfal (2008) for Brownian processes, which is a special case of an AR model but does not experience the exponential decay in the correlation between the past and the future.

Similar to the aforementioned works, our work is motivated with the goal of exploiting the temporal structure of the reward distributions to devise well-performing learning algorithms. However, the AR process is much more unpredictable than the temporal structures studied previously, due to its infinite state space and the fast decay in the values of the past observations. We are thus prompted to take a different approach that is designed specifically for adapting to the rapid changes attributed to the AR process.

# 1.1. Main Contributions

In our work, we introduce a novel setting of dynamic bandits where the reward distributions evolve according to an AR-based temporal structure. To measure the performance of algorithms in our setting, we use our notion of per-round steady state dynamic regret, where the dynamic regret at a given round is the difference between the collected reward and the highest expected reward at that round. Such a notion is more demanding than the static regret used in stochastic and adversarial MAB problems, since the algorithm competes against the best arm at each round and needs to adapt well to the temporal changes. In face of such challenges, we contribute in the following aspects.

Regret lower bound. We characterize the per-round steady state regret lower bound as a function of the stochastic rate of the change and the temporal correlation of the rewards

in Theorem 1. Our result shows that in the AR setting, the per-round dynamic regret does not go to zero as  $T$  increases, implying that any good algorithm should keep exploring over time. Furthermore, it shows that the regret lower bound increases as the stochastic rate of the change and the temporal correlation of the rewards increase. Our regret lower bound formally characterizes the challenges embedded in rapidly changing AR temporal processes.

An alternating-and-restarting algorithm. We present and analyze an algorithm for the dynamic AR bandits. Our algorithm, which we call AR2, stands for "Alternating and Restarting" algorithm for dynamic "AR" bandits. AR2 crucially relies on two mechanisms: (i) it alternates between exploration and exploitation. During exploitation, AR2 goes with a superior arm that is expected to have high reward based on past information; during exploration, it pulls one of the triggered arms, which has the potential to outperform the superior arm (see definitions in Section 4). (ii) AR2 also adopts a restarting mechanism, which allows AR2 to get rid of potentially misleading information. Via these two mechanisms, AR2 balances two levels of tradeoffs—exploration and exploitation; "remembering" and "forgetting"—intrinsic to the fast-changing environment introduced by the AR process; see Section 4.

We provide an upper bound on the regret of AR2 (Theorem 3) and show that it is close to the regret lower bound. From a technical point of view, the analysis of the upper bound is rather intricate, as it involves distributing the regret incurred at each round over subsequent rounds, and identifying a high-probability event on which the rewards do not experience drastic changes (see Section 6).

Numerical studies. We numerically verify the efficacy of AR2 by comparing it against benchmark algorithms designed for both stationary and non-stationary settings (see Section 7). We show that our algorithm outperforms algorithms designed for both stationary settings (e.g., "explore-then-commit" (Garivier et al. 2016) and UCB (Auer et al. 2002b)), and nonstationary settings (e.g., RExp3 (Besbes et al. 2014) and a modified UCB algorithm that also considers the AR temporal structure). In particular, the superior performance of AR2 against non-stationary benchmarks shows its unique strength in adapting to rapid changes.

The proofs of all analytical results of this paper can be found in the appendix (Appendix D to G are included in the online supplement).

# 2. Preliminaries

Expected rewards. Consider a dynamic MAB problem with  $k$  arms over  $T$  rounds. The state (expected reward) of arm  $i \in [k]$  at any round  $t \in [T]$  is denoted by  $r_i(t)$ , where

$r_i(t) \in [-\mathfrak{R}, +\mathfrak{R}]$ . Conditioned on the state  $r_i(t)$ , the realized reward of arm  $i$  at round  $t$ , denoted by  $R_i(t)$ , is given by

$$
R _ {i} (t) = r _ {i} (t) + \epsilon_ {i} (t),
$$

where  $\epsilon_{i}(t)\sim N(0,\sigma)$  is independent across arms and rounds and we call  $\sigma \in (0,1)$  the stochastic rate of change. (More generally, if each arm  $i\in [k]$  has a different, unknown stochastic rate of change  $\sigma_{i}$ , as long as we have an upper bound  $\sigma \geq \max_{i}\sigma_{i}$ , all of our theoretical results naturally carry over with the same dependency on  $\sigma$ .)

Evolution of expected rewards. The expected reward of each arm  $i \in [k]$ ,  $r_i(t)$ , evolves over time. In our model, we assume that  $r_i(t)$  undergoes an independent AR-1 process:

$$
r _ {i} (t + 1) = \alpha (r _ {i} (t) + \epsilon_ {i} (t)) = \alpha R _ {i} (t),
$$

or equivalently,  $R_{i}(t + 1) = \alpha R_{i}(t) + \epsilon_{i}(t + 1)$ , where  $\alpha \in (0,1)$  is the parameter of the AR-1 model and can be used as a proxy to measure temporal correlation over time. As  $\alpha$  increases, the temporal correlation increases. $^{1}$  The expected reward  $r_{i}(t + 1)$  could be very large or small as the noise  $\sigma_{i}(t)$ 's are unbounded. As commonly seen in the bandit literature (e.g., Lai et al. (1985)), we make the rewards bounded by truncating  $r_{i}(t + 1)$  at  $\mathfrak{R}$  when  $r_{i}(t + 1)$  exceeds  $\mathfrak{R}$  for some finite  $\mathfrak{R} > 0$ . Similarly, when  $r_{i}(t + 1)$  is less than  $-\mathfrak{R}$ , we set it to  $-\mathfrak{R}$ . That is,  $r_{i}(t + 1) = \mathcal{B}\big(\alpha (r_{i}(t) + \epsilon_{i}(t))\big)$ , where  $\mathcal{B}(y) = \min\{\max\{y, -\mathfrak{R}\}, \mathfrak{R}\}$ .

Note that here we assume that the AR parameter  $\alpha$  is known to the decision maker. As we will discuss in Section 8, this assumption—which is common in the literature (See, e.g., Besbes et al. (2014), Grünewälder and Khaleghi (2019) and Slivkins and Upfal (2008)) and is made to ease the analysis—can be relaxed. We also assume here that all arms share the same AR parameter  $\alpha$ . As our numerical studies in Section 7 suggest, this assumption can be relaxed too. There, we generalize our model by considering heterogeneous AR parameters  $\alpha_{i}$  for each arm  $i \in [k]$ , and our algorithm maintains its performance. Nonetheless, it is worth noting that the assumption of homogeneous  $\alpha$  can be justified in some applications. Consider an online seller that displays a fashion product in a variety of colors (arms). The evolution of demands (rewards) for each variation can be modeled as an AR-1 process, where the parameter  $\alpha$  captures the amount of consumer inertia, i.e., their tendency to keep selecting

the color they liked previously. Since the arms are variations of the same product,  $\alpha$  is mostly determined by exogenous factors that impact all arms in the same way.

Feedback structure and goal. Our goal is to design an algorithm that obtains high reward, against a strong dynamic benchmark that in every round  $t$ , pulls an arm that has the highest expected reward. This benchmark is much more demanding than the time-invariant benchmark used in traditional MAB literature. Obtaining good performance against the dynamic benchmark is particularly challenging as in any round  $t \in [T]$ , only the reward of the pulled arms can be observed. Not being able to observe the reward of unpulled arms in dynamic and time-varying environments introduces missing values in the AR process associated with arms. This, in turn, deteriorates the prediction quality of the future expected rewards and the performance of any algorithm that relies on such predictions (see Anava et al. (2015) for a work that studies the impact of missing values on the prediction quality in AR processes).

We now formally define how we measure the performance of any non-anticipating algorithm.

DEFINITION 1 (DYNAMIC STEADY STATE REGRET). Consider a non-anticipating algorithm  $\mathcal{A}$  that pulls arm  $I_{t}$  at round  $t\in [T]$ . The dynamic regret of  $\mathcal{A}$  at round  $t$  is defined as

$$
\mathrm {R E G} _ {\mathcal {A}} (t) = r ^ {\star} (t) - r _ {I _ {t}} (t),
$$

where  $r^{\star}(t) = \max_{i\in [k]}\{r_i(t)\}$  is the maximum expected reward at round  $t$ . Furthermore, the per-round steady state regret of algorithm  $\mathcal{A}$  is defined as

$$
\overline {{\mathrm {R E G}}} _ {\mathcal {A}} = \lim \sup _ {T \to \infty} \mathbb {E} \left[ \frac {1}{T} \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} \mathrm {R E G} _ {\mathcal {A}} (t) \right].
$$

We would like to design an algorithm with a small per-round steady state regret, where we present the per-round steady state regret as a function of the stochastic rate of the change  $(\sigma)$  and the temporal correlation of the rewards  $(\alpha)$ .

# 3. Regret Lower Bound

We now provide a lower bound on the best achievable dynamic per-round steady state regret.

THEOREM 1 (Regret Lower Bound). Consider a dynamic MAB problem with  $k$  arms, where the expected reward of each arm evolves as an independent AR-1 process with parameter  $\alpha$ , stochastic rate of change  $\sigma$  and truncating boundaries  $[-\mathcal{R}, \mathcal{R}]$ . Then the per-round steady

state regret of any algorithm  $\mathcal{A}$  is at least  $\Omega(g(k, \alpha, \sigma)\alpha\sigma)$ , where  $g(k, \alpha, \sigma)$ , defined in Equation (20) in Section C, denotes the probability that two best arms are within  $\alpha\sigma$  distance of each other at any given round, under the stationary distribution.

At a high level, the proof of Theorem 1 builds on the following idea: at any given round  $t$ , even if an algorithm  $\mathcal{A}$  has access to all of the past information, due to the stochasticity introduced by the noises  $\epsilon_i(t)$ , the algorithm  $\mathcal{A}$  will select a sub-optimal arm at the  $(t + 1)$ th round with constant probability. We first show that the probability that the expected rewards of the two best arms at round  $t$  are within  $\alpha \sigma$  of each other can be expressed as  $g(k, \alpha, \sigma)$ , formally defined in Equation (20). We next show that if the two best arms are  $\alpha \sigma$  close, then at round  $t + 1$ ,  $\mathcal{A}$  would incur  $\Omega(\alpha \sigma)$  regret with constant probability. See Section C for the proof.

In the extreme case when  $\alpha$  is close to one, the stationary distribution of  $r_i(t)$  can be approximated with a uniform distribution within the boundaries and two probability masses at the boundaries (see Section B). Using the uniform approximation, one can show that  $g(k,\alpha ,\sigma) = \Omega (k\alpha \sigma)$  (see discussion in Section C). This then yields a regret lower bound of order  $\Omega (k\alpha^2\sigma^2)$ .

An important implication of Theorem 1 is that in dynamic bandits, when the regret is measured against a strong dynamic benchmark, the best achievable per-round regret does not converge to zero. This stands in contrast to stationary or adversarial bandits with the time-invariant benchmark, for which there are algorithms (e.g., UCB (Auer et al. 2002b) for stationary bandits; Exp3 (Auer et al. 2002a) for adversarial bandits) that asymptotically achieves zero per-round regret as  $T$  goes to infinity. In the dynamic setting, each arm undergoes changes that scale linearly with  $T$ . The rapid changes of each arm's expected reward along with the presence of missing values makes it difficult for any algorithm to adapt to the changing environment in time. Note that our result agrees with the lower bound provided in Besbes et al. (2014), which states that when the total variation of expected rewards scale linearly with  $T$ , the regret also grows linearly.

# 4. Algorithm AR2

In this section, we present and analyze an algorithm, AR2 (which stands for "Alternating and Restarting" algorithm for dynamic "AR" bandits). Our algorithm AR2, presented in Algorithm 1, works in epochs of a predetermined length. Within each epoch, the algorithm

\widehat {r} _ {\mathrm {s u p}} (t) = \widehat {r} _ {i _ {\mathrm {s u p}} (t)} (t)
$$

-If  $t$  is odd and  $\mathcal{T}\neq \emptyset$ , pull a triggered arm with the earliest triggering time:  $I_{t} = \arg \min_{j\in \mathcal{T}}\tau_{j}^{\mathrm{trig}}$ .

- Otherwise, pull the superior arm  $I_{t} = i_{\mathrm{sup}}(t)$ .

- Receive a reward  $R_{I_t}(t)$ , and set  $\tau_{I_t} = t$ .  
- Maintain Estimates: Set  $\widehat{r}_{I_t}(t + 1) = \mathcal{B}(\alpha R_{I_t}(t))$  and set  $\widehat{r}_i(t + 1) = \alpha \widehat{r}_i(t)$  for  $i\neq I_t$

keeps and updates an estimate of the expected reward of each arm  $i \in [k]$ . Using these estimates, the algorithm alternates between exploitation and exploration steps. In the exploitation step, AR2 plays a superior arm, which is identified using the estimate of the expected rewards and is expected to yield high reward. In the exploration step, AR2 plays a triggered arm that has not been pulled recently, but has the potential of generating higher expected reward than the superior arm. At the end of the current epoch, we restart the algorithm at the beginning of the next epoch. AR2 dynamically balances two tradeoffs intrinsic to the dynamic MAB problem. (i) It deals with the tradeoff of exploration and exploitation by alternating between exploiting superior arm and exploring triggered arm within an epoch. (ii) Via restarting, it also deals with the tradeoff between "remembering" and "forgetting", which is commonly considered tradeoff in non-stationary environments (see Besbes et al. (2014), Bogunovic et al. (2016)).

Maintaining estimates of expected reward of arms. For any round  $t$  and arm  $i \in [k]$ , let  $\tau_{i}(t)$  be the last round before  $t$  (including  $t$ ) at which arm  $i$  is pulled, and let  $\tau_{i}^{\mathrm{next}}(t)$  be the next round after  $t$  (excluding  $t$ ) at which arm  $i$  is pulled. Let  $\Delta \tau_{i}(t) = \tau_{i}^{\mathrm{next}}(t) - \tau_{i}(t)$  be the gap between two consecutive pulls of arm  $i$  around  $t$ . Define  $\widehat{r}_{i}(t)$  as the estimate of the

reward of arm  $i$  at  $t$  based on the most recent observed reward of arm  $i$  (i.e.,  $R_{i}(\tau_{i}(t))$ ). Via recursive updates (see Step (b)), AR2 maintains the following estimate of expected reward for each arm:

$$
\widehat {r} _ {i} (t) = \alpha^ {t - \tau_ {i} (t) - 1} \widehat {r} _ {i} (\tau_ {i} (t) + 1) = \alpha^ {t - \tau_ {i} (t) - 1} \mathcal {B} \big (\alpha R _ {i} (\tau_ {i} (t)) \big).
$$

Superior arms. The superior arm at round  $t$ , denoted by  $i_{\mathrm{sup}}(t)$ , is one of the two most recently pulled arms, i.e.,  $I_{t-1}$  or  $I_{t-2}$ , that has the higher estimated reward; that is,

$$
i _ {\sup } (t) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l} I _ {t - 1} \text {i f} \widehat {r} _ {I _ {t - 1}} (t) \geq \widehat {r} _ {I _ {t - 2}} (t) \\ I _ {t - 2} \text {i f} \widehat {r} _ {I _ {t - 1}} (t) <   \widehat {r} _ {I _ {t - 2}} (t) \end{array} , \right. \tag {1}
$$

We further define  $\widehat{r}_{\mathrm{sup}}(t) = \widehat{r}_{i_{\mathrm{sup}}(t)}(t)$  as the estimated reward of the superior arm at round  $t$ . We remark that here, for simplicity of analysis, our definition of the superior arm only considers the two most recently pulled arms. We can in fact set the superior arm to be the one with the highest estimated reward among the  $m$  most recently pulled arms for any constant  $m \geq 2$ . A similar theoretical analysis will then yield the same theoretical guarantee, as shown in Theorem 3. We further verify the robustness of AR2 to the choice of  $m$  in our numerical studies, where we consider all arms as potential candidates for the superior arm, and observe that AR2 maintains its competitive performance (see Section 7).

Triggered arms. As stated earlier, in order to adapt to changes, AR2 identifies and keeps track of a set of arms with potential that are not pulled recently. We refer to these arms as triggered arms and denote their associated set by  $\mathcal{T}$ . In a round  $t$ , an arm  $i \neq i_{\sup}(t)$  gets triggered if the following triggering criteria is satisfied:

$$
\widehat {r} _ {\mathrm {s u p}} (t) - \widehat {r} _ {i} (t) \leq c _ {1} \sigma \sqrt {(\alpha^ {2} - \alpha^ {2 (t - \tau_ {i} (t) + 1)}) / (1 - \alpha^ {2})}.
$$

Here,  $c_{1}$  depends on  $\alpha$ ,  $\sigma$ ; we determine the value of  $c_{1}$  later. We call the earliest such time  $t > \tau_{i}(t)$  as the triggering time of arm  $i$  and denote it as  $\tau_{i}^{\mathrm{trig}}(t)$ . If arm  $i$  is triggered, it is added to the triggered set  $\mathcal{T}$ . When arm  $i$  gets pulled or is chosen as the superior arm, it is removed from  $\mathcal{T}$ . The right-hand side of the triggering criteria is a confidence bound constructed based on Hoeffding's Inequality; the details will be discussed in Section 6 and Section E.

# 5. Regret of Algorithm AR2

Our algorithm AR2 works for AR-1 model with any choice of parameter  $\alpha \in (0,1)$ . However, it turns out that the problem is less challenging when the future is weakly correlated with the past (i.e., when  $\alpha$  is small). We show in Theorem 2 that even the naive algorithm achieves near-optimal performance when  $\alpha$  is too small. This is because with small  $\alpha$ , (i) for any arm  $i \in [k]$ , the stationary distribution of its expected reward  $r_i(t)$  concentrates around zero with high probability, and (ii) our observations of past rewards quickly deteriorate in their value of providing insights on the evolution of expected rewards in the future.

THEOREM 2. In the dynamic MAB problem, a naive algorithm that randomly selects one arm and keeps selecting it throughout the horizon incurs per-round steady state regret at most  $O\Big(\sqrt{\log(1 / \alpha\sigma) + \log k}\frac{\alpha\sigma}{\sqrt{1 - \alpha^2}}\Big)$ .

In Figure 1, we compare the order of the regret upper bound in Theorem 2 under the naive algorithm with the order of the regret lower bound in Theorem 1. Observe that  $\bar{\alpha} \triangleq 0.5$  serves as a rough threshold value such that when  $\alpha \in (0, \bar{\alpha})$ , the orders of lower and upper bounds almost match each other, which suggests limited room for improvement. (Nonetheless, in Section 7, we show via numerical studies that our algorithm still outperforms the benchmark algorithms even when  $\alpha$  is small.) On the other hand, as  $\alpha \to 1$ , the gap between lower and upper bounds starts to expand, implying that the problem becomes more difficult and the naive algorithm no longer produces satisfying performance. In the rest of the discussion, we thereby focus on the more challenging regime when  $\alpha \in [\bar{\alpha}, 1)$ .

We note that as  $\sigma$  increases, the upper bound loosens up as the per-round regret of AR2 experiences smaller growth and approaches the worst-case lower bound. This especially highlights the strength of AR2 in adapting to a rapidly changing environment.

(For example, when  $\alpha = 0.95$ ,  $\mathcal{K}(\alpha) = 20$ .) In particular, the bound  $\mathcal{K}(\alpha)$  results from our loose upper bound for the number of triggered arms in the proof of Lemma 7, where we used the fact that there are at most  $k - 1 < \mathcal{K}(\alpha)$  triggered arms at any round to limit how quickly the values of the past observations diminish. If, at any given round, the number of triggered arm is  $O(1)$ , the upper bound  $\mathcal{K}(\alpha)$  would no longer be required, and the regret upper bound can be further reduced to  $O(c_0^2\alpha^2\sigma^2k\log (c_0\alpha\sigma))$ , which essentially matches the lower bound (up to logarithmic factors).

# 6. Proof of Theorem 3

The proof of Theorem 3 proceeds in four steps. In Step 1, we define a new notion called distributed regret by distributing the instantaneous regret, incurred at the round we pull the arm, over the few subsequent rounds. We then rewrite the per-round steady state regret in terms of distributed regrets. In Step 2, we define a high-probability good event that is critical for our analysis of the distributed regret. In Step 3, we provide an upper bound for the distributed regret at each round, with the help of the important implications of the good event. In Step 4, we sum the distributed regrets over rounds and epochs, which establishes the per-round steady state regret of AR2 against the dynamic benchmark.

Step 1: Distributed regrets. Let  $\Delta r_{i}(t) = r^{\star}(t) - r_{i}(t)$  be the instantaneous regret incurred from pulling arm  $i$  at round  $t$ , where  $r^{\star}(t) = \max_{i\in [k]}r_{i}(t)$  is the maximum expected reward at round  $t$ . Consider the expected per-round regret

$$
\overline {{\mathrm {R E G}}} _ {\mathcal {A}} = \mathbb {E} \left[ \frac {1}{T} \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} \mathrm {R E G} _ {\mathcal {A}} (t) \right] = \frac {1}{T} \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} \mathbb {E} \left[ \Delta r _ {I _ {t}} (t) \right]. \tag {2}
$$

Here,  $\Delta r_{I_t}(t) = r^\star (t) - r_{I_t}(t)$  is the instantaneous regret incurred at round  $t$ . In the following, we show that we can distribute this instantaneous regret over subsequent rounds.

Note that for any  $i \in [k]$ , let  $\mathfrak{C}_i^{(j)}$  denote the round at which arm  $i$  gets pulled for the  $j$ th time within an epoch. Then during the period between two consecutive pulls  $\mathfrak{C}_i^{(j)} \leq t < \mathfrak{C}_i^{(j+1)}$ , the only regret incurred from pulling arm  $i$  is the instantaneous regret  $\Delta r_i(\mathfrak{C}_i^{(j)})$ . By previous definition, we also have  $\tau_i(t) = \mathfrak{C}_i^{(j)}$ ,  $\tau_i^{\mathrm{next}}(t) = \mathfrak{C}_i^{(j+1)}$  and  $\Delta \tau_i(t) = \mathfrak{C}_i^{(j+1)} - \mathfrak{C}_i^{(j)}$  for all  $\mathfrak{C}_i^{(j)} \leq t < \mathfrak{C}_i^{(j+1)}$ . Now observe that we can decompose  $\Delta r_i(\mathfrak{C}_i^{(j)})$  as follows

$$
\Delta r _ {i} (\mathcal {C} _ {i} ^ {(j)}) = \sum_ {t = \mathcal {C} _ {i} ^ {(j)}} ^ {\mathcal {C} _ {i} ^ {(j + 1)} - 1} \Big (\frac {\Delta r _ {i} (\mathcal {C} _ {i} ^ {(j)}) \alpha^ {2 (t - \mathcal {C} _ {i} ^ {(j)})}}{1 + \alpha^ {2} + \cdots + \alpha^ {2 (\mathcal {C} _ {i} ^ {(j + 1)} - 1 - \mathcal {C} _ {i} ^ {(j)})}} \Big) = \sum_ {t = \mathcal {C} _ {i} ^ {(j)}} ^ {\mathcal {C} _ {i} ^ {(j + 1)} - 1} \Big (\frac {\Delta r _ {i} (\tau_ {i} (t)) \alpha^ {2 (t - \tau_ {i} (t))}}{1 + \alpha^ {2} + \cdots + \alpha^ {2 (\Delta \tau_ {i} (t) - 1)}} \Big).
$$

This leads to the following notion of distributed regret:

DEFINITION 2 (DISTRIBUTED REGRET). The distributed regret of arm  $i$  at round  $t$  is defined as

$$
D _ {i} (t) = \left(\frac {\Delta r _ {i} \left(\tau_ {i} (t)\right)}{1 + \alpha^ {2} + \cdots + \alpha^ {2 \left(\Delta \tau_ {i} (t) - 1\right)}}\right) \alpha^ {2 \left(t - \tau_ {i} (t)\right)} \tag {3}
$$

Note that  $D_{i}(t) = 0$  if arm  $i$  is the best arm at round  $\tau_{i}(t)$  (i.e.,  $\Delta r_{i}(\tau_{i}(t)) = 0$ ), and there is no regret to be distributed. Now, let  $T_{i}$  be the total number of rounds we pull arm  $i$  in the horizon, we can rewrite (2) as

$$
\overline {{\mathrm {R E G}}} _ {\mathcal {A}} = \frac {1}{T} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {k} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {T _ {i}} \mathbb {E} \Big [ \Delta r _ {i} (\mathfrak {T} _ {i} ^ {(j)}) \Big ] = \frac {1}{T} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {k} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {T} \mathbb {E} \Big [ D _ {i} (t) \Big ].
$$

In addition, recall that AR2 proceeds in epochs of length  $\Delta_{\mathrm{ep}}$ , and  $S = T / \Delta_{\mathrm{ep}}$  is the total number of epochs. We can further rewrite the expected per-round regret to be

$$
\overline {{\mathrm {R E G}}} _ {\mathcal {A}} = \frac {1}{T} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {k} \sum_ {s = 1} ^ {S} \sum_ {t = (s - 1) \Delta_ {\mathrm {e p}} + 1} ^ {s \Delta_ {\mathrm {e p}}} \mathbb {E} [ D _ {i} (t) ]. \qquad \qquad (4)
$$

Step 2: Good event and its implications. Before proceeding, we first define a good event  $\mathcal{G}(t)$  at round  $t$ , which would help simplify our analysis. In principle, we say that a good event  $\mathcal{G}(t)$  happens at round  $t$  if the noises within the epoch including  $t$  are not too large in magnitude. Recall from Section 2 that  $r_i(t)$  follows an AR-1 process with truncating boundaries:  $r_i(t + 1) = \mathcal{B}(\alpha (r_i(t) + \epsilon_i(t)))$ , where  $\mathcal{B}(y) = \min \{\max \{y, -\mathfrak{R}\}, \mathfrak{R}\}$ . Hence, we need to first define a new noise term that shows the influence of the truncating boundary.

$$
\tilde {\epsilon} _ {i} (t) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \epsilon_ {i} (t) & \text {i f} \alpha \left(r _ {i} (t) + \epsilon_ {i} (t)\right) \in [ - \mathfrak {R}, \mathfrak {R} ] \\ \frac {1}{\alpha} \left[ \mathcal {B} \left(\alpha \left(r _ {i} (t) + \epsilon_ {i} (t)\right)\right) - \alpha r _ {i} (t) \right] & \text {o t h e r w i s e} \end{array} \right. \tag {5}
$$

In particular, the new noise  $\tilde{\epsilon}_i(t)$  satisfies the following recursive relationship:  $r_i(t + 1) = \alpha r_i(t) + \alpha \tilde{\epsilon}_i(t)$ . We now formally define the good event  $\mathcal{G}(t)$ , which states that for any sub-interval  $[t_0,t_1]$  within the epoch including  $t$ , the weighted sum of  $\tilde{\epsilon}_i(t)$  satisfies a concentration inequality.

DEFINITION 3 (GOOD EVENT AT ROUND  $t$ ). We say that the good event  $\mathcal{G}(t)$  occurs at round  $t$  if for every  $i \in [k]$ , and for every sub-interval  $[t_0, t_1]$ , where  $t - \Delta_{\mathrm{ep}} \leq t_0 < t_1 \leq t + \Delta_{\mathrm{ep}}$ , the weighted sum of the noises  $\tilde{\epsilon}_i(t)$  satisfies

$$
\left| \sum_ {t = t _ {0}} ^ {t _ {1} - 1} \alpha^ {t _ {1} - t} \tilde {\epsilon} _ {i} (t) \right| <   c _ {0} \sigma \sqrt {\left( \right.\alpha^ {2} - \alpha^ {2 \left(t _ {1} - t _ {0} + 1\right)} / \left(1 - \alpha^ {2}\right)} , \tag {6}
$$

where  $c_0 = \sqrt{4\log(1 / \alpha\sigma) + 4\log\Delta_{\mathrm{ep}} + 2\log(4k)}$ .

By building a connection between  $\tilde{\epsilon}_i(t)$  and  $\epsilon_{i}(t)$  and Hoeffding's inequality, we show Lemma 1, which confirms that the good event  $\mathcal{G}(t)$  happens with high probability.

LEMMA 1. For any  $t \in [T]$ , we have  $\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{G}^c(t)] \leq (\alpha \sigma)^2$ .

In Lemmas 6 to 8 in Section E.1, we show that the good event  $\mathcal{G}(t)$  comes with three important implications. If  $\mathcal{G}(t)$  occurs, (i) our estimated reward  $\widehat{r}_i(t)$  does not deviate too much from the true expected reward  $r_i(t)$ ; (ii) AR2 does not wait too long to play an arm with high expected reward; (iii) the best expected reward  $r^{\star}(t)$  is not too far away from our estimated reward for the superior arm  $\widehat{r}_{\mathrm{sup}}(t)$ . These then help simplify our distributed regret analysis.

Step 3: Distributed regret analysis. For a given round  $t$  in the  $s$ -th epoch, we can bound its expected distributed regret  $\mathbb{E}[D_i(t)]$  by first decomposing it into two terms:

$$
\mathbb {E} [ D _ {i} (t) ] = \mathbb {E} [ D _ {i} (t) \mathbb {1} _ {\mathcal {G} ^ {c} (t)} ] + \mathbb {E} [ D _ {i} (t) \mathbb {1} _ {\mathcal {G} (t)} ],
$$

where  $\mathbb{1}_{\mathcal{E}}$  is the indicator function of event  $\mathcal{E}$ . We can bound the first term by applying Lemma 1

$$
\mathbb {E} \left[ D _ {i} (t) \mathbb {1} _ {\mathcal {G} ^ {c} (t)} \right] \leq 2 \Re \cdot \mathbb {P} \left[ \mathcal {G} ^ {c} (t) \right] \leq 2 \Re \cdot (\alpha \sigma) ^ {2} = O \left(\sigma^ {2} \alpha^ {2}\right). \tag {7}
$$

To bound the second term, we rely on the implications of the good event  $\mathcal{G}(t)$  to show the following.

LEMMA 2. Suppose that we apply AR2 to the dynamic MAB problem with  $\alpha \in [\bar{\alpha},1)$  and  $k \leq \mathcal{K}(\alpha)$ , for every arm  $i \in [k]$  and some round  $\tau_i(t) \leq t < \tau_i^{\mathrm{next}}(t)$ , where  $\tau_i(t)$  and  $\tau_i^{\mathrm{next}}(t)$  are two consecutive rounds at which  $i$  gets pulled, we have

$$
\mathbb {E} \left[ D _ {i} (t) \mathbb {1} _ {\mathcal {G} (t)} \right] \leq O \left(c _ {0} ^ {2} \alpha^ {2} \sigma^ {2} k ^ {2} \log \left(c _ {0} \sigma \alpha \sqrt {k}\right)\right). \tag {8}
$$

The proof of Lemma 2 is deferred to Section E.2. There, we provide a bound for the nominator and denominator of  $D_{i}(t)$  respectively, conditioning on the good event  $\mathcal{G}(t)$  and the value of  $\Delta r_{i}(t)$ . In the proof, we critically use the three implications stated in Step 2. Now, combining (7) and (8), for any round  $t$  within the  $s$ th epoch such that  $\tau_{i}(t) \leq t < \tau_{i}^{\mathrm{next}}(t)$ , we have

$$
\mathbb {E} [ D _ {i} (t) ] = \mathbb {E} [ D _ {i} (t) \mathbb {1} _ {\mathcal {G} ^ {c} (t)} ] + \mathbb {E} [ D _ {i} (t) \mathbb {1} _ {\mathcal {G} (t)} ] \leq O \left(c _ {0} ^ {2} \sigma^ {2} \alpha^ {2} k ^ {2} \log \left(c _ {0} \sigma \alpha \sqrt {k}\right)\right). \tag {9}
$$

Step 4: Summing the distributed regrets. Given Equations (4), (9) and  $\Delta_{\mathrm{ep}} = \lceil k\alpha^{-3}\sigma^{-3}\rceil$ , summing the expected distributed regrets first within an epoch, and then along the horizon yields

$$
\overline {{\mathrm {R E G}}} _ {\mathcal {A}} = \frac {1}{T} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {k} \sum_ {s = 1} ^ {S} \sum_ {t = (s - 1) \Delta_ {\mathrm {e p}} + 1} ^ {s \Delta_ {\mathrm {e p}}} \mathbb {E} [ D _ {i} (t) ] \leq \frac {1}{T} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {k} S \cdot [ O (\tilde {C} ^ {2} \log (\tilde {C})) \Delta_ {\mathrm {e p}} + 2 \mathfrak {X} ] = O (k \tilde {C} ^ {2} \log (\tilde {C})),
$$

where the additional  $2\mathfrak{R}$  term comes from the initialization round and the last round we pull arm  $i$  within an epoch. This concludes the proof if we plug in  $\tilde{C} = c_0\alpha \sigma \sqrt{k}$ .

# 8. Extension on Learning the AR Parameter

In the previous sections, we have assumed that we have full knowledge of the AR parameter, which as discussed in Section 2, is a reasonable assumption when the AR evolution is

introduced by exogenous factors known to the decision-maker. In this section, we discuss an extension to our algorithm that considers the more general setting where the AR parameter needs to be estimated. To do so, we propose a maximum likelihood method for estimating AR parameter. In Section 8.1, we then numerically evaluate the performance of AR2 and a few other benchmark algorithms, and show that even when we use the estimated AR parameter that involves certain level of noises, the performance of AR2 remains competitive.

A Maximum Likelihood Estimator. If the decision maker does not have prior knowledge of the AR parameter  $\alpha$ , one can learn the AR parameter via Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE). In the first  $T_{\mathrm{est}}$  rounds of the time horizon, where  $T_{\mathrm{est}} = O(T^{\beta})$  for some  $\beta \in (0,1)$ , we pull one arm  $i \in [k]$  consecutively for a fixed arm  $i$  and observe rewards  $R_{i}(1), \ldots, R_{i}(T_{\mathrm{est}})$ . We then let  $\widehat{\alpha}$  be the maximum likelihood estimator of  $\alpha$ , which solves the following optimization problem:

$$
\widehat {\alpha} = \underset {\alpha \in (0, 1)} {\arg \min } \mathcal {L} (\alpha), \tag {10}
$$

where  $\mathcal{L}(\alpha)$  is the negative of the log-likelihood function, which is formally defined as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathcal {L} (\alpha) = - \frac {1}{T _ {\mathrm {e s t}}} \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T _ {\mathrm {e s t}}} \mathbb {1} \{R _ {t} = \mathfrak {A} \} \log \left[ 1 - \Phi \left(\frac {\mathfrak {A} - \alpha R _ {t - 1}}{\sigma}\right) \right] + \mathbb {1} \{R _ {t} = - \mathfrak {A} \} \log \left[ \Phi \left(\frac {- \mathfrak {A} - \alpha R _ {t - 1}}{\sigma}\right) \right] \\ + \mathbb {1} \{- \mathfrak {R} <   R _ {t} <   \mathfrak {R} \} \log \left[ \Phi \left(\frac {\mathfrak {R} - \alpha R _ {t - 1}}{\sigma}\right) - \Phi \left(\frac {- \mathfrak {R} - \alpha R _ {t - 1}}{\sigma}\right) \right], \tag {11} \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $\Phi(.)$  is the CDF of standard normal distribution. Note that the three terms in the loss function respectively represent the log likelihood of the observed reward at the upper boundary, at the lower boundary and in between the boundaries.

Observe that here we cannot directly apply linear regression for the estimation of the AR parameter because the existence of truncating boundaries would lead to a biased estimator. The maximum likelihood estimation, on the other hand, remains robust even when the expected reward of our arm hits the boundary multiple times within the horizon. Since the number of rounds used for estimation, i.e.,  $T_{\mathrm{est}} = O(T^{\beta})$ , scales sublinearly with  $T$ , the regret incurred within the first  $T_{\mathrm{est}}$  rounds would not add to the order of our per-round regret upper bound.

In the following proposition, we quantify the amount of noise in the estimated AR parameter by providing an upper bound on the estimation error.

![](images/2c0e01c2584427054a896635cd3954539c93e78cee0bd66568be96e1637ba8b5.jpg)  
Figure 3 Box plots of normalized regret incurred by each algorithm at  $T = 10,000$  (AR2 highlighted in gray). The input AR parameter  $\hat{\alpha}_i$  is an maximum-likelihood estimator of the true AR parameter obtained by solving (10). We vary the magnitudes of AR parameters  $\mathbb{E}[\alpha_i] = \{0.4,0.9\}$  and the number of rounds used for estimation  $T_{\mathrm{est}} = \{50,100,150\}$ . The parameters of our instances are generated in the same way as in Section 7, and we take  $k = 6$ .

PROPOSITION 1. Let  $\widehat{\alpha}$  be the estimated AR parameter obtained by solving (10). Then, for  $\gamma > 0$ , with probability at least  $1 - 2 / T_{est}^{\gamma} - 2\exp(-T_{est}V^{2} / 2\mathfrak{R}^{2})$ , there exists constants  $L_{1}, L_{2}$  such that

$$
| \widehat {\alpha} - \alpha | \leq \frac {4 \sigma \mathcal {R} L _ {1}}{V L _ {2}} \sqrt {\frac {2 \gamma \log T _ {e s t}}{T _ {e s t}}},
$$

where  $V$  is the variance of the observed reward in the stationary distribution, which is independent of our algorithm or the time horizon.

In the case when we have heterogeneous AR parameters  $\alpha_{i}$  for different arms  $i\in [k]$  (as in our numerical studies in Sections 7 and 8.1), one can simply pull each arm  $i\in [k]$  consecutively for  $T_{\mathrm{est}}$  rounds and perform MLE for each  $\alpha_{i}$ .

# 9. Conclusion and Future Directions

We studied a dynamic MAB problem with an AR-based reward structure, which at a high level, draws attention to MAB problems in a rapidly evolving environment that are scarcely studied in the non-stationary bandits literature. Fast-changing temporal structures like AR processes, however, are prevalent in real-world applications. In our work, we highlighted the challenges embedded in learning such fast-changing environments, by establishing a lower bound that scales linearly with the time horizon (see Section 3). This then motivated us to balance two levels of tradeoffs while designing our learning algorithm AR2: (i) exploration and exploitation; (ii) “remembering” and “forgetting”. We showed, both theoretically and numerically, the efficacy of AR2 in online decision-making in an dynamic environment.

One natural direction for further investigation is to model the evolution of rewards using more sophisticated temporal structures, such as AR- $k$  models ( $k > 1$ ) or time series with trends and seasonality. Our work mainly focuses on the AR-1 model since it serves as a good proxy for general AR- $k$  processes (Brockwell and Davis 2016). Nonetheless, the high-level challenges and tradeoffs in our work naturally extend to more complex time series. To consider general AR- $k$  models, for example, one can modify the notions of superior/triggered arms and the estimated reward for each arm accordingly. However, this introduces an extra challenge which we leave for future research: to obtain an estimated reward for each arm, one needs to have at least  $k$  past observations of this arm, but such an estimate can be rather inaccurate if the observations are made from the distant past. Another interesting future direction is to theoretically characterize the regret for the extension to our algorithm that learns the AR parameter via maximum likelihood estimation and makes decisions based on the estimated parameter  $\widehat{\alpha}$ ; see Section 8. Our experiments in Section 8.1 suggest that AR2 attains good performance that is robust to the estimation error. Hence, it is reasonable to expect that we can achieve similar near-optimal performance even with our estimated AR parameter.
