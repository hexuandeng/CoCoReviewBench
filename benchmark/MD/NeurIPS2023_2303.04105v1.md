# Abstract

We propose InCA, a lightweight method for transfer learning that cross-attends to any activation layer of a pretrained model. During training, InCA uses a single forward pass to extract multiple activations, which are passed to external cross-attention adapters, trained anew and combined or selected for downstream tasks. We show that, even when selecting a single top-scoring adapter, InCA achieves performance comparable to full fine-tuning, at a cost comparable to fine-tuning just the last layer. For example, with a cross-attention probe  $1.3\%$  the size of a pre-trained ViT-L/16 model, we achieve performance within  $0.2\%$  of the full fine-tuning paragon at  $51\%$  training cost of the baseline, on average across 11 downstream classification tasks. Unlike other forms of efficient adaptation, InCA does not require backpropagating through the pre-trained model, thus leaving its execution unaltered at both training and inference. The versatility of InCA is best illustrated in fine-grained tasks, which may require accessing information absent in the last layer but accessible in intermediate layer activations. Since the backbone is fixed, InCA allows parallel ensembling as well as parallel execution of multiple tasks. InCA achieves state-of-the-art performance in the ImageNet-to-Sketch multi-task benchmark.

# 1. Introduction

In exciting developments starting with natural language understanding, large neural networks trained on massive datasets, referred to as Foundation Models, promise to achieve top performance with minimal adaptation on any downstream task. In the realm of language, both the data and the hypothesis spaces are shared, and many tasks can be unified into a homogeneous representation [46] that outperforms even task-specific models, thanks to synergistic information among tasks.

Visual inference tasks, on the other hand, are highly heterogeneous and possibly antagonistic: For instance, the hypothesis space for pose estimation is geometric, whereas for scene classification it is semantic. Identity recognition needs to factor out pose, whereas pose estimation needs to factor out identity. Even domains that appear homogeneous, for instance image classification into the 10 CIFAR classes, can trigger interference in the trained model in the presence of modest perturbations of the image statistics such as blurring [2].

Antagonistic tasks interfere within the activations of the latter layers, which cannot be simultaneously optimal (minimal and sufficient) for all domains. Nonetheless, even for a model trained for a specific task, information about an antagonistic task may be present in earlier layers, and certainly in the input data which is trivially sufficient for any task. Of course, going back to the data means training an independent model for each new task, forgoing synergistic information. We need a way of harvesting all and only the relevant information, wherever it may be in the pre-trained model.

Since modern model architectures are deep, and so is the variety of possible downstream tasks, iterating over all the possible representations of a model is prohibitively expensive. To tackle the problem of efficient and comprehensive adaptation, we introduce Introspective Cross-Attention (dubbed InCA), along with "One-to-Many" training that may operate on any number of representations by learning independent lightweight adapters that take as input different activations of a pre-trained model.

In detail, during "One-to-Many" training of InCA, a set of adapters sharing the same architecture are trained independently and in parallel on a downstream task. Each adapter accepts an assigned activation and does not feed back to the backbone, leaving its execution unaltered during both training and inference (Fig. 2). The design approach of InCA, where adapters are trained on top of a frozen model, is computationally efficient during training, making it possible to leverage very large architectures even when compute resources are limited. Fig. 1B shows that on a single V100 GPU one can train InCA with 40 adapters during "One-to-Many" training using an architecture as big as "ViT-Giant/14" or large input resolutions. Because of the fixed network execution, a single backbone inference may support multiple adapter predictions, and during inference the computation graph can again have a "One-to-Many" structure. We showcase this parallel in-

With InCA we are able to significantly reduce computation by sharing the pre-trained backbone computation across an ensemble of diverse adapters.

Each adapter module corresponds to a cross attention layer, and a classification head. Since the adapters are extremely lightweight (1.3% of the parameters of the network) we are able to partially disentangle the cost and requirements of training from the size of the base model. The overall number of parameters of the set of disjoint adapters remains small enough, enabling us to run efficient parallel training of the adapters that extends to massive scale models. After training the different adapters in parallel, one may select top performing adapters, or ensemble multiple adapters to further boost performance.

- Introduce InCA along with "One-to-Many" training that enables utilizing representations of frozen pretrained models and achieve performance within  $0.2\%$  on average as full fine-tuning on ViT-L/16 and within  $0.7\%$  for SWIN-L models when evaluated on a challenging set of downstream tasks.  
- On the efficiency front, InCA enables adapting a ViT-G/14 or ViT-L @ 384 resolution model on a single GPU with regular batch size, uses  $\sim 1\%$  parameters as full fine-tuning, and enables "One-to-Many" parallel inference for multiple tasks.  
- In Section 5 we provide an analytical proof for the advantage of cross-attention extraction as opposed to linear-probing under the presence of "noisy" tokens.

# 2. Related works

We review approaches for knowledge transfer in deep learning, adaptations of transformers, the use of cross attention for information aggregation, and the use of intermediate network representations in deep learning.

Transfer learning Transfer learning in deep neural networks aims at endowing existing models with new "downstream" knowledge. The de-facto approach for transfer learning in deep learning is to modify an existing pretrained network by applying full, partial or linear finetuning on the pre-trained model parameters [27,32,49]. Depending on the domain variability between the source training and downstream task different approaches aim at delineating different transfer domains [1,42], extending coverage of transfer via suitable pre-trainings such as meta-learning [16, 30], or by selecting from a set of expert models [14]. More broadly the field of representation learning focuses on learning transferable representations via different pretraining strategies that can be applicable for downstream tasks [10,28]. InCA is general and can be applied on any vision architecture for classification. We evaluate it for transferring both convolutional and transformer models, yet our main focus is on transferring knowledge from transformer-based vision architectures. Some existing transfer methods are designed for convolutional architectures or filters and pose challenges to generalize to some of the new architectures [39].

Efficient adaptation methods Recently the top-performing pre-trained model architectures are becoming considerably larger, dubbed "foundational models", and we arrive at a cross roads as full-fine-tuning of such large models is becoming increasingly out of reach in many practical settings. Many recent works focus on parameter efficient transfer learning to alleviate the cost of modifying and storing all of the transferred model parameters, which include adaptation works such as [11, 22, 25, 48]. InCA is also very parameter efficient consisting of  $\sim 1\%$  of all model parameters. However, InCA, in contrast with other parameter-efficient adaptation methods that modify the execution of the pre-trained model, is also highly computationally efficient during training and multi-task inference (see Fig. 1B). In the settings of multi-task efficient adaptation learning, we compare our method with other architecture-independent multi-task adaptation works such as [18, 53]. Below we review transformers and the attention mechanisms used therein.

Self-Attention and the Transformer The self-attention mechanism has revolutionized deep learning as being one of the key ingredients of the transformer architecture. During the execution of a self-attention layer, a set of tokens  $[x^1,\ldots x^T]$  aggregate representations selectively from all other tokens  $x^{j}$  to create a corresponding new set of outputs  $[z^1,\dots z^T]$ . Self-attention is given as

$$
z ^ {j} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {T} W _ {V} x ^ {i} \left(\operatorname {s o f t m a x} \left\langle W _ {Q} x ^ {j}, W _ {K} x ^ {i} \right\rangle\right). \forall j \in [ T ] \tag {1}
$$

The softmax is being computed over the  $T$  tokens and the attention weights  $\left(\text{softmax}\langle W_{Q}x^{j},W_{K}x^{i}\rangle\right)$  take into account the importance of token  $x^i$  on the new representation of token  $x^{j}$ . In the influential work introducing the transformer architecture, [52] defines a generalization of (1) where a set of tokens  $[x^{1},\dots x^{T}]$  are passed as input

to  $H$  parallel self-attention "heads" that concatenate their output. Multiple parallel attention heads allow for different attention scoring of different heads and by reducing the dimension of each head the overall computation remains constant. The Transformer architecture uses the multi-head self-attention within transformer block that are stacked with residual connections to build strong representations. Originally the transformer architecture is used in NLP with tokens roughly corresponding to words. To process image data the work [15] "tokenizes" images by vectorizing non-overlapping image patches that are used as in the NLP transformer architecture. With this visual attention corresponds to relations between different image patches.

Cross Attention Cross attention is an attention mechanism, generalizing self-attention, by operating asymmetrically on two sets of tokens  $[x^1,\ldots x^T]\in \mathbb{R}^{d_1\times T}$  and  $[y^1,\dots y^M ]\in \mathbb{R}^{d_2\times M}$  without requiring  $d_{1} = d_{2}$  or  $N = M$  . The cross attention layer is given as follows,

$$
z ^ {j} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {T} W _ {V} x ^ {i} \big (\mathrm {s o f t m a x} \langle W _ {Q} y ^ {j}, W _ {K} x ^ {i} \rangle \big).
$$

Note that the computed output set will have  $M$  tokens corresponding to the  $\{y^i\}$  query set. As a quick check, note cross attention simplifies to self-attention when the same tokens are passed to both inputs cross-attn  $([x^{1},\dots x^{T}],[x^{1},\dots x^{T}]) =$  self-attn  $([x^{1},\dots x^{T}])$  . Nonetheless, in general the complexity of self-attention scales as  $O(T^2)$  whereas the complexity of cross-attention scales as  $O(T\cdot M)$  .As with self-attention, cross-attention is generalized to multi-head cross attention.

Feature extraction with cross attention When using cross attention, one can cross attend between different sets of data inputs or between a set of data inputs and a set of fixed learnable latents. In InCA we apply cross attention with learnable latents taken in as queries. Our adapter architecture uses extracted representations from a pre-trained network as input to the cross-attention, inspired by the cross attention module of Perceiver [24], where the base model activations constitute the input byte array. However, we train multiple adapters in parallel and avoid the iterative re-sampling architecture present in their work. Recently [57] trained a massive captioning model and illustrate lightweight adaptation with the use of a cross attention layer. Nonetheless their study is restricted to a reduced set of tasks, and a single massive pre-trained model. In contrast our work operates on multiple activations via "One-to-Many" training and on a diverse set of downstream fine-grained classification tasks showing various architectures & pre-trainings already contain strong downstream representations. Cross attention layers have been a vital piece of many existing object detection and multi-modal systems that fuse representations [8, 31], or that use cross attention with learnable latents [4,9,24,57,60].

Learning with intermediate features The use of intermediate representations in deep learning is a broad research topic that spans from the works of interpretability [58], to state of the art approaches for object detection that harness intermediate layers for multi-resolution feature pyramids [17, 33, 51] and segmentation [19, 23]. The use of intermediate representations in convolutional architectures is often viewed as harnessing multi-resolution information that's present in the convolutional architectures that have a decreasing spatial resolution. On the other hand this property is not present in the uniform intermediate representations of transformers yet the representations still prove to be useful. For ConvNet architectures, the work of [3] studies classification using linear-probing of intermediate representations and the authors observe a decrease in accuracy when probing earlier layers.

# 3. Method

Let  $f(x) = g_{n} \circ g_{n-1} \circ \ldots \circ g_{1}(x)$  be a pre-trained feedforward neural network of  $n$  layers, with  $g_{j}(\cdot)$  corresponding to the  $j$ -th layer of the network. Let  $\mathcal{D} = \{(x_{i}, y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}$  be the supervised dataset of a downstream task. The aim of InCA is to adapt a model  $f$  to solve the downstream task  $\mathcal{D}$  by identifying existing useful representations in  $f$  and processing them to solve a downstream task while remaining lightweight. A useful baseline to consider is the common practice of linear probing, where we perform lightweight adaptation by adding a trainable linear head on top of the last layer activations  $\hat{y} = \mathrm{head}_{\theta} \circ f(x)$ . This solution is lightweight, both in terms of training time (since the gradients are not propagated through the backbone) and storage. However, the solution has limited accuracy compared to full-model fine-tuning. In Section 5, we argue the reduced accuracy may be due to the fact that task-relevant information may not be available in the last layer of the pretrained network, and that linear probing may not be able to effectively extract specific information in intermediate layers. To bridge this gap, InCA uses representations from any layer along with a novel adapter architecture.

InCA adapter. Let  $f_{m}(x) = g_{m} \circ \ldots \circ g_{1}(x)$  denote the intermediate activation tokens or feature maps of the network at a layer  $m \leq n$ . After extraction of the layer representation, we have access to the large dimensional  $f_{m}(x)$  in our disposal. How can this representation be harnessed? To predict a target label  $\hat{y}$  from the high-dimensional  $f_{m}(x)$  the typical approach is to first reduce the dimension by averaging over a subset of the dimensions and then applying a linear classification head:

$$
\hat {y} = \operatorname {h e a d} \circ \operatorname {a v g - p o o l} \circ f _ {m} (x).
$$

Nonetheless, a simple averaging approach leads to loss of information which we observe empirically in Section 4. In

this work, we claim that for many tasks the needed information is already contained in intermediate layers, however it requires having the right extraction capability. For this, we use a cross-attention layer to aggregate and extract information. The cross-attention operation has the benefit of efficiently mapping the large dimensional  $f_{m}(x)$  to a fixed-sized representation based on the size of the query input. Specifically, we define the InCA adapter layer as follows:

$$
v _ {\mathrm {c r o s s}} (\mathbf {z}) := \operatorname {c r o s s - a t t n} _ {\theta} ([ z ^ {1}, \dots , z ^ {T} ], [ q ])
$$

$$
\operatorname {I n C A} _ {\theta} (\mathbf {z}) := \operatorname {h e a d} _ {\theta} \circ \operatorname {n o r m} \left(v _ {\text {c r o s s}} (\mathbf {z})\right)
$$

where  $q$  is a learnable query tokens (which is part of the parameters  $\theta$  of the adapter layer) and  $\mathbf{z} = f_m(x) = [z^1, \dots, z^K]$  are the tokens of an intermediate layers. The multi-head cross-attention layer output  $v_{\mathrm{cross}}$  corresponding to the query token  $q$  is a learned adaptive representation of the task-relevant information in the activations at layer  $m$ . A classification output  $\hat{y} = \operatorname{InCA}_{\theta}(\mathbf{z})$  is obtained through a fully-connected classification head after normalizing with LayerNorm [5].

The InCA adapter is simple to implement, and only adds a negligible overhead compared with the linear probing baseline both in terms of parameters and inference/training cost. Nonetheless, we show that it can dramatically improve the performance of the adaptation (Table 1) and matches the performance of the full fine-tuning paragon for many models in many tasks. We now discuss several implementation considerations, ablations, and extensions.

Selecting the right layer The InCA adapter can be applied over any intermediate layer of the network. We observe that the performance of InCA on a task can hinge critically on choosing the right intermediate layer to use for that task. Generally, we observe that using the activations immediately following a residual connection tends to perform better. Moreover, we find that middle and later stages of the network provide a stronger representation for the adapter. On the other hand, very early layers may not yet have learned discriminative enough features for most high-level tasks. We also observe that the optimal layers to use for a task tend to remain the same when using different pre-trainings for the backbone and even when using different architectures. For example, the Stanford Cars [29] dataset consistently prefers middle layer representations of the network across different pre-trainings and architectures. Detailed ablations on selecting layers for different tasks and architectures can be found in the supplementary.

One-to-Many training Different intermediate representations of the adapters result in significant differences in performance for fine-grained datasets. Rather than having to sequentially search through  $m$  layers to find the best intermediate layer to attend, we now introduce an efficient mechanism to train adapters on any number of layers at the same time, which we call "One-to-Many" training. On the other hand, for each input mini-batch activations of multiple layers can be computed simultaneously and cached through a single forward pass sharing most of the computations. $^{1}$  The cached activations can then be fed to each adapter in parallel on a single GPU to compute their loss. Two-Stage training In settings where the backbone forward-propagation computation of InCA is too constraining, one may run training in two stages. In the first stage, we cache the activations that serve as the input for the adapter for the entire training set, by running the forward pass for a single epoch. After caching, the second stage proceeds by training the adapters for  $T$  epochs with the cached activations. Suppose per-epoch cost of the pre-trained model forward-propagation is  $C_{PT}$  and adapter optimization epoch is  $C_A$ , then two-stage training reduces the time

of training from  $O((C_{PT} + C_A) \times T)$  to  $O(C_A \times T + C_{PT})$ , where  $C_{PT} \gg C_A$ . With two-stage training, 1 epoch training time is in the order of 5 seconds per 1000 samples, which makes a 30-epoch training job into the order of minutes. We believe that with further optimization of the learning recipe and algorithm for InCA this can be reduced to "real-time" training, which we leave for a future work.

# 4. Experiments

In our experiments, we measure the capabilities of InCA on an array of fine-grain classification datasets. In total, we evaluate the proposed method on an diverse set of 11 FGVC datasets. These datasets consists of CUB-200 [55], Aircrafts, [38], Stanford Cars [29], Stanford Dogs [26], Oxford Flowers 102 [40], MIT-67 [44], Oxford Pets [43]), Describable Textures (DTD) [12], European Flood [6], FGVC Herbarium [41], and EuroSAT Land Use dataset [21]. In Table 3 we explore InCA in the settings of multi-task learning and evaluate it on the ImageNet-to-Sketch benchmark that is comprised of 5 datasets: WikiArt [47], Oxford Pets [43]), Sketch [54], Stanford Cars [29], and CUB-200 [55].

Hyper-parameters In all of our results (including multi-task settings) we use the same training configuration for InCA. We only change the adapter architecture to fit with different architectures and activation sizes. InCA is robust to hyper-parameters and our training schedule is consistent for all runs. This amounts to 30 training epochs, AdamW optimizer [36] with 2 learning rates, and cosine annealing learning schedule; we provide the full training details in the appendix. Note that we do not use modern regularization techniques (e.g. AutoAugment [13], MixUp [59], etc.) that are known to boost performance. While InCA is efficient enough to operate at higher resolutions, we use 224 image resolution unless otherwise stated. We observe that InCA performance improves at 384 resolution (see Table 2) while remaining computationally competitive.

Transfer learning on ViT In Table 1, we demonstrate the transfer performance of InCA applied to ViT-L/16. For each dataset we train InCA and extract activations at residual layers of the ViT, and use the last  $50\%$  of such layers as we observed a clear trend in different models that the very early layers still have rudimentary representations. For the pre-training we use the modern ViT DEIT [50], (see Sup. for ViT-L/16 original pre-training). Using InCA's One-to-Many training procedure, for a single dataset we can train the InCA modules with 2 learning rates in parallel, this corresponds to 26 InCA modules with identical architectures that "listen" to 13 activations. In this case, the total training cost of InCA on a single dataset corresponds to a one pre-trained model run. # 5. Analysis

In Section 4, we consistently observe that using cross-attention as opposed to a linear layer that averages over an activation layer enables InCA to harness existing model representations. Below, we present a theoretical analysis that proves that using a cross attention layer for aggregation as opposed to linear averaging, or even a large dimen

sional feature-concatenation followed by a linear layer is capable of learning over a strictly broader set of data distributions. We give the precise statement in Theorem 1, and intuitively, we argue that cross-attention with learned queries has the ability to sift through irrelevant pieces of the representation that may be at variable positions in different data samples. Recall in the settings considered thus far, the extracted activation of an image data-point is viewed as a 2D matrix  $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times T}$  constructed out of  $T$  tokens e.g.  $\mathbf{x}_i = [x_i^1, x_i^2 \dots x_i^T]$ , with each of  $x_i^j \in \mathbb{R}^d$ . We argue that in many scenarios, task-pertinent information is a property of individual tokens (e.g.  $x_i^j$ ) within a data-point  $\mathbf{x}_i$  and not a property of the overall map. To this end we define a Token-Separability (TS) notion of a dataset.

Definition 1 (Token-separable Dataset). A dataset  $\mathcal{D} = \{(\mathbf{x}_1, y_1), \ldots, (\mathbf{x}_n, y_n)\}$  with  $\mathbf{x}_i = [x_i^1, x_i^2, \ldots, x_i^T] \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times T}$  and  $y_i \in \{-1, 1\}$  is said to be linearly-token-separable if there exists a scalar  $c > 0$  and  $w \in \mathbb{R}^d$  satisfying  $\| w \|_2 = 1$ , such that for each data point  $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathcal{D}$  there exists a token  $x_i^{j_i} \in \mathbf{x}_i$  with

$$
y _ {i} \left(\left\langle x _ {i} ^ {j _ {i}}, w \right\rangle + b\right) \geq c. \tag {2}
$$

We define  $w_0$  and  $c_0$  as the maximal margin solution and maximum margin, i.e.  $c_0 = \max_{\{\| w\| = 1,b\in \mathbb{R}\}}\min_{\mathcal{D}}\{y_i(\langle x_i^j,w\rangle +b)\}$  for  $\mathcal{D}$ .

Intuitively,  $\mathcal{D}$  is a TS-dataset if each of its data points contain a token that leads to linear separability (the same  $w$  shared among all points  $\mathbf{x}_i\in \mathcal{D}$ ). One can further distinguish between aligned-TS Datasets, where the index  $j_{i}$  of the linearly separating token is consistent among the  $n$  data points, or permutable-TS where  $j$  is dependent on  $i$ . We present an analytical statement for the advantage of cross-attn, the theorem is provided for binary classification via a scalar prediction, but can conventionally extend to  $C$ -class classification. For binary classification we define a prediction via the standard scalar binary aggregator as  $\sigma(u) = \mathrm{sign}(\sum_{i} u_{i})$  that converts a vector into a binary prediction. With this we present the theorem.

Theorem 1. Let  $\mathcal{D}$  be a binary-class, token-separable dataset with max-margin  $c_{\mathcal{D}}$ , suppose that  $\mathcal{D}$  is distributed such that for  $\mathbf{x}_i = (x_i^1,\ldots x_i^T) = \mathbf{x}_i\in \mathcal{D}$  are normalized for separating tokens  $x_{i}^{j_{i}}\in \mathcal{D}$  and that the rest of the tokens correspond to "noise", as  $x_{i}^{k}\sim N\left(0,I_{d}\cdot \frac{1}{d}\right)$ ,  $\mathbb{E}\| x_i^k\| _2^2 = 1$ . Assume  $c_{ca,\mathcal{D}}$  satisfies

$$
c _ {\mathcal {D}} \geq c _ {c a, \mathcal {D}} = \sqrt {\frac {8}{d} \left(\log (1 / \delta) + \log (2 T)\right)}. \tag {3}
$$

Then with probability at least  $1 - \delta$  there exists a cross attention classifier

$$
f (x; q, \{W \}) = \sigma (\operatorname {c r o s s - a t t n} (\mathbf {x}, q)) \tag {4}
$$

that separates  $\mathcal{D}$ . In contrast, for the linear classifier family,

$$
g (\mathbf {x}; w) = \sigma (\mathbf {x} \cdot w) \tag {5}
$$

there does not exist a classifier that separates  $\mathcal{D}$  with a probability at least  $A_0\cdot \exp (-\frac{d}{T} (c_{\mathcal{D}})^2)\sim \exp (-\frac{\log(T)}{T})$

The proof of the theorem is relegated to the supplement. In InCA, we use the same cross-attn layer with latent  $q$ , which we show can be simplified via reparameterization.

Observation 1 (Query Collapse Reparameterization). A single-head cross attention parameterization with latent  $q$  (2), is equivalent to the following simplified layer, cross-attn(x,q) = ∑ softmax(q* x) ⊙ Wx.

This can be derived by decomposing the attention score, that is the input to softmax.

$$
\begin{array}{l} a _ {j} = \langle \mathbf {W} _ {q} q, \mathbf {W} _ {k} x ^ {j} \rangle = (\mathbf {W} _ {q} q) ^ {\top} (\mathbf {W} _ {k} x ^ {j}) = \\ q ^ {\top} \mathbf {W} _ {q} ^ {\top} \mathbf {W} _ {k} x ^ {j} = (x ^ {j}) ^ {\top} q ^ {*}. \\ \end{array}
$$

Where  $q^{*} = q^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{q}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{k}$ , and  $q^{*}\in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ , hence the cross-attn layer simplifies, which is used in the proof.

As our proof shows, the cross-attn layer can operate on a large data bandwidth, e.g.  $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times T}$  while still being selective in finding task specific representations. Empirically we also observe that increasing the number of heads of cross-attn also improves the performance of the InCA (see Sup.). This is in part because it enables the learned latent query parameter  $q$  to identify more useful token patterns, and since  $q$  is fixed using more heads remain stable (as opposed to when  $q$  is a data input).

# 6. Discussion

In this work, we present InCA as a lightweight method for transfer learning. We show that InCA is general and achieves strong performance across a variety of architectures, pre-training and learning settings. We observe

that the adapter architecture of InCA is especially suited to transformer based architectures on which it is capable of reaching comparable full fine tuning performance on an extended set of challenging fine-grained visual classification tasks. Due to the non-propagation of gradients, "One-to-Many" and "two stage" training strategies, InCA is especially desirable approach to tap the strengths of large-scale models efficiently and InCA is significantly more efficient and scalable to train, and avoids expensive hyper-parameter sweeps. Besides the training process, we effectively harness InCA in multi-task learning and ensembling by running multiple learned adapters in parallel, we believe this property can be valuable to many learning applications and leave further flexible learning applications as future work. Our contribution also analyzes InCA considering the use of intermediate representation, as well as the application of the cross attention layer and we prove the first result for the advantage of cross attention for extraction. Overall we conclude that InCA provides evidence that various pre-trained architectures are already endowed with relevant representations sufficient to solving downstream tasks, and that the previous bottleneck has been in their effective extraction.
