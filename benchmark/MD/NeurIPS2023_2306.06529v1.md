# Abstract

Injective multiset functions have a key role in the theoretical study of machine learning on multisets and graphs. Yet, there remains a gap between the provably injective multiset functions considered in theory, which typically rely on polynomial moments, and the multiset functions used in practice which typically rely on neural moments, whose injectivity on multisets has not been studied to date.

In this paper we bridge this gap by showing that moments of neural network do define an injective multiset function, provided that an analytic non-polynomial activation is used. The number of moments required by our theory is optimal up to a multiplicative factor of two. To prove this result, we state and prove a finite witness theorem, which is of independent interest.

As a corollary to our main theorem, we derive new approximation results for functions on multisets and measures, and new separation results for graph neural networks. We also provide two negative results: We show that (1) moments of piecewise-linear neural networks do not lead to injective multiset functions, and (2) even when moment-based multiset functions are injective, they will never be bi-Lipschitz.

# 1 Introduction

A finite multiset  $\{\{x_1,\ldots ,x_k\}\}$  is an unordered collection of  $k$  elements where repetitions are allowed. Starting from the seminal Deep-Sets paper [38], there has been significant interest in learning functions that are defined on multisets [29, 21, 39, 40], using architectures that respect their unordered structure. Multiset functions also arise naturally as the aggregation functions in message passing architectures for graphs.

Multiset functions that are injective have emerged as a useful concept in the study of neural networks for multisets and graphs. Their importance stems mainly from the following two seminal results:

(a) Universality for multisets. Under some weak assumptions, any multiset function  $g$  can be exactly expressed as a composition  $g = h \circ f$  where  $f$  is an injective multiset function, and  $h$  is some vector-to-vector function [38, 36]. This characterization is useful for proving universal approximation properties of architectures for multisets.  
(b) Separation of Graph Neural Networks. The separation power of Message-Passing Neural Networks (MPNNs) is equivalent to that of the Weisfeiler-Leman (WL) graph isomorphism test, assuming that the multiset functions used in the message-passing procedure are injective [37]. Injective multiset functions are also used in various generalizations of this result, such as the equivalence of high-order Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) to high-order WL tests [26, 23], and recent results that study geometric GNNs and corresponding geometric WL tests [14, 15, 22, 28, 7].

Injective multiset functions The domain of injective multiset functions is typically a collection of multisets whose elements come from a fixed alphabet  $\Omega \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ , and whose cardinality  $k$  is bounded by some fixed  $n$ . We denote this collection of multisets by  $\mathcal{S}_{\leq n}(\Omega)$ .

Most injective multiset functions  $\hat{f}$  in the literature are defined by summing a function  $f:\Omega \to \mathbb{R}^m$  over all multiset entries:

$$
\hat {f} \left( \right.\left\{ \right.\left. \right.\left. \right.\left. \right.\left. \right.\left. \right.\left. \right.\left. \right.\left. \right.\left. \right.\left.\left.\left.\left.\left.\left.\left.\left.\left.\left.\left.\left.\left.\left.\left.\left.\left.\left.\left.\left.\left.\left.\left.\left.\left.\right.\right.\right.\right.\right.\right.\right.\right.\right.\right.\right.\right.\right.\right.\right.\right.\right.\right.\right.\right\}\right\}\right) = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {k} f \left(x _ {i}\right). \right.\right.\right.
$$

This is a well-defined multiset function, as the sum does not depend on the order of  $\pmb{x}_1, \dots, \pmb{x}_k$ . When  $\hat{f}$  is injective we say that it is an injective multiset function on  $S_{\leq n}(\Omega)$ , and that the original  $f$  is moment-injective on  $S_{\leq n}(\Omega)$ . We call  $m$  the embedding dimension of  $\hat{f}$ .

In [37] it is shown that when  $\Omega$  is countable,  $S_{\leq n}(\Omega)$  can be mapped injectively using a function  $f$  with embedding dimension  $m = 1$ . In [35, 5, 36] it is shown that when  $\Omega = \mathbb{R}$  and  $f$  is continuous, an embedding dimension of  $m \geq n$  is necessary for moment injectivity on  $S_{\leq n}(\Omega)$ . Moreover, there is a polynomial  $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^n$  that achieves this lower bound.

In a Euclidean space  $\Omega = \mathbb{R}^d$  with  $d > 1$ , the situation is similar: On the one hand, it was shown in [15] that  $n \cdot d$  is a lower bound on the embedding dimension  $m$  achievable by any moment-injective  $f$  that is continuous. On the other hand, while some moment-injective polynomials discussed in the literature have very high embedding-dimension [3, 23, 32], recent work [8] showed that an embedding dimension of  $m = 2nd + 1$  can be achieved with a polynomial  $f$  with randomly chosen coefficients. Thus, the lower bound on  $m$  is achieved up to a multiplicative factor of 2.

In this paper we address the following caveat: while the works detailed above provide us with moment injective functions of optimal or near-optimal embedding dimension, these are not the functions used in practice in learning algorithms, where typically  $f$  is a multilayer perceptron (MLP). In some papers [37, 23], this gap is justified by the fact that MLPs can approximate any function, and hence also any moment injective function. However, using this argument we have no control on the number of hidden features needed to achieve moment-injectivity, and it is not even clear that there is a network of finite size that achieves separation. Thus, the main question we focus on in this paper is whether MLPs are moment injective, and if so how many parameters are necessary to achieve this injectivity.

We also address a natural generalization of this question from multisets to measures. Note that if we identify multisets  $\{\{\pmb{x}_1,\dots ,\pmb{x}_k\}\}$  with measures  $\mu = \sum_{j = 1}^{k}\delta_{\pmb{x}_j}$ , then the induced multiset function  $\hat{f}$  of (1) is just the integral of  $f$  with respect to the measure  $\mu$ . Thus, we could generalize the notion of moment injectivity from multisets to discrete signed measures  $\mu = \sum_{i = 1}^{n}w_{i}\delta_{\pmb{x}_{i}}$  with weights  $w_{i}\in \mathbb{R}$  and points  $\pmb{x}_i$  coming from some alphabet  $\Omega$ . A function  $f:\Omega \to \mathbb{R}^m$  induces a moment function  $\hat{f}$  defined for measures of the form  $\mu = \sum_{i = 1}^{n}w_{i}\delta_{\pmb{x}_{i}}$  by

$$
\hat {f} (\mu) = \int_ {\Omega} f (\boldsymbol {x}) d \mu (\boldsymbol {x}) = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} f \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {i}\right). \tag {2}
$$

We would like  $\hat{f}$  to be injective on the space of all measures on  $\Omega$  supported on  $k\leq n$  points. We note this space<sup>1</sup> of measures by  $\mathcal{M}_{\leq n}(\Omega)$ . The subset of  $\mathcal{M}_{\leq n}(\Omega)$  described by weights  $w_{i}\in \{0,1\}$  corresponds to the space of multiset sets  $\mathcal{S}_{\leq n}(\Omega)$ .

To summarize, the main question we focus on in this paper is:

Main Question: Do the moments of MLPs define an injective function on spaces of multisets  $S_{\leq n}(\Omega)$  and measures  $\mathcal{M}_{\leq n}(\Omega)$ ? If so, how many neurons are needed to achieve injectivity?

Our proof of moment injectivity for MLPs with non-polynomial analytic activation consists of two steps: First, we show that non-polynomial activation functions  $\sigma$  are discriminatory, meaning that measures  $\mu \in \mathcal{M}_{\leq n}(\Omega)$  are uniquely determined by the integrals

$$
\int_ {\Omega} \sigma (\boldsymbol {a} \cdot \boldsymbol {x} + b) d \mu (\boldsymbol {x}), \quad \boldsymbol {a} \in \mathbb {R} ^ {d}, b \in \mathbb {R}
$$

for all values of  $\pmb{a}, b$ . Then we prove a theorem we name the finite witness theorem, which implies that if  $\sigma$  is, in addition, analytic, then measures  $\mu \in \mathcal{M}_{\leq n}(\Omega)$  are in fact determined by a finite number of such integrals, with parameters  $\pmb{a}_i, b_i, i = 1, \dots, m$ . Here  $m$  is proportional to the dimension of  $\mathcal{M}_{\leq n}(\Omega)$ , and this property holds for Lebesgue almost any choice of parameters  $\{(a_i, b_i)\}_{i=1}^m$ .

In essence, the finite witness theorem reduces an infinite, parametric family of analytic equality constraints, to a finite subset of that family. We believe it shall find applications beyond the multi-set/measure setting discussed here. This theorem is a generalization of the results in [8], where a special case of it was proved in the setting of semialgebraic domains and functions. The theorem we prove here extends these results to a much wider collection of domains and functions, among which are analytic functions. These results are described in detail in Appendix A.

In Section 6 we discuss some corollaries from our main result. We prove all continuous functions on spaces of multiset measures, can be characterized as a composition of a continuous vector-to-vector function and the moment injective MLPs we found. Essentially, this result enables replacing the moment injective polynomials in the classical characterizations of multiset functions [38, 36] with MLPs. We also show that MPNNs with analytic activations are equivalent to 1-WL, even when the parameters are chosen randomly and only a single hidden feature is used throughout the message passing procedure. This can be compared on the one hand with the construction in [37], which requires a single hidden feature but uses multiset aggregators that are not MLPs, and on the other hand with works that do consider MLPs with ReLU activations [26, 1], but require a number of neurons which depends polylogarithmically on the number of nodes, while our construction requires a small constant number (though we have no bounds on the number of bits necessary for achieving separation using floating-point arithmetic). # 3 Moment injectivity with analytic activations

In this section we prove the moment injectivity of MLPs with analytic activations. We begin by showing that for almost all analytic activations  $\sigma$ , a measure  $\mu \in \mathcal{M}_{\leq n}(\Omega)$  is uniquely determined by the integrals of all functions of the form  $\{\sigma(\boldsymbol{a} \cdot x + b) \mid \boldsymbol{a} \in \mathbb{R}^d, b \in \mathbb{R}\}$ . When this holds we say that the activation  $\sigma$  is discriminatory:

Definition 3.1. Let  $\sigma : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$  be a continuous function. We say that  $\sigma$  is discriminatory if for any signed Borel measure  $\mu$  on  $\mathbb{R}^d$  that is finite (i.e.  $|\mu(A)| < \infty$  for all Borel  $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ ), compactly supported and not identically zero, there exist  $a \in \mathbb{R}^d$ ,  $b \in \mathbb{R}$  such that

$$
\int_ {\mathbb {R} ^ {d}} \sigma (\boldsymbol {a} \cdot \boldsymbol {x} + b) d \mu (\boldsymbol {x}) \neq 0. \tag {4}
$$

Our definition of discriminatory activation functions comes from Cybenko's celebrated paper on the universality of MLPs [6]. In this paper, it is proved that sigmoid-like activation functions are discriminatory, and this is used to show universality of MLPs with such activations. Later papers [27] showed that universality can be achieved for all continuous non-polynomial activations. In the following simple proposition, we use a reverse argument to that used by Cybenko, and show that activations that allow for universality are automatically discriminatory:

Proposition 3.2. Let  $\sigma : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$  be a continuous non-polynomial function, then  $\sigma$  is discriminatory.

Proof idea. By the universality theorem for shallow MLPs [27], all continuous functions can be approximated by linear combinations of functions of the form  $\sigma (\pmb {a}\cdot \pmb {x} + b)$ . Thus if the integral of  $\sigma (\pmb {a}\cdot \pmb {x} + b)$  according to  $\mu$  is zero for all  $\pmb {a},\pmb{b}$ , then  $\mu$  is the zero measure.

Next, we shall prove our main result: if  $\sigma$  is analytic and discriminatory, then shallow MLPs of reasonable width with  $\sigma$  as activation are moment injective.

Theorem 3.3. Let  $\sigma : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$  be an analytic function that is not a polynomial, let  $n, d \in \mathbb{N}$ , and set  $m = 2n(d + 1) + 1$ . Then for Lebesgue almost every  $\pmb{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d}$ ,  $\pmb{b} \in \mathbb{R}^m$ , the shallow MLP  $\sigma(\pmb{A} \cdot \pmb{x} + \pmb{b})$  is moment injective on  $\mathcal{M}_{\leq n}(\mathbb{R}^d)$ .

For moment injectivity on  $\mathcal{S}_{\leq n}(\mathbb{R}^d)$  (respectively  $\mathcal{M}_{\leq n}(\Sigma)$  or  $\mathcal{S}_{\leq n}(\Sigma)$  with  $\Sigma$  countable) it suffices to take  $m = 2nd + 1$  (respectively  $m = 2n + 1$  or  $m = 1$ ).

Proof. Set  $m = 2n(d + 1) + 1$ . Recall that a measure in  $\mathcal{M}_{\leq n}(\mathbb{R}^d)$  is determined (albeit not uniquely) by a matrix  $\mathbf{X} = (\pmb{x}_1, \dots, \pmb{x}_n) \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$  that represents  $n$  points in  $\mathbb{R}^d$ , and a weight vector  $\pmb{w} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ . Let  $\mathbb{M}$  denote the space of pairs of measure parameters

$$
\mathbb {M} = \left\{\left(\boldsymbol {w}, \boldsymbol {w} ^ {\prime}, \boldsymbol {X}, \boldsymbol {X} ^ {\prime}\right) \in \mathbb {R} ^ {n} \times \mathbb {R} ^ {n} \times \mathbb {R} ^ {d \times n} \times \mathbb {R} ^ {d \times n} \right\},
$$

and let

$$
F (\boldsymbol {w}, \boldsymbol {w} ^ {\prime}, \boldsymbol {X}, \boldsymbol {X} ^ {\prime}; \boldsymbol {a}, b) = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} \sigma (\boldsymbol {a} \cdot \boldsymbol {x} _ {i} + b) - \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} ^ {\prime} \sigma (\boldsymbol {a} \cdot \boldsymbol {x} _ {i} ^ {\prime} + b). \tag {5}
$$

Note that since  $\sigma$  is discriminatory,  $F(\boldsymbol{w}, \boldsymbol{w}', \boldsymbol{X}, \boldsymbol{X}'; \boldsymbol{a}, b) \neq 0$  if and only if the two measures induced by  $(\boldsymbol{w}, \boldsymbol{X})$  and by  $(\boldsymbol{w}', \boldsymbol{X}')$  are distinct. In that case, the two measures can be distinguished by integrating  $\sigma(\boldsymbol{a} \cdot \boldsymbol{x} + b)$  against them. We prove the theorem by showing that for Lebesgue almost every  $\boldsymbol{A} = (\boldsymbol{a}_1, \ldots, \boldsymbol{a}_m) \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d}$  and  $\boldsymbol{b} = (b_1, \ldots, b_m) \in \mathbb{R}^m$ ,

$$
\begin{array}{l} \left\{\left(\boldsymbol {w}, \boldsymbol {w} ^ {\prime}, \boldsymbol {X}, \boldsymbol {X} ^ {\prime}\right) \in \mathbb {M} \mid \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} \delta_ {\boldsymbol {x} _ {i}} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} ^ {\prime} \delta_ {\boldsymbol {x} _ {i} ^ {\prime}} \right\} \\ \stackrel {(*)} {=} \left\{\left(\boldsymbol {w}, \boldsymbol {w} ^ {\prime}, \boldsymbol {X}, \boldsymbol {X} ^ {\prime}\right) \in \mathbb {M} \mid F \left(\boldsymbol {w}, \boldsymbol {w} ^ {\prime}, \boldsymbol {X}, \boldsymbol {X} ^ {\prime}; \boldsymbol {a}, b\right) = 0, \forall \boldsymbol {a} \in \mathbb {R} ^ {d}, b \in \mathbb {R} \right\} \\ \stackrel {(*)} {=} \left\{\left(\boldsymbol {w}, \boldsymbol {w} ^ {\prime}, \boldsymbol {X}, \boldsymbol {X} ^ {\prime}\right) \in \mathbb {M} \mid F \left(\boldsymbol {w}, \boldsymbol {w} ^ {\prime}, \boldsymbol {X}, \boldsymbol {X} ^ {\prime}; \boldsymbol {a} _ {i}, b _ {i}\right) = 0, \forall i = 1, \dots , m \right\} \\ \stackrel {(*)} {=} \left\{\left(\boldsymbol {w}, \boldsymbol {w} ^ {\prime}, \boldsymbol {X}, \boldsymbol {X} ^ {\prime}\right) \in \mathbb {M} \mid \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} \sigma \left(\boldsymbol {A} \boldsymbol {x} _ {i} + \boldsymbol {b}\right) = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} ^ {\prime} \sigma \left(\boldsymbol {A} \boldsymbol {x} _ {i} ^ {\prime} + \boldsymbol {b}\right) \right\}. \\ \end{array}
$$

This is indeed sufficient to prove the theorem as the equality of the first and last sets shows that the function  $\sigma(A\pmb{x} + \pmb{b})$  separates all pairs of distinct measures in  $\mathcal{M}_{\leq n}(\mathbb{R}^d)$ .

Note that the equality  $(\ast)$  follows from the fact that  $\sigma$  is discriminatory, and therefore two measures in  $\mathcal{M}_{\leq n}(\mathbb{R}^d)$  can be separated by some pair  $(a,b)$  if and only if they are not identical. The last equality  $(\ast\ast\ast)$  is obtained by simple reordering of terms. Finally, the equality  $(\ast\ast)$  follows from the following theorem, which we name the finite witness theorem. We prove a stronger version of this theorem in the appendix:

Theorem 3.4. (Finite Witness Theorem, first version) Let  $\mathbb{M} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^L$  be a countable union of affine subspaces of  $\mathbb{R}^L$ , all of dimension  $\leq D$ , and let  $F: \mathbb{R}^L \times \mathbb{R}^{D_\theta} \to \mathbb{R}$  be an analytic function. Then for almost any  $\theta^{(1)}, \ldots, \theta^{(D+1)}$  in  $\mathbb{R}^{D_\theta}$ ,

$$
\{z \in \mathbb {M} \mid F (z; \theta) = 0, \forall \theta \in \mathbb {R} ^ {D _ {\theta}} \} = \{z \in \mathbb {M} \mid F (z; \theta^ {(i)}) = 0, \forall i = 1, \dots D + 1 \}.
$$

This concludes the proof of moment injectivity on  $\mathcal{M}_{\leq n}(\mathbb{R}^d)$ . If we are only interested in moment injectivity on  $S_{\leq n}(\mathbb{R}^d)$ , it is sufficient to apply the theorem to the domain

$$
\mathbb{M} = \bigcup_{\boldsymbol {w},\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}\in \{0,1\}^{n}}\left\{(\boldsymbol {w},\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime},\boldsymbol {X},\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime})| \boldsymbol {X},\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\right\} ,
$$

which is a finite union of affine subspaces of dimension  $2nd$ . Thus, the theorem only requires  $m = 2nd + 1$  to achieve injectivity on  $S_{\leq n}(\mathbb{R}^d)$ . Similarly, when considering  $\mathcal{M}_{\leq n}(\Sigma)$  with a countable  $\Sigma$ , the theorem can be applied to a domain  $\mathbb{M}$  that can be written as a countable union of affine spaces of dimension  $2n$ , which yields  $m = 2n + 1$ . Finally,  $S_{\leq n}(\Sigma)$  is a countable union of points, namely zero-dimensional affine subspaces, and therefore  $m = 1$  is sufficient in this case.

# 3.1 Finite witness theorem

We believe Theorem 3.4 shall prove useful for proving moment injectivity of classes of functions, beyond the pointwise-activated form of 3, and possibly for other related problems. As an example, we show in the following proposition that Gaussian functions with random parameters are moment injective:

Proposition 3.5. Let  $n, d \in \mathbb{N}$ , and set  $m = 2n(d + 1) + 1$ . Then for Lebesgue almost every  $(\pmb{y}_i, \sigma_i)_{i=1}^m$ , the function

$$
f (\boldsymbol {x}) = \left(\exp \left(- \frac {\| \boldsymbol {x} - \boldsymbol {y} _ {1} \| ^ {2}}{\sigma_ {1} ^ {2}}\right), \dots , \exp \left(- \frac {\| \boldsymbol {x} - \boldsymbol {y} _ {m} \| ^ {2}}{\sigma_ {m} ^ {2}}\right)\right)
$$

is moment injective on  $\mathcal{M}_{\leq n}(\mathbb{R}^d)$ .

Proof idea. Any two measures with bounded support can be separated by the moment of a Gaussian function supported in a small ball around a point where the measures disagree. Thus a measure in  $\mathcal{M}_{\leq n}(\Omega)$  is uniquely defined by the continuous family of all its Gaussian moments. The finite witness theorem then shows that a finite number  $m$  suffices.

We note that the proof of the finite witness theorem is non-trivial, and we regard it as the main technical contribution in this work. In Appendix A we provide the proof of this theorem, along with a much stronger version, which allows  $\mathbb{M}$  to be not only a countable union of affine sets, but any  $\sigma$ -subanalytic set. The analyticity assumption on  $F$  can also be relaxed to a weaker assumption — that  $F$  is a  $\sigma$ -subanalytic function, although this requires an additional (moderate) assumption on  $F$ . The definition of these concepts appear in the appendix.

As an example of the usefulness of this more general version of the finite witness theorem, in the appendix we prove the following generalization of Theorem 3.4

Proposition 3.6. Let  $\sigma : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$  be an analytic function that is not a polynomial. Let  $n, d \in \mathbb{N}$  and set  $m = 2n(d + 1) + 1$ . Let  $F: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^L$  be an injective function that is a composition of  $PwL$  functions and analytic functions. Then for Lebesgue almost every  $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d_L}$ ,  $b \in \mathbb{R}^m$ , the function  $\sigma(AF(x) + b)$  is moment injective on  $\mathcal{M}_{\leq n}(\mathbb{R}^d)$ .

In particular,  $F$  could be a neural network with increasing widths, linear layers with full rank, and injective activations that are either  $\mathrm{PwL}$  or analytic (such as leaky ReLU or sigmoid). Therefore, Proposition 3.6 shows that making the network deeper will not have a negative effect on its moment-injectivity. While this may seem trivial, what is not immediate in this formulation is that the embedding dimension  $m$  depends linearly on  $n\cdot d$  rather than  $n\cdot L$ . The reason this is true is that the shallow neural network applied to  $F(\pmb {x})$  will only 'see' features from the set  $F(\mathbb{R}^d)$ , and in the appendix we will show that this is a  $\sigma$ -subanalytic set of dimension  $\leq d$ .

# 4 Failure of Moment injectivity for PwL networks

In this section we show that moment injectivity for MLPs with PwL activation functions (such as ReLU, leaky ReLU or the hard hyperbolic tangent) is in general impossible when the alphabet is infinite. On the other hand, it is possible for finite alphabets and for some irregular countable-infinite alphabets.

Our first negative result for PwL activations pertains continuous features:

Proposition 4.1. Let  $d, n \geq 2$  be natural numbers and  $\Omega \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$  an open set. If  $\psi: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^m$  is piecewise linear, then it is not moment injective on  $S_{\leq n}(\Omega)$ .

Proof. We can choose some  $\pmb{x}_0$  such that  $\psi(\pmb{x})$  is of the form  $\psi(\pmb{x}) = \pmb{A}\pmb{x} + \pmb{b}$  in a small enough open neighborhood  $U$  of  $\pmb{x}_0$ . Now choose some arbitrary non-zero vector  $\pmb{d} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ . For small enough  $\epsilon$  we have that  $\pmb{x}_0 + \epsilon\pmb{d}$  and  $\pmb{x}_0 - \epsilon\pmb{d}$  are in  $U$ . It follows that the multiset  $\{\{\pmb{x}_0, \pmb{x}_0\}\}$  and  $\{\{\pmb{x}_0 - \epsilon\pmb{d}, \pmb{x}_0 + \epsilon\pmb{d}\}\}$  have the same moments:

$$
\boldsymbol {\psi} (\boldsymbol {x} _ {0}) + \boldsymbol {\psi} (\boldsymbol {x} _ {0}) = 2 (\boldsymbol {A} \boldsymbol {x} _ {0} + \boldsymbol {b}) = \boldsymbol {A} (\boldsymbol {x} _ {0} - \epsilon \boldsymbol {d}) + \boldsymbol {b} + \boldsymbol {A} (\boldsymbol {x} _ {0} + \epsilon \boldsymbol {d}) + \boldsymbol {b} = \boldsymbol {\psi} (\boldsymbol {x} _ {0} - \epsilon \boldsymbol {d}) + \boldsymbol {\psi} (\boldsymbol {x} _ {0} + \epsilon \boldsymbol {d}).
$$

This proves that  $\psi$  is not moment injective on  $S_{\leq n}(\Omega)$ .

The basic idea behind the proof of Proposition 4.1 was that inside a linear region of  $\psi$ , different multisets with the same sum will have the same moments. This same idea can be used to prove failure of moment injectivity for  $\mathcal{M}_{\leq n}(\Omega)$  for any infinite  $\Omega$ , and for  $S_{\leq n}(\mathbb{Z}^d)$ . On the other hand, PwL networks can be moment injective for finite alphabets, as well as on  $S_{\leq n}(\Sigma)$  when  $\Sigma$  is a somewhat pathological infinite countable alphabet. These results are described in Appendix B.

# 5 Instability of moment injective functions

So far, we have shown that neural networks  $f: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^m$  can induce injective functions  $\hat{f}: S_{\leq n}(\mathbb{R}^d) \to \mathbb{R}^m$ . Ideally, we would like such  $\hat{f}$  to be bi-Lipschitz, meaning that there exist constants  $0 < c \leq C$  such that

$$
c \| \hat {f} (S _ {1}) - \hat {f} (S _ {2}) \| \leq W _ {2} (S _ {1}, S _ {2}) \leq C \| \hat {f} (S _ {1}) - \hat {f} (S _ {2}) \|, \quad \forall S _ {1}, S _ {2} \in \mathcal {S} _ {\leq n} (\mathbb {R} ^ {d}), \tag {6}
$$

where  $W_{2}(S_{1}, S_{2})$  denotes the 2-Wasserstein distance between the measures  $\mu_{i}, i = 1, 2$  that assign unit weights to the points in  $S_{i}$ . Moreover, one would ideally want the ratio  $c / C$  to be as close as possible to one. This would ensure that the embedding does not significantly distort the distances in the original space of measures. Unfortunately, we find that the multiset function induced by the moments of any function  $f: \mathbb{R}^{d} \to \mathbb{R}^{m}$  that is differentiable in at least one point, will not be bi-Lipschitz:

Theorem 5.1. Let  $n \geq 2$  and  $d, m$  be natural numbers, and let  $f: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^m$  be differentiable at some point  $x_0 \in \mathbb{R}^d$ . Then the induced moment function  $\hat{f}: S_{\leq n}(\mathbb{R}^d) \to \mathbb{R}^m$  defined in (1) is not bi-Lipschitz.

This result points to a possible disadvantage of injective multiset functions based on moments, in comparison with those based on sorting [3] or 'max-filters' [4], which are bi-Lipschitz.

Figure 1(b) visualizes the underlying reason for the failure of bi-Lipschitzness discussed above, and its relationship to the non-injectivity of PwL networks: in the left we consider a shallow neural network  $f:\mathbb{R}\to \mathbb{R}^{10}$  with ReLU activations and the induced moment function  $\hat{f} (\{x_1,x_2\}) = f(x_1) + f(x_2)$  on multisets of two points. The left hand side of the figure visualizes the ratio  $\sigma_{2} / \sigma_{1}$  of the smallest and largest singular values of the differential matrix of  $\hat{f}$ . The function  $f$  is PwL, and its linear regions in [0, 1] are four intervals  $I_{1},\ldots ,I_{4}$ . The linear regions of  $\hat{f}$  are the rectangles  $I_{i}\times I_{j}$ , and there are degeneracies in the rectangles that intersect the diagonal, as for all small enough  $\epsilon$ , we have  $\hat{f} (\{x_0 + \epsilon ,x_0 - \epsilon \}) = \hat{f} (\{x_0,x_0\})$  as shown in the proof of Proposition 4.1. The right-hand side of the figure depicts the same ratio of singular values when  $f$  is defined using the analytic SiLU activation. We see that the differential of  $\hat{f}$  is singular on the diagonal. Intuitively, this is because the differentiability of  $f$  implies that near the diagonal it behaves like a linear function, up to second order corrections. The singularity of  $\hat{f}$  on the line does not stop it from being injective, but it does prevent it from being bi-Lipschitz. A full proof of this phenomenon is given in the appendix.

# 6 Applications: Approximation and graph separation

In the beginning of the paper, we mentioned that injective multiset functions are useful for constructing multiset architectures with universal approximation properties, and for proving separation results for graph neural networks. In this section we discuss some immediate corollaries of our results for these two applications.

# 6.1 Approximation of functions on multisets and measures

Our first approximation result focuses on the space of multisets of a fixed size  $n$  with an alphabet  $K$  that is a compact subset of  $\mathbb{R}^d$ . Any such multiset is defined by a choice of  $n$  vectors in  $K$ , where permuting the orders of the vectors does not change the obtained multiset. Thus multiset functions on this collection are equivalent to permutation-invariant functions on  $K^n$ . We prove:

Corollary 6.1. Let  $n, d \in \mathbb{N}$  and set  $m = 2nd + 1$ . Let  $\sigma : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$  be a non-polynomial analytic function. Let  $K \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$  be a compact set. Then there exist  $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d}$ ,  $b \in \mathbb{R}^d$  such that for any

continuous permutation-invariant function  $f: K^n \to \mathbb{R}$ , there exists a continuous  $F: \mathbb{R}^m \to \mathbb{R}$  such that

$$
f (\boldsymbol {x}) = F \left(\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} \sigma \left(\boldsymbol {A} \boldsymbol {x} _ {j} + \boldsymbol {b}\right)\right), \quad \forall \boldsymbol {x} \in K. \tag {7}
$$

We note that due to the universality of MLPs, any continuous permutation-invariant function on  $K^n$  can be approximated by expressions of the form (7) with  $F$  replaced by an MLP. Similar results were obtained in [36], but only for the case  $d = 1$ , and where the injective multiset function was a polynomial rather than an MLP.

It is worth noting that Proposition 4.1 implies, that a moment function constructed from  $PwL$  MLP cannot separate all orbits in  $K^n$ , if  $K$  has non-empty interior. Therefore, a decomposition such as (7) with a  $PwL \sigma$  cannot be obtained for functions  $f: K^n \to \mathbb{R}^N$  that do separate orbits. However, with  $PwL \sigma$ , one may obtain arbitrarily accurate approximations of any given  $f$ , by taking the embedding dimension  $m$  to infinity. In contrast, for analytic activation functions we are able to specify a finite  $m = 2nd + 1$  that guarantees an exact equality.

Since our injectivity results on multiset extensions extend to measures, it is natural to seek an extension of the above approximation result to functions defined on measures. In the following we denote the space of probability measures supported on  $\leq n$  points in the alphabet  $K$  by  $\mathcal{P}_{\leq n}(K)$ , and endow it with the 2-Wasserstein metric.

Corollary 6.2. Let  $n, d \in \mathbb{N}$  and set  $m = 2n(d + 1) + 1$ . Let  $\sigma : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$  be a non-polynomial analytic function. Let  $K \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$  be a compact set. Then there exist  $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d}$ ,  $b \in \mathbb{R}^d$  such that for any continuous (in the 2-Wasserstein sense) function on measures  $f : \mathcal{P}_{\leq n}(K) \to \mathbb{R}$ , there exists a continuous  $F : \mathbb{R}^m \to \mathbb{R}$  such that

$$
f (\mu) = F \left(\int_ {\boldsymbol {x} \in K} \sigma (\boldsymbol {A} \boldsymbol {x} + \boldsymbol {b}) d \mu (\boldsymbol {x})\right), \quad \forall \mu \in \mathcal {P} _ {\leq n} (K).
$$

# 6.2 Graph separation

We now consider the implications of Theorem 3.3 for graph separation, using the terminology from [37]. Let  $\mathcal{G}_{\leq n}(\Sigma)$  denote the collection of all graphs  $G = (V,E,\pmb{h}^{(0)})$  with at most  $n$  vertices, endowed with vertex features  $\pmb{h}_v^{(0)}$  that assume values in a countable alphabet  $\Sigma \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ . We consider message passing neural networks that recursively, for  $t = 1,\dots ,T$ , create a new node feature  $\pmb{h}_v^{(t)}$  from the previous feature  $\pmb{h}_v^{(t - 1)}$  via

$$
\boldsymbol {h} _ {v} ^ {(t)} = \sum_ {u \in \mathcal {N} (v)} \sigma \left(\boldsymbol {A} ^ {(t)} \left(\eta^ {(t)} \boldsymbol {h} _ {v} ^ {(t - 1)} + \boldsymbol {h} _ {u} ^ {(t - 1)}\right) + \boldsymbol {b} ^ {(t)}\right). \tag {8}
$$

After the  $T$  iterations are concluded, a global feature is computed via a readout function:

$$
\boldsymbol {h} _ {G} = \sum_ {v \in V} \sigma \left(\boldsymbol {A} ^ {(T + 1)} \boldsymbol {h} _ {v} ^ {(T)} + \boldsymbol {b} ^ {(T + 1)}\right). \tag {9}
$$

We choose all features  $h_G$  and  $h_v^{(t)}$  for  $1 \leq t \leq T$ , to have the same dimension  $m$ . Based on the fact that MPNNs are equivalent to 1-WL when the multiset functions are injective [37], and on our injectivity results for countable alphabets, we prove that:

Theorem 6.3. Let  $n, d, T \in \mathbb{N}$  and let  $\Sigma \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$  be countable. Let  $m \geq 1$  be any integer. Let  $\sigma: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$  be a non-polynomial analytic function. Then for Lebesgue almost every choice of  $A^{(t)}, b^{(t)}$  and  $\eta^{(t)}$ , the MPNN defined by (8) and (9) assigns distinct global features to a pair of distinct graphs from  $G_{\leq n}(\Sigma)$ , if and only if the pair can be separated by  $T$  iterations of 1-WL.

Graph separation with continuous features Up to now we have discussed graphs with node features coming from a countable universe. Since our Theorem 3.3 applies for multisets with continuous alphabets, it can be applied for separation of graphs with continuous node features as well. In particular, the paper [14] explains how the random semialgebraic multiset function from [8] can be used to obtain equivalence with WL tests for graphs with continuous features. Their focus is on

showing that the embedding dimension in their construction depends linearly on the dimension of the graph space, rather than grows exponentially with the number of message-passing iterations  $T$ . Similar results can now be obtained by using our random analytic multiset functions. We leave a full description of these aspects to future work.

# 7 Experiments

Empirical injectivity and Bi-Lipschitzness We empirically investigated the injectivity and Bi-Lipschitzness of moments of shallow networks as in (3), by randomly generating a large number of pairs of multisets of  $n$  vectors in  $\mathbb{R}^d$ , and computing the optimal constants  $c, C$  for which (6) holds on the pairs we generated. The ratio  $c / C$  for varying activations and embedding dimension  $m$  is shown in Figure 2. Here  $n = 1000$  and  $d = 3$ . Similar qualitative results were obtained for other values of  $d$  and  $n$ ; see Figure 3 in the Appendix.

We observe several interesting phenomena: First, at low embedding-dimensions, the ratio  $c / C$  for PwL networks is exactly zero, indicating that they are not injective even on the final sample set. In contrast, for analytic activations  $c / C$  is always positive. Indeed, we expect analytic activations to be injective on a finite sample even with embedding dimension  $m = 1$ , since a finite sample set has an intrinsic dimension of zero. Next, we observe that  $c / C$  naturally improves as  $m$  increases. Finally, we note that even for high  $m$ ,  $c / C$  is rather small.

This corroborates Theorem 5.1, which states that differentiable moment functions are not bi-Lipschitz even when they are injective. Indeed, if it were possible to consider all pairs of multisets when computing  $c / C$ , we would get zero for all activations and all embedding dimensions. For additional details on this experiment, see Appendix E.1.

Graph Separation To corroborate Theorem 6.3 we conducted the following experiment: we considered 600 graphs from the TUDataset [25]. On each graph we ran three iterations of the WL test, and three iterations of MPNNs with the Graph Convolutional Layers from [16] with different activations and hidden dimensions. Our goal was to check in how many graphs the MPNNs returned a vertex coloring that differs

from the coloring provided by the WL test. The results are shown in Figure 1(a). As seen in the table, with the three analytic activations tested, the vertex coloring of MPNN was always equivalent to 1-WL, even with a hidden dimension of 1. On the other hand, for the three PwL activations, there were inconsistencies in about  $1\%$  of the graphs, even with a hidden dimension of 50.

We note that while the distance between features of non-equivalent nodes computed by the MPNNs was typically around 0.1, the least-separated features had a distance of  $\sim 10^{-7}$ . In future work it could be interesting to investigate whether MPNNs can be trained to yield larger distances between the features of all non-equivalent nodes. # 8 Conclusion

Our main contribution in this paper is showing that the multiset functions induced by the moments of neural networks with analytic activations are injective. We also show how this result can be harnessed to prove approximation results for multiset functions, as well as separation results for graph neural networks. A key advantage of our approach is that it enables constructing these proofs using real models used in practice, rather than idealized versions of them, as done in previous works.

It may seem tempting, due to our theoretical results, to conclude that analytic activations should perform better than PwL activations when applied to multisets. We stress that we make no such claim. Indeed, while the separation results in Figure 1(a) corroborate our theory, PwL networks fail

to separate only  $1\%$  of the graphs in our experiment. Similarly, at high embedding dimensions, the empirical bi-Lipschitzness in Figure 2 does not seem to strongly depend on the regularity of the activation function. Our claim is thus much more modest: we claim that multiset networks with analytic activations are easier to theoretically analyze, and we hope that pursuing this theoretical analysis will lead to fruitful theoretical and practical insights, which may ultimately benefit multiset networks using either type of activation.

Finally, we note that the finite witness theorem, which is presented here as a tool for proving moment injectivity, may prove as a valuable general tool for reducing an infinite number of equality constraints to a finite number, and we believe it will find additional applications beyond the scope of this paper.
