# ABSTRACT

Cultural sentiments of a society characterize social behaviors, but modeling sentiments to manifest every potential interaction remains an immense challenge. Affect Control Theory (ACT) offers a solution to this problem. ACT is a generative theory of culture and behavior based on a three-dimensional sentiment lexicon. Traditionally, the sentiments are quantified using survey data which is fed into a regression model to explain social behavior. The lexicons used in the survey are limited due to prohibitive cost. This paper uses a fine-tuned Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) model for developing a replacement for these surveys. This model achieves state-of-the-art accuracy in estimating affective meanings, expanding the affective lexicon, and allowing more behaviors to be explained.

# 1 INTRODUCTION

Consider talking to your mentor for some advice about how to behave with your colleague. Your mentor probably starts asking questions about the culture in the workspace and may continue asking about the identity of the person. These questions could be about institutional constraints such as being the manager, or they could be personality sentiment such as being nice or active. Knowing all this information, your mentor may have some initial recommendations, but you adapt your behavior after observing the reactions from the colleague. This is a descriptive scenario for a daily interaction. Affect Control Theory (ACT) quantifies the variables in this scenario and produces equations for simulating human behavior in different scenarios.

ACT was introduced in the 1970s (Heise, 1977) and has been validated in more than 100 social science research (Robinson & Smith-Lovin, 2018). Recently ACT has been used in applications such as Human-Computer Interactions (Robillard & Hoey, 2018). ACT uses a three-dimensional affective meaning space as a quantized form of meaning (Heise, 1977).

ACT uses Evaluation [good vs. bad], Potency [powerful vs. powerless], and Activity [active vs. passive] (EPA) space introduced by Osgood et al. (1957) as a semantic differential form of affective meaning. These affective lexicons represent the word of interest within cultural and social boundaries (Robinson & Smith-Lovin, 1992). Fontaine et al. (2007) reduced dimensionality from 144 features representing the main components of emotions, and the first three principal components were EPA space.

A survey study can measure these values within a cultural group. To compensate for unreliability in the survey, each rating should be replicated at least 25 times. Other than baseline sentiments, ACT needs a sentiment rating after observing social interactions to model the behavior. Finding these affective meanings is a challenging task, and it limits the applicability of ACT in many applications. As an alternative approach, researchers used supervised (Mostafavi & Porter, 2021; Li et al., 2017) and semi-supervised (Alhothali & Hoey, 2017) methods on shallow word-embeddings to find affective lexicons. However, their result specifically in activity and potency dimensions needs improvement. In this paper, we discuss the main limitations for the applicability of shallow word-embeddings. Then we discuss how to generate a data-set to pass to a deep neural network. We use Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) embedding and fine-tune it for

# 2 AFFECT CONTROL THEORY

Social interactions or events at least include an actor that behaves an object. These Actor-Behavior-Object (ABO) are the first pieces of modeling interactions (Heise, 2010). The actor/object in an event may have a modifier specifying their characteristics. The baseline EPA representation of words is known as sentiments. Sentiments for some sample words are plotted in Figure 1. In this plot, we can observe that "suicidal" and "nervous" both have bad, powerless, and passive meanings but "suicidal" is more negative in all three dimensions. On the other hand, "happy" is a pleasant, powerful, and active word. Note the range of EPA ratings is from 4.3 to 4.3.

Social events have impressions on ABO characters. Experimental tests explain what contributing facts derive the impressions of events (Robinson & Smith-Lovin, 1999). Consider an example of observing "a bossy employer argues with an employee". This observation makes people evaluate both the actor and the object of the interaction are less pleasant than initial thoughts. On the other hand, they may feel the employer is more powerful, and the employee is more powerless than their baseline sentiments after this event.

Deflection is the euclidean distance between the baseline sentiments of ABO characters and their impressions following an event. If the impression of an ABO event is close to initial sentiments, deflection is small, and it gets bigger when the impression of the event leaves the initial sentiments. Minimizing deflection is the driving force in human activity. Highly deflecting events create social and physiological distress (Goldstein, 1989). For example, if a grandmother fights with her grandchild, they feel distressed and prefer to do something. We expect one side to take an action, like apologize, to bring the impression of their identities back to where society views them. This event is very different from two soldiers fighting in a battle. The soldiers are supposed to fight with enemies in battle, so they do not feel social pressure.

Heise (2013) developed a software called INTERACT. It simulates these types of interaction. For a simple simulation, we simulated the following scenario using INTERACT,

1. Employee greets bossy employer.  
2. Bossy employer asks employee.  
3. Employee replies to bossy employer.  
4. Bossy employer argues with employee.  
5. Employee listens to / disobeys bossy employer.

We can explain impression changes in a descriptive form such as the following two ones,

- Actors seem nice when they behave in a positive way toward others. This describes morality fact in ACT literature. Observing the evaluation dimension for the actors after he greets the object, we can find this behavior resulted in an impression of being nicer (getting larger evaluation) comparing to the state in the last step.  
- Active behaviors make the actors seem more active. Observing the boss's activity, he is considered more active after he argues with [active behavior] the employee.

As we have seen in the descriptive analyses, events change the impression of ABO in EPA space. They move them toward or away from their sentiments. ACT uses regression models, known as impression change equations, to estimate these changes. These models resulted from surveys that asked people to evaluate ABO in EPA space after events (Heise, 2013). Let  $X = [A_e, A_p, A_a, B_e, B_p, B_a, O_e, O_p, O_a]^T$ ,  $X^2 = [A_eB_e, A_eO_e, A_eB_a, \ldots, A_aO_a]^T$ , and  $X^3 = [A_eB_eO_e, A_eB_eO_p, A_eB_eO_a, \ldots, A_aB_aO_a]^T$  then we can write basic impression change equations in the following format,

$$
X ^ {\prime} = \alpha X + \beta X ^ {2} + \gamma X ^ {3} \tag {1}
$$

where  $\alpha, \beta$ , and  $\gamma$  are constant matrices,  $\{A, B, O\}$ , represent the ABO characters, and  $\{e, p, a\}$  represent EPA components before the event, and  $X'$  represent the resulting impression after the event. Equation (1) models the basic interaction scenario. When we have modified or the interaction happens in a specific social institution, we can use similar approaches to model it. When we have modified identities, a bossy employer for example, amalgamation equations similar to (2) gives the sentiments for an identity with a modifier (Averett & Heise, 1987).

$$
C _ {e} = - 1 7 + 0. 6 2 M _ {e} - 0. 1 4 M _ {p} - 0. 1 8 M _ {a} + 0. 5 I _ {e} \tag {2}
$$

where,  $C_e$ ,  $M_e$ ,  $M_p$ ,  $M_a$ ,  $I_e$  represent evaluation of the modifier-identity, evaluation of the modifier, potency of the modifier, activity of the modifier, and evaluation of the identity respectively. Equation (2) is a weighted average of the evaluation for the modifier and the identity. Similarly, potency and activity of the modifier affect the evaluation of the modifier-identity (Averett & Heise, 1987).

Li et al. However, ACT is developed only based on affective meanings of the words. So word-embedding graph propagation considers similarities that reduce accuracy for finding affective meaning.

However, all these approaches are limited by using one representation for different meanings of a word.

The words in affective dictionaries are categorized as identity, modifier, and behavior. The words are mapped to different points in EPA space when they are evaluated in each category. On the other hand, if a word has two or more meanings or different parts of speech, it still has only one representation in the embedding space. So, for example, "mother", "coach", and "fool" appear in both categories of behavior and identities, but they have only one representation in embedding space. In Figure 3 we can observe how some words are mapped very differently based on their category. For example, "baby" as an identity is a pleasant and active character but is unpleasant and passive as a behavior.

# 4 BIDIRECTIONAL ENCODER REPRESENTATIONS FROM TRANSFORMERS

In 2018, Google open-sourced BERT as the state-of-the-art pre-training model for Natural Language Processing (NLP) (Devlin et al., 2018). This model is designed to be pre-trained on unlabeled data and uses both left and right context in all layers. BERT gives state of the art in many challenging NLP tasks (McCormick & Ryan, 2019). BERT has been pre-trained on Wikipedia and book corpora that includes more than 10000 books. We use the base version of BERT in this study.

The main advantage of word-embeddings derived from BERT over shallow word-embeddings is being context-based embeddings. So we can get different representations when we have a word as a behavior or an identity. But we have to feet the dataset so that it can understand in which category we are working.

To use BERT as a contextual word-embedding we should have the following processing (McCormick & Ryan, 2019),

1. BERT requires two special tokens in a sentence. First, [CLS] represents the start of a sentence. Special token [SEP] comes at the end of the sentence when we are only passing one sentence.  
2. BERT tokenizer tokenizes the sentence. This tokenizer assigns multiple tokens to a word if the word is not in its vocabulary. In that case, tokens are WordPieces, prefix, and suffix are sub-words.  
3. Token IDs are assigned from the tokenizer.  
4. BERT always expects two sentences. Since we are passing only one sentence, the sentence ID is set to 1 for all the tokens in the sentence.

# 5 METHOD

As we discussed earlier, BERT can be a contextual embedding space. There are a couple of different approaches to find word and sentence embedding from the BERT model. We discovered that concatenating the last two layers gives a sentence embedding that results in our task's state of the art.

We should process the data to use the BERT for extending affective dictionaries. The following steps show how we use BERT model to extend affective dictionaries,

1. One affective dictionary that includes both identities and behaviors is selected.  $85\%$  of the data is used for the training and  $15\%$  for the test.  
2. Two identities and one behavior are randomly selected from the train set. They are combined to make ABO form.  
3. ABO sentences are pre-processed and passed to the pre-trained BERT model.  
4. The last two layers of the BERT model are concatenated to make a sentence embedding.  
5. A four-layer neural network is used to find the 9-dimensional vector of EPA values for ABO characters. The first three layers have 9 dense and 9 Tanh parts. The last layer is a dense layer that makes the 9-dimensional vector.  
6. A regression model is used to fine-tune the neural network.

# 7 CONCLUDING REMARKS

A big limitation in the applicability of ACT to different contexts such as NLP tasks was the limitation of affective vocabulary collected from surveys.
