# Abstract

Many tasks in explainable machine learning, such as data valuation and feature attribution, perform expensive computation for each data point and can be intractable for large datasets. These methods require efficient approximations, and learning a network that directly predicts the desired output, which is commonly known as amortization, is a promising solution. However, training such models with exact labels is often intractable; we therefore explore training with noisy labels and find that this is inexpensive and surprisingly effective. Through theoretical analysis of the label noise and experiments with various models and datasets, we show that this approach significantly accelerates several feature attribution and data valuation methods, often yielding an order of magnitude speedup over existing approaches.

# 1. Introduction

Many tasks in explainable machine learning (XML) perform some form of costly computation for every data point in a dataset. For example, common tasks include analyzing individual data points to understand their impact on a model's accuracy (Ghorbani & Zou, 2019), or quantifying each input feature's influence on the model's predictions (Lundberg & Lee, 2017). Many of these techniques are prohibitively expensive: in particular, those with game-theoretic formulations have exponential complexity in the number of features or data points, making their exact calculation intractable.

Accelerating these methods is therefore a topic of great practical importance. This has been addressed primarily with Monte Carlo approximations (Strumbelj & Kononenko, 2010; Covert & Lee, 2021; Mitchell et al., 2021), which are faster than brute-force calculations but can be slow to converge and impractical for large datasets. Alternatively, a promising idea is to amortize the computation, or to approxi-

There are several reasons why amortization is appealing, particularly with neural networks: 1) similar data points often have similar outputs, 2) pretrained networks extract relevant features and can be efficiently fine-tuned, 3) deep learning enables stochastic optimization and scales to large datasets, and 4) amortization offers cheap inference on new examples. Furthermore, amortization can also potentially be efficient: if the combined training and inference time is low, then amortization can be faster than computing the object of interest (e.g., data valuation scores) for the entire dataset.

However, it is not obvious how to train such amortized models, because standard supervised learning requires a dataset of ground truth labels that can be intractable to generate. Our goal in this work is therefore to explore how we can efficiently train amortized models when exact labels are costly. Our main insight is that amortization is surprisingly effective with noisy labels: we train with inexpensive estimates of the ground truth labels, and we find that this is theoretically justified when the estimates are unbiased. We refer to this approach as stochastic amortization (Figure 1), and we find that it is applicable to a variety of XML tasks.

In particular, we show that it is effective for feature attribution with Shapley values (Lundberg & Lee, 2017), Banzhaf values (Chen & Jordan, 2020) and LIME (Ribeiro et al., 2016); for several formulations of data valuation (Ghorbani & Zou, 2019; Ghorbani et al., 2020; Kwon & Zou, 2021; Wang & Jia, 2022); and that is it applicable to data attribution with datamodels (Ilyas et al., 2022). Our experiments demonstrate significant speedups for several of these tasks, whether we consider usage with new inference examples or the training data: that is, we find that amortizing across an entire dataset is often more efficient than existing perexample approximations, particularly for large datasets.

# 2. Background

This section introduces the idea of amortization in ML, which we discuss in a general setting before considering any specific XML tasks.

# 2.1. Amortization

Consider a scenario where we repeat similar computation for a large number of data points. We represent this general setup with a context variable  $b \in \mathcal{B}$  and a per-context output denoted by  $a(b) \in \mathcal{A}$ . For example, these can be an image and its data valuation score, or an image and its feature attributions. Amortization can be used with arbitrary domains, but we assume Euclidean spaces, or  $\mathcal{A} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$  and  $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ , because many XML tasks involve real-valued outputs.

The computation performed for each context  $b \in \mathcal{B}$  can be arbitrary as well. In some cases  $a(b)$  is an intractable expectation, in which case we typically use a Monte Carlo estimator (Strumbelj & Kononenko, 2010; Ghorbani & Zou, 2019). In other situations it involves solving an optimization problem (Shu, 2017; Amos, 2022), in which case we can define  $a(b)$  via a parametric objective  $h: \mathcal{A} \times \mathcal{B} \mapsto \mathbb{R}$ :

$$
a (b) \equiv \underset {a ^ {\prime} \in \mathcal {A}} {\arg \min } h \left(a ^ {\prime}; b\right). \tag {1}
$$

We do not require a specific formulation for  $a(b)$  in this work, but we will see that both the expectation and optimization setups provide useful perspectives on our main proposal of training with noisy labels (Section 3).

In situations with repeated computation, our two options are generally to perform the computation separately for each  $b \in \mathcal{B}$ , or to amortize the computation with a learned model. We typically implement this with a deep neural network  $a(b; \theta)$ , and our goal is to train it such that  $a(b; \theta) \approx a(b)$ . In training these models, the main challenge occurs when the per-instance computation is costly: specifically, it is not obvious how to train  $a(b; \theta)$  without a dataset of ground truth solutions  $a(b)$ , which is often too slow to generate. We address this challenge in Section 3, where we prove that amortization tolerates training with noisy labels.

# 2.2. Related work

The general view of amortized computation captures many tasks arising in physics, engineering, control and ML (Amos, 2022). For example, amortization is prominent in variational inference (Kingma & Welling, 2013; Rezende et al., 2014), meta learning (Ha et al., 2016; Finn et al., 2017; Rajeswaran et al., 2019) and reinforcement learning (Heess et al., 2015; Levine & Koltun, 2013; Haarnoja et al., 2018). A comprehensive review is provided by Amos (2022), although this does not discuss applications of amortization in XML.

In the XML context, many recent works have explored amor-

tization to accelerate costly per-datapoint calculations (Chen et al., 2018; Yoon et al., 2018; Jethani et al., 2021a;b; Covert et al., 2022; 2023). Some of these works are reviewed by Chuang et al. (2023a), and we offer a detailed overview in Appendix A. One line of work predicts Shapley value feature attributions based on a weighted least squares loss (Jethani et al., 2021b; Covert et al., 2022); these methods are somewhat complex due to the custom training routine and projection step for the predictions, whereas our simpler approach can use any unbiased estimator and resembles a standard regression task. For works related to our main proposal, two suggest modeling feature attributions with supervised learning (Schwarzenberg et al., 2021; Chuang et al., 2023b); however, these recommend training with high-quality labels, which can be orders of magnitude slower than training with noisy labels. Concurrently, Zhang et al. (2023) proposed training with a custom estimator for Shapley value feature attributions; our work is distinct in deriving stochastic amortization algorithms for a range of settings, including the use of any unbiased estimator (Section 3), and we demonstrate speedups for various XML tasks including data valuation (Section 4).

Amortization is less explored in the data influence literature. Two works consider predicting data valuation scores (Ghorbani et al., 2020; 2022), but these also use high-quality labels that limit the applicability to large datasets; our approach instead uses noisy labels and can more easily scale to large datasets. Finally, while there are works that accelerate data attribution with datamodels (Park et al., 2023), we are not aware of any that explore the use of amortization.

# 3. Stochastic Amortization

We now discuss how to efficiently train amortized models with noisy labels. Following Section 2, we present this as a general approach before focusing on specific XML tasks.

# 3.1. Amortization with noisy labels

One natural idea is to treat amortization like a supervised learning problem. We can parameterize a model  $a(b;\theta)$ , adopt a distribution  $p(b)$  over the context variable, and then train our model with the following objective:

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {\text {r e g}} (\theta) = \mathbb {E} \left[ \| a (b; \theta) - a (b) \| ^ {2} \right]. \tag {2}
$$

This approach is called regression-based amortization (Amos, 2022) because it reduces the problem to a simple regression task. The challenge is that this approach is impossible to use when we cannot generate a large dataset of exact labels  $a(b)$ , which is common in XML (Section 4).

A relaxation of this idea is to train the model with inexact labels (see Figure 1). We assume that these are generated by a noisy oracle  $\tilde{a}(b)$ , which is characterized by a distribution

of outputs for each context  $b \in \mathcal{B}$ . For example, the noisy oracle could be a statistical estimator of a data valuation score (Ghorbani & Zou, 2019). With this, we can train the model using a modified version of Eq. (2), where we measure the loss in expectation over both  $p(b)$  and  $\tilde{a}(b)$ :

$$
\tilde {\mathcal {L}} _ {\mathrm {r e g}} (\theta) = \mathbb {E} \left[ \| a (b; \theta) - \tilde {a} (b) \| ^ {2} \right]. \tag {3}
$$

It is not immediately obvious when this approach is worthwhile: if the noisy oracle is too inaccurate, we will naturally learn the wrong function, so it is important to choose the oracle carefully. We find that there are two properties of the oracle that matter, and these relate to its systematic error and level of noise, or more intuitively its bias and variance. We denote these quantities as follows for a specific value  $b$ ,

$$
\mathrm {B} (\tilde {a} \mid b) = \| a (b) - \mathbb {E} [ \tilde {a} (b) \mid b ] \| ^ {2}
$$

$$
\mathrm {N} (\tilde {a} \mid b) = \mathbb {E} \left[ \| \tilde {a} (b) - \mathbb {E} [ \tilde {a} (b) \mid b ] \| ^ {2} \mid b \right],
$$

and based on these we can also define the global measures  $\mathrm{B}(\tilde{a})\equiv \mathbb{E}_p[\mathrm{B}(\tilde{a}\mid b)]$  and  $\mathrm{N}(\tilde{a})\equiv \mathbb{E}_p[\mathrm{N}(\tilde{a}\mid b)]$  for the distribution  $p(b)$ .<sup>1</sup> These terms are useful because they reveal a relationship between the two amortization objectives:

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm {r e g}} (\theta) \leq \left(\sqrt {\tilde {\mathcal {L}} _ {\mathrm {r e g}} (\theta) - \mathrm {N} (\tilde {a})} + \sqrt {\mathrm {B} (\tilde {a})}\right) ^ {2}. \tag {4}
$$

This relationship means that by reducing  $\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{reg}}(\theta)$  towards its minimum value  $\mathrm{N}(\tilde{a})$ , we minimize an upper bound on the original objective  $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{reg}}(\theta)$ . However, the bias  $\mathrm{B}(\tilde{a})$  represents a source of irreducible error in Eq. (4), and we recover an incorrect function asymptotically when the bias is nonzero. The variance plays a role as well, not in determining the function we learn but in making the model's optimization unstable or require more noisy labels. To illustrate the role of variance, we consider the simplest case of a linear model  $a(b;\theta)$  trained with SGD, which shows that high label noise slows convergence (see proof in Appendix D).

Theorem 1. Consider a noisy oracle  $\tilde{a}(b)$  that satisfies  $\mathbb{E}[\tilde{a}(b) \mid b] = \tilde{\theta}b$  with parameters  $\tilde{\theta} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d}$  such that  $\|\tilde{\theta}\|_F \leq D$ . Given a distribution  $p(b)$ , define the norm-weighted distribution  $q(b) \propto p(b) \cdot \|b\|^2$  and the terms  $\Sigma_p \equiv \mathbb{E}_p[bb^\top]$  and  $\Sigma_q \equiv \mathbb{E}_q[bb^\top]$ . If we train a linear model  $a(\theta; b) = \theta b$  with the noisy objective  $\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{reg}}(\theta)$  using SGD with step size  $\eta_t = \frac{2}{\alpha(t+1)}$ , then the averaged iterate  $\bar{\theta}_T = \sum_{t=1}^T \frac{2t}{T(T+1)} \theta_t$  at step  $T$  satisfies

$$
\mathbb {E} \left[ \tilde {\mathcal {L}} _ {\text {r e g}} \left(\bar {\theta} _ {T}\right) \right] - \mathrm {N} (\tilde {a}) \leq \frac {4 \operatorname {T r} \left(\Sigma_ {p}\right) \left(\mathrm {N} _ {q} (\tilde {a}) + 4 \lambda_ {\max } \left(\Sigma_ {q}\right) D ^ {2}\right)}{\lambda_ {\min } \left(\Sigma_ {p}\right) (T + 1)},
$$

where  $\mathrm{N}_q(\tilde{a})\equiv \mathbb{E}_q[\mathrm{N}(\tilde{a}\mid b)]$  is the noisy oracle's norm-weighted variance, and  $\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\cdot),\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\cdot)$  are the maximum and minimum eigenvalues.

The bound in Theorem 1 illustrates the role of the noisy oracle's variance: noise slows the rate of convergence by its presence in the numerator, and interestingly, it appears in the form of a weighted version  $\mathrm{N}_q(\tilde{a})$  that puts more weight on contexts with large norm  $\|b\|$ ; this is a consequence of assuming a linear model, but we expect the general conclusion of label variance slowing convergence to hold even with neural networks. When viewed together with Eq. (4), Theorem 1 also emphasizes the role of bias, because the original loss  $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{reg}}(\theta)$  does not tend to zero as  $T \to \infty$  when we have  $\mathrm{B}(\tilde{a}) > 0$ . As a corollary, we can see that the rate in Theorem 1 applies directly to  $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{reg}}(\theta)$  when the noisy oracle is unbiased (see Appendix D).

Overall, our analysis shows that amortization with noisy labels is possible, although perhaps more difficult to optimize than training with exact labels. We later show that unbiased estimates are available for many XML tasks (Section 4), and we find that this form of amortization is often effective with the noise levels observed in practice (Section 5). As a shorthand, we refer to training with the noisy objective  $\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{reg}}(\theta)$  in Eq. (3) as stochastic amortization.

# 3.2. Objective-based amortization

For completeness, we briefly discuss another perspective on stochastic amortization. First, recall that many tasks with repeated computation have an optimization view where  $a(b) \equiv \arg \min_{a'} h(a'; b)$  (see Section 2.1). In this case, an alternative is to train  $a(b; \theta)$  directly with the  $h$  objective:

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm {o b j}} (\theta) = \mathbb {E} [ h (a (b; \theta); b) ]. \tag {5}
$$

This idea is known as objective-based amortization (Amos, 2022), and it provides an alternative training approach when exact labels are costly. Stochastic amortization can be viewed as a form of objective-based amortization but with a new objective: given a noisy oracle  $\tilde{a}(b)$ , it is equivalent to  $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{obj}}(\theta)$  with the objective  $h(a'; b) = \mathbb{E}\left[\|a' - \tilde{a}(b)\|^2\right]$ , and if the noisy oracle is unbiased then this objective shares the same optimizer as  $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{reg}}(\theta)$ . In Appendix C, we discuss how this resolves potential issues where the task does not have a natural objective  $h(a'; b)$  or where this objective is not clearly connected to the estimation error  $\|a(b; \theta) - a(b)\|$ , which is often a more meaningful accuracy measure in XML (Jethani et al., 2021b; Wang & Jia, 2022; Chen et al., 2022).

# 4. Applications to Explainable ML

We now consider XML tasks that can be accelerated with stochastic amortization. Rather than using generic variables  $b \in \mathcal{B}$  and  $a(b) \in \mathcal{A}$ , this section uses an input variable  $x \in \mathcal{X}$ , a response variable  $y \in \mathcal{Y}$ , and a model  $f$  or a measure of its performance. As we describe below, each application of stochastic amortization is instantiated by a noisy oracle that generates labels for the given task.

# 4.1. Shapley value feature attribution

One of the most common tasks in XML is feature attribution, which aims to quantify each feature's influence on an individual prediction. The Shapley value has gained popularity because of its origins in game theory (Shapley, 1953; Lundberg & Lee, 2017), and like many feature attribution methods it is based on querying the model while removing different feature sets (Covert et al., 2021). Given a model  $f$  and input  $x$ , we assume that the input consists of  $d$  separate features  $x = (x_{1},\ldots ,x_{d})$  and that we can calculate the prediction  $f(x_{S})\in \mathbb{R}$  for any feature set  $S\subseteq [d]$ . With this setup, the Shapley values  $\phi_i(x)\in \mathbb{R}$  for each feature  $i\in [d]$  are defined as follows:

$$
\phi_{i}(x) = \frac{1}{d}\sum_{S\subseteq [d]\setminus \{i\}}\binom {d - 1}{|S|}^{-1}\big(f(x_{S\cup \{i\}}) - f(x_{S})\big).
$$

These scores satisfy several desirable properties (Shapley, 1953), but they are impractical to calculate due to the exponential summation. Our goal is therefore to learn an amortized attribution model  $\phi(x; \theta) \in \mathbb{R}^d$  that directly predicts feature attribution scores given the input  $x \in \mathcal{X}$ .

Many recent works have studied efficient Shapley value estimation (Chen et al., 2022), and we first consider noisy oracles derived from the equation above, which defines the attribution as the feature's expected marginal contribution. There are several unbiased statistical estimators that rely on sampling feature subsets or permutations (Castro et al., 2009; Štrumbelj & Kononenko, 2010; Okhrati & Lipani, 2021; Mitchell et al., 2021; Kolpaczki et al., 2023), and following Section 3 we can use any of these for stochastic amortization. Our experiments use the classic permutation sampling estimator (Štrumbelj & Kononenko, 2010; Mitchell et al., 2021), which approximates the values  $\phi_i(x)$  as an expectation across feature orderings. We defer the precise definition of this unbiased noisy oracle to Appendix E, along with the other estimators used in our experiments.

Next, we can also consider noisy oracles derived from an optimization perspective on the Shapley value. A famous result from Charnes et al. (1988) shows that the Shapley values are the solution to the following problem (with abuse of notation we discard the solution's intercept term),

$$
\phi (x) = \underset {a \in \mathbb {R} ^ {d + 1}} {\arg \min } \sum_ {S \subseteq [ d ]} \mu (S) \left(f (x _ {S}) - a _ {0} - \sum_ {i \in S} a _ {i}\right) ^ {2},
$$

where we use a least squares weighting kernel defined as  $\mu^{-1}(S) = \binom{d}{|S|} |S|(d - |S|)$ . Several works have proposed approximating Shapley values by solving this problem with sampled subsets, either using projected gradient descent

(Simon & Vincent, 2020) or analytic solutions (Lundberg & Lee, 2017; Covert & Lee, 2021). Among these, we use KernelSHAP (Lundberg & Lee, 2017) and SGD-Shapley (Simon & Vincent, 2020) as noisy oracles in our experiments. The first is an M-estimator whose bias shrinks as the number of sampled subsets grows (Van der Vaart, 2000), so we expect it to lead to successful amortization; the latter has been shown to have non-negligible bias (Chen et al., 2022), so our theory in Section 3 suggests that it should lead to learning an incorrect function when used for amortization.

# 4.2. Alternative feature attributions

Next, we consider two alternative feature attribution methods: Banzhaf values (Banzhaf, 1964; Chen & Jordan, 2020), and LIME (Ribeiro et al., 2016). These are closely related to Shapley values and are similarly intractable (Dubey & Shapley, 1979; Hammer & Holzman, 1992), but we find that they offer statistical estimators that can be used for stochastic amortization.

First, Banzhaf values assign the following scores to each feature for a prediction  $f(x)$  (Banzhaf, 1964):

$$
\phi_ {i} (x) = \frac {1}{2 ^ {d - 1}} \sum_ {S \subseteq [ d ] \backslash \{i \}} \left(f (x _ {S \cup \{i \}}) - f (x _ {S})\right).
$$

These differ from Shapley values only in their choice of weighting function, and they admit a range of similar statistical estimators. One option is the MSR estimator from Wang & Jia (2022), which is unbiased and re-uses all model evaluations for each feature attribution estimate. We adopt this as a noisy oracle in our experiments (see a precise definition in Appendix E), but several other options are available.

Second, LIME defines its attribution scores  $\phi_i(x)$  as the solution to the following optimization problem, given a weighting kernel  $\pi(S)$  and penalty term  $\Omega$  (Ribeiro et al., 2016):<sup>3</sup>

$$
\underset {a \in \mathbb {R} ^ {d + 1}} {\arg \min } \sum_ {S \subseteq [ d ]} \pi (S) \left(f \left(x _ {S}\right) - a _ {0} - \sum_ {i \in S} a _ {i}\right) ^ {2} + \Omega (a).
$$

As our noisy oracle for LIME, we use the popular approach of solving the above problem for subsets sampled according to  $\pi(S)$ . Similar to KernelSHAP (Lundberg & Lee, 2017), this is an M-estimator whose bias shrinks to zero as the sample size grows (Van der Vaart, 2000), so we expect it to lead to successful amortization.

Aside from these methods, several other costly feature interpretation methods rely on unbiased statistical estimators, and these can be amortized in a similar fashion (Sundararajan et al., 2020; Kwon & Zou, 2022; Fumagalli et al., 2023).

# 4.3. Data valuation

Next, data valuation aims to quantify how much each training example affects a model's accuracy. We consider labeled examples  $z = (x,y)$  and a training dataset  $\mathcal{D} = \{z_i\}_{i=1}^n$ , and we analyze each data point's value by fitting models to subsampled datasets  $\mathcal{D}_T \subseteq \mathcal{D}$  with  $T \subseteq [n]$  and observing a measure of the model's performance  $v(\mathcal{D}_T) \in \mathbb{R}$  (e.g., its 0-1 accuracy). This general approach was introduced by Ghorbani & Zou (2019), who defined the Data Shapley scores  $\psi(z_i) \in \mathbb{R}$  as follows:

$$
\psi (z_{i}) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{T\subseteq [n]\setminus \{i\}}\binom {n - 1}{|T|}^{-1}\left(v(\mathcal{D}_{T}\cup \{z_{i}\}) - v(\mathcal{D}_{T})\right).
$$

Subsequent work generalized the approach in different ways, which we briefly summarize before discussing amortization. For example, Wang & Jia (2022) used the Banzhaf value rather than Shapley value, and Kwon & Zou (2021) considered the case of arbitrary seminalues (Monderer et al., 2002). These correspond to adopting a different weighting function, and the general case can be written as follows for a normalized weighting function  $w(k):^4$

$$
\psi (z _ {i}) = \sum_ {T \subseteq [ n ] \backslash \{i \}} w (| T |) \left(v (\mathcal {D} _ {T} \cup \{z _ {i} \}) - v (\mathcal {D} _ {T})\right).
$$

Next, another extension is the case of distributional data valuation. Ghorbani et al. (2020) incorporate an expectation over the original dataset  $\mathcal{D}$ , which they show leads to well defined scores even for data points  $z = (x,y)$  outside the training set. Given a distribution over datasets of size  $|\mathcal{D}| = n - 1$  and a weighting function  $w(k)$ , this version defines the score  $\psi(z) \in \mathbb{R}$  for arbitrary  $z$  as follows:

$$
\psi (z) = \mathbb {E} _ {\mathcal {D}} \sum_ {T \subseteq [ n - 1 ]} w (| T |) \left(v (\mathcal {D} _ {T} \cup \{z \}) - v (\mathcal {D} _ {T})\right).
$$

When using these methods in practice, each of these scores is difficult to calculate due to the intractable expectation across datasets. However, the crucial property they share is that they can all be estimated in an unbiased fashion, and their estimates can be used as noisy labels for stochastic amortization. We focus on Data Shapley and Distributional Data Shapley in our experiments (Ghorbani & Zou, 2019; Ghorbani et al., 2020), and we use the Monte Carlo estimator from Ghorbani & Zou (2019) as a noisy oracle (see the precise definition in Appendix E). Doing so allows us to train an amortized valuation model  $\psi(z; \theta) \in \mathbb{R}$  that accelerates valuation within our training dataset, and that can also be applied to external data, e.g., when selecting informative new data points for active learning (Ghorbani et al., 2022).

# 4.4. Datamodels

Finally, we briefly consider the data attribution technique known as datamodels (Ilyas et al., 2022). This method measures how much each training data point  $z_{i} \in \mathcal{D}$  affects the prediction for an inference example  $x \in \mathcal{X}$ , and it defines attribution scores  $\zeta(z_{i}, x) \in \mathbb{R}$  based on a least squares problem that can be solved after training models with many subsampled datasets. We defer our discussion to Appendix B because our experiments do not implement the proposed approach, but we show that the datamodels scores are equal to a simple expectation that can be estimated in an unbiased fashion, and that they can be amortized by using the unbiased estimates as training targets. Implementing this requires a more complex architecture to handle the two model inputs, but we speculate that if trained effectively, amortization could accelerate attribution within the training set and generalize to new inference examples  $x \in \mathcal{X}$  with no additional overhead.

# 5. Experiments

Our experiments apply stochastic amortization to several of the tasks discussed in Section 4. We consider both feature attribution and data valuation, on image and tabular datasets, and using multiple architectures for our amortized models, including fully-connected networks (FCNs), ResNets (He et al., 2016b) and ViTs (Dosovitskiy et al., 2020). The full implementation details are provided in Appendix F, including our exact models and hyperparameters.

Our goal in each experiment is to perform feature attribution or data valuation for an entire dataset, and to compare the accuracy from running existing estimators on each point to stochastic amortization. We adopt a noisy oracle for each task (e.g., a Monte Carlo estimator of data valuation scores), we fit an amortized network with one noisy label per training example, and our evaluation focuses on the accuracy of the amortized predictions relative to the ground truth. Our ground truth is obtained by running the noisy oracle for a large number of samples: for example, we run KernelSHAP (Lundberg & Lee, 2017) for feature attribution with 1M samples, and the TMC estimator (Ghorbani & Zou, 2019) for data valuation with 10K samples. We test amortization when using different numbers of training examples, and for both training and unseen external data to evaluate the model's generalization. We find that amortization often denoises and improves upon the noisy labels, leading to a significant accuracy improvement for the same computational budget.

# 5.2. Data valuation

Next, we consider data valuation with Data Shapley. For our noisy oracle, we obtain training labels by running the TMC estimator with different numbers of samples (Ghorbani & Zou, 2019). We first test our approach with the adult census and MiniBooNE particle physics datasets (Dua & Graff, 2017; Roe et al., 2005), and following prior work we conduct experiments using versions of each dataset with different numbers of data points (Jiang et al., 2023). Our valuation model must predict scores for each example  $z = (x,y)$ , so we train FCNs that output scores for all classes and use only the relevant output for each data point.<sup>7</sup>

As a first result, Figure 5 shows the estimation accuracy for the MiniBooNE dataset when using 1K and 10K data points. The Monte Carlo estimates converge as we use more samples, but we see that the amortized estimates are always more accurate in terms of both squared error and correlation with the ground truth. The improvement is largest for the noisiest estimates, where the amortized predictions have correlation  $>0.9$  when using only 50 samples. The benefits of amortization are more pronounced for the 10K dataset, which suggests that training with more noisy labels can be a substitute for high-quality labels. Appendix G shows similar results with the adult census dataset.

# 5.3. Distributional data valuation

Finally, we consider Distributional Data Shapley (Ghorbani et al., 2020), which is similar to the previous experiments but defines valuation scores even for points outside the training dataset; this allows us to test the valuation model's generalization to unseen data. We use our approach with CIFAR-10 (Krizhevsky et al., 2009), which contains 50K training examples, and for our valuation model we train a ResNet-18 network that outputs scores for each class.

Similar to the previous experiments, Figure 7 evaluates the estimation accuracy for different numbers of Monte Carlo samples. We observe that the distributional scores converge faster because we use a smaller maximum dataset cardinality (see Appendix F), but that amortization still provides a significant benefit. The improvement is largest for the noisiest estimates, which in this case use just 5 samples: amortization achieves correlation 0.81 with the ground truth, versus 0.58 for the Monte Carlo estimates. The improvement is

consistent across several measures of accuracy, including squared error, Pearson and Spearman correlation, and the portion of estimates with the correct sign (see Appendix G).

Next, we study generalization and the role of dataset size. We focus on the noisiest estimates with 5 Monte Carlo samples, because this low-sample regime is most relevant for larger datasets with millions of examples. We train the valuation model with different portions of the 50K training set, and we measure the estimation accuracy for both internal and unseen external data. Figure 8 shows improvement in squared error with as few as 1K data points, and improvement in correlation when we use at least 5K data points (10% of the dataset). We also observe a small generalization gap, suggesting that the valuation model can be trained with a subset of the data and reliably applied to unseen examples.

# 6. Conclusion

This work explored the idea of stochastic amortization, or training amortized models with noisy labels. Our main finding is that fast, noisy supervision provides substantial compute and accuracy gains over existing XML approximations. This approach makes several feature attribution and data valuation methods more practical for large datasets and real-time applications, and it may have broader applications to amortization beyond XML (Amos, 2022). Our work suggests several directions for future research, including scaling to datasets with millions of examples to test the limits of noisy supervision, leveraging more sophisticated data valuation estimators (Wu et al., 2023), testing efficient alternatives to model retraining (Koh & Liang, 2017; Kwon et al., 2023; Grosse et al., 2023), and exploring the use of amortization for datamodels (discussed in Appendix B).
