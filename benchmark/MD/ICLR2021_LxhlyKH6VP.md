# PROGAE: A GEOMETRIC AUTOENCODER-BASED GENERATIVE MODEL FOR DISENTANGLING PROTEIN DYNAMICS

Anonymous authors

Paper under double-blind review

# ABSTRACT

Understanding molecular dynamics is critical, especially for large flexible molecules like proteins. Protein function, as well as modulations thereof due to ligand binding or changes in environment, are intimately connected with these dynamics. This work focuses on learning a generative neural network on molecular simulation trajectories to characterize the distinct dynamics of a protein bound to various drug molecules. Specifically, we use a geometric autoencoder framework to learn separate latent space encodings of the intrinsic and extrinsic geometries of the system. For this purpose, the proposed Protein Geometric AutoEncoder (ProGAE) model is trained on the length of the alpha-carbon pseudobonds and the orientation of the backbone bonds of the protein. Using ProGAE latent embeddings, we reconstruct protein simulation trajectories at or near the experimental resolution. Empowered by the disentangled latent space learning, the extrinsic latent embedding is successfully used for classification or property prediction of different drugs bound to a specific protein. Additionally, ProGAE is able to be transferred to a trajectory of a different state of the same protein or to a completely different protein, where only the dense layer decoding from the latent representation needs to be retrained. Results show that our geometric learning-based method enjoys both accuracy and efficiency for generating complex molecular dynamics, charting the path toward scalable and improved approaches for analyzing and enhancing molecular simulations.

# 1 INTRODUCTION

The complex and time-consuming calculations in molecular simulations have been significantly impacted by the application of machine learning techniques in recent years. In particular, deep learning has been applied to analysis and simulation of molecular trajectories to address diverse problems, such as estimating free energy surfaces, defining optimal reaction coordinates, constructing Markov State Models, and enhancing molecular sampling. For a comprehensive review of deep learning methods for analyzing and enhancing molecular simulations, see (Noé et al., 2020a) and (Noé et al., 2020b).

Specifically, there has been interest in modeling the underlying conformational space of protein dynamics via deterministic (Ramaswamy et al., 2020) or probabilistic (Varolgüneş et al., 2020; Bhowmik et al., 2018; Guo et al., 2020) autoencoder (AE) -based approaches. This line of work has mainly attempted to respect the domain geometry by using convolutional AEs on features extracted from 3D structures. In parallel, learning directly from 3D structure has recently developed into an exciting and promising application area for deep learning. In this work, we learn the protein dynamics directly from the protein structures using geometric deep learning. We also investigate how the geometry of the proteins itself can assist learning and improving latent conformational space interpretability. To this end, we propose a Protein Geometric AutoEncoder model, named ProGAE, to separately encode intrinsic and extrinsic protein geometries.

The main contributions of this work are summarized:

Inspired by the recent unsupervised geometric disentanglement learning works (Tatro et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2020), we propose a novel geometric autoencoder named ProGAE that

directly learns from protein 3D structures by separately encoding the intrinsic and the extrinsic geometries into disjoint latent spaces and generates protein structures from these latent spaces.

- We further propose a novel formulation, in which network intrinsic input is taken as the  $\mathrm{C}_{\alpha} - \mathrm{C}_{\alpha}$  pseudo-bond distances, and the extrinsic input is the backbone bond orientations.  
- Analysis shows that the learned extrinsic geometric latent space can be used for drug classification and drug property prediction, where the drug is bound to the given protein.  
- Experiments confirm that the extrinsic geometry of the proteins generated by ProGAE is essentially invariant to changes in the intrinsic latent code. This allows for better latent space interpretability.  
- We also demonstrate that the learned ProGAE can be transferred to a trajectory of the protein in a different state or a trajectory of a different protein all-together.

# 1.1 RELATED WORK

Recently, there has been a body of work on using deep learning to learn from structures involving proteins (Graves et al., 2020; Klicpera et al., 2020; Jing et al., 2020). For example, Gainza et al. (2019) uses geometric deep learning to predict docking sites for protein interactions. Ingraham et al. (2019a) solves the inverse folding problem using a graph transformer on the protein backbone. Degiacomi (2019) uses an AE to generate candidate proteins for docking. Hermosilla et al. (2020) leverages the notion of intrinsic and extrinsic geometry to define an architecture for fold classification task.

Additionally, there has been focus on learning molecular dynamics from simulation trajectories. Wang et al. (2020) learns the dynamics between a particular starting conformation and a particular target conformation by training a control Hamiltonian represented by a graph neural network. From the perspective of Hamiltonian systems, Chen et al. (2020) introduces Symplectic RNNs which leverages symplectic integration when learning the dynamics of a physical system. Ingraham et al. (2019b) learns dynamics via a differentiable simulator using Langevin dynamics. Mardt et al. (2018) introduces VAMPnet which maps molecular coordinates to Markov states capturing molecular kinetics. Lee et al. (2019) augments molecular dynamics simulation with deep learning to improve the sampling of the folded states of proteins. Tsai et al. (2020) uses a LSTM language model to predict protein dynamics. Additionally, several other works exist that use AE-based approaches for analyzing the protein conformational space (Ramaswamy et al., 2020; Varolgunes et al., 2020; Bhowmik et al., 2018; Guo et al., 2020).

Another line of work involves enhanced sampling with neural net approximations of slow variables (Chen & Ferguson, 2018; Chen et al., 2019; Ribeiro et al., 2018). Deep and adversarial learning have been employed for enhanced sampling (Bonati et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2019). Noé et al. (2019) uses deep generative neural net to directly sample the equilibrium distribution of a many-body system defined by an energy function, without using molecular simulation.

These mentioned works have not considered explicit disentangling of intrinsic and extrinsic geometries. To the best of our knowledge, this work is the first to propose an autoencoder for the unsupervised geometric disentanglement of protein conformational space captured in molecular dynamics simulations. This disentangled representations provide better interpretability of the latent space in terms of the physico-chemical and geometric attributes.

# 2 PROGAE FOR PROTEIN DYNAMICS

First, we introduce a novel geometric autoencoder (ProGAE) for generating protein dynamics. After that, We discuss the input signals, and how they can reconstruct the protein coordinates.

Geometric Features of Protein (Pseudo)Bonds as Network Input ProGAE functions by separately encoding intrinsic and extrinsic geometry with the goal of achieving better latent space interpretability. To be precise, we here clarify the notions of intrinsic and extrinsic geometry. Mathematically, we can consider a manifold independently of its embedding in Euclidean space. Properties that do not depend on this Euclidean embedding are said to be intrinsic geometric properties, while properties that do are referred to be extrinsic. As an example, given two atoms of a protein, the

For a more in-depth discussion of intrinsic and extrinsic geometry, we refer the reader to (Do Carmo, 2016).

We note that the protein backbone trace can be viewed as a discretized curve in Euclidean space. The protein backbone itself can be viewed as a discretized curve locally. Both objects are depicted in Figure 1 with their geometric features as network input. To be clear, a discretized curve is a curve approximated by a polygonal chain. Without difficulty, we can see that a polygonal chain can be determined up to translation given both the length and orientation of its line segments. Then it follows that the protein backbone can be determined given the length and orientation of its bonds. Here the length of these bonds is intrinsic while the orientation is extrinsic. Thus, to decouple the intrinsic and extrinsic geometry, we can consider encoding these signals.

While we could use the length of the bonds in the backbone as the intrinsic signal input into the network, this is not the signal that we use. This is because the length of covalent bonds undergoes very little change during a simulation performed using an empirical force-field, like the simulations considered in this work. A standard deviation of less than  $0.059\AA$  from target bond lengths is common in PDB structures (Jaskolski et al., 2007). To this end, we consider intrinsic geometry at a coarser level, so that the resulting signal has more variability. Specifically, we consider the length of the  $\mathbf{C}_{\alpha} - \mathbf{C}_{\alpha}$  pseudobonds in the backbone trace as a representative of the intrinsic geometry of a protein, where as backbone bond orientations are considered for capturing extrinsic geometry.

We model the backbone by the graph,  $\mathcal{G}_b = (\mathbb{V}_b,\pmb {E}_b)$ , and the backbone trace by the graph,  $\mathcal{G}_t = (\mathbb{V}_t,\pmb {E}_t)$ . Then our intrinsic and extrinsic signals,  $Int:\pmb {E}_t\to \mathbb{R}$  and  $Ext:\pmb {E}_b\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^3$  are defined:

$$
I n t \left(E _ {i j}\right) = \left\| E _ {i j} \right\| _ {2}, \quad E _ {i j} \in \boldsymbol {E} _ {t}, \quad \operatorname {E x t} \left(E _ {i j}\right) = s g n (j - i) \frac {E _ {i j}}{\left\| E _ {i j} \right\|}, \quad E _ {i j} \in \boldsymbol {E} _ {b}. \tag {1}
$$

Network Architecture With the network inputs defined, we discuss the architecture of ProGAE. The core idea is to create an intrinsic latent space,  $L_{I}\in \mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}$ , and an extrinsic latent space,  $L_{E}\in \mathbb{R}^{n_{e}}$ , via separately encoding the intrinsic and extrinsic signals. To this end, our network contains two encoders, Enci and Ence where:

$$
\operatorname {E n c} _ {i} \circ \operatorname {I n t} \left(\boldsymbol {E} _ {t}\right) \in L _ {I}, \quad \operatorname {E n c} _ {e} \circ \operatorname {E x t} \left(\boldsymbol {E} _ {b}\right) \in L _ {E}. \tag {2}
$$

We then jointly decode these latent vectors to recover the coordinates of the atoms in the protein backbone. Thus, we formally define the decoder:

$$
D e c: L _ {I} \times L _ {E} \rightarrow \mathbb {R} ^ {\left| \mathbb {V} _ {b} \right| \times 3}. \tag {3}
$$

This high level structure of ProGAE is depicted in Figure 1. We now describe the architecture of the encoders and decoders. As these edge-based signals are defined on a geometric domain, it is sensible to learn feature representations using geometric convolution that respects the geometry of the data. The intrinsic encoder is the most simple, as the signal is defined on the backbone trace, which corresponds to a set of discrete curves. Here each curve corresponds to a protein fragment. Then we define  $Enc_{i}$  to be a series of 1D convolutions operating on each protein fragment. Each convolution is taken to have a stride of 2, and is followed with batch normalization layers and ReLU.

In contrast, the extrinsic encoder operates on a signal defined on the protein backbone, which we associate with a graph. So the graph attention layers of graph attention networks (GATs) introduced in (Veličković et al., 2017) are a natural tool to use in our architecture, albeit with some modification. Since the input signal is defined only on the edges of the graph,  $E_{b}$ , we define a signal on the graph vertices,  $\mathbb{V}_{b}$ , as the average value of its incident edges. Then the first layer of the extrinsic encoder uses the edge-convolution operator of (Gong & Cheng, 2019) to map this graph signal to a signal defined exclusively on the graph vertices,  $\mathbb{V}_{b}$ . The rest of the encoder contains successive graph attention layers with sparsity defined by a given neighborhood radius and a stride of 2. Given  $L$  layers, this can be thought to define a sequence of graphs,  $\{\mathcal{G}_{b,i}\}_{i=0}^{L}$ , with increasing decimation. As with  $Enc_{i}$ , each layer is followed with batch normalization and ReLU.

Global average pooling is applied to the output of each encoder to introduce invariance to the number of vertices in  $\mathbb{V}_t$  and  $\mathbb{V}_b$ . Dense layers then map each result to their respective latent spaces,  $L_I$  and  $L_E$ . The Tanh activation function is applied to bound the latent space. This produces the intrinsic and extrinsic latent codes,  $z_i$  and  $z_e$ .

To decode from the latent embedding, the latent code  $\mathbf{z}$  is taken to be the concatenation of the two latent codes,  $[z_i, z_e]$ . A dense layer then maps  $\mathbf{z}$  to the a signal defined on the most decimated backbone graph,  $\mathcal{G}_{b,L}$ . The structure of the decoder,  $Dec$ , is then analogous to  $Enc_e$ , though the convolutions are essentially transposed. The output of  $Dec$  is the point cloud,  $\hat{\mathbf{P}}$ , corresponding to the predicted coordinates of the backbone atoms,  $\mathbb{V}_b \approx \mathbf{P}$ .

Loss Function The first term in the loss function is a basic reconstruction loss, where  $P$  and  $\hat{P}$  are taken to be the true and predicted coordinates of the protein backbone atoms. Namely, we evaluate their difference using Smooth-  $L_{1}$  loss. This loss is defined as

$$
S m o o t h L _ {1} (\boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {y}) := \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {\# \boldsymbol {x}} z _ {i}, \quad \text {w h e r e} \quad z _ {i} = \min  \left(\frac {\delta^ {2}}{2} (x _ {i} - y _ {i}) ^ {2}, \delta | x _ {i} - y _ {i} | - \frac {1}{2}\right), \tag {4}
$$

where we choose  $\delta = 2$ . This loss function can roughly be viewed as  $L_{2}$  loss altered to be more robust to outliers and prevent exploding gradients (Girshick, 2015).

As the reconstruction loss depends on the embedding of the protein in Euclidean space, it may not necessarily best measure if the intrinsic geometry is being faithfully reconstructed. We would also like to promote the function of the intrinsic latent space during training. To be clear, we consider two encoded proteins with latent codes,  $[z_{i,1}, z_{e,1}]$  and  $[z_{i,2}, z_{e,2}]$ . Then we form a new latent variable,

$$
\hat {z} _ {i} = (1 - \beta) \boldsymbol {z} _ {i, 1} + \beta \boldsymbol {z} _ {i, 2}, \quad \hat {\boldsymbol {z}} _ {e} = \boldsymbol {z} _ {e, 1}, \quad \beta \sim \mathbf {U} [ 0, 1 ]. \tag {5}
$$

Each of these latent variable decodes to some point cloud  $\hat{P}$ . We let  $Int(\pmb{E}_{t,\beta})$ ,  $Int(\pmb{E}_{t,1})$ , and  $Int(\pmb{E}_{t,2})$  be the lengths of the  $C_{\alpha} - C_{\alpha}$  pseudobonds of the generated proteins from the interpolated latent code and the two given latent codes. We then introduce a bond length penalty given by,

$$
\mathcal {R} \left(\hat {\boldsymbol {P}} _ {1}, \hat {\boldsymbol {P}} _ {2}; \beta\right) = \left| \left| I n t \left(\boldsymbol {E} _ {t, \beta}\right) - \left((1 - \beta) I n t \left(\boldsymbol {E} _ {t, 1}\right) + \beta I n t \left(\boldsymbol {E} _ {t, 2}\right)\right) \right| \right| _ {1}. \tag {6}
$$

This penalty can be viewed as promoting faithful reconstruction of the pseudobond length between  $C_{\alpha}$  atoms, as well as a smooth interpolation of these lengths along paths in  $L_{I}$ . This penalty is analogous to the metric preservation regularizer introduced in (Cosmo et al., 2020) for 3D meshes. Importantly, this promotes the generation of a protein whose metric of the backbone trace is invariant to the extrinsic latent code,  $z_{e}$ . Thus, the loss function  $\mathcal{L}$  for ProGAE is,

$$
\mathcal {L} \left(\left(\hat {\boldsymbol {P}} _ {1}, \hat {\boldsymbol {P}} _ {2}\right), \left(\boldsymbol {P} _ {1}, \boldsymbol {P} _ {2}\right)\right) := \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {2} S m o o t h L _ {1} \left(\hat {\boldsymbol {P}} _ {i}, \boldsymbol {P} _ {i}\right) + \lambda_ {R} \mathbb {E} _ {\beta \in \mathbf {U} [ 0, 1 ]} \mathcal {R} \left(\hat {\boldsymbol {P}} _ {1}, \hat {\boldsymbol {P}} _ {2}; \beta\right). \tag {7}
$$

# 3 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP

In this section, we describe the setup of our numerical experiments confirming the usefulness of ProGAE in generating protein dynamics. For each dataset, we train three models, each from a different random seed, and report both mean and standard deviation in our results. Details on the choice of network hyperparameters can be found in the Appendix A.2.

Datasets Datasets used in this work are atomistic simulation trajectories  ${}^{1}$  (D.E. Shaw Research, 2020). The two main datasets we use are simulations of proteins in presence of FDA approved or under-investigation molecules. These are: (1) 50 independent trajectories, each simulating the SARS-CoV-2 trimeric spike protein (S protein) in presence of a distinct drug for  ${2\mu }\mathrm{s}$  . The simulation is limited to 3 receptor binding domains (RBDs) of the S protein, as well as another short region needed for the system to maintain a trimer assembly; (2) 75 independent trajectories, each simulating the ectodomain protein of human ACE2 (hACE2) in presence of a distinct drug for  ${2\mu }\mathrm{s}$  .

The S protein and the hACE2 protein contains 3,690 backbone atoms and 2,386 backbone atoms, respectively. The time resolution is 1,200 ps. We use the first  $70\%$  of frames from each trajectory to form the training set. The next  $10\%$  and the last  $20\%$  of frames form the validation set and test set, respectively. This split tests the ability of the trained model to extrapolate or recovery from the dynamics present in the first part of the trajectory to the last part of the trajectory.

# 4 RESULTS

Structure Reconstruction (or Recovery) Figures 2a and 2b display the ability of ProGAE to accurately reconstruct protein trajectories for both the S protein and hACE2. From the log of atom-wise  $L_{2}$  error mapped on bonds, as well as from visual comparison, it is clear that our network can capture and reconstruct notable conformational changes of a protein. Figures 5a and 5b in the appendix display these reconstructions with color denoting fragment instead of  $L_{2}$  error. Consistent with low  $L_{2}$  error, visually the reconstructed backbones appear consistent with ground truths, with larger deviations observed in the flexible loop regions.

Table 1 contains performance metrics of ProGAE on training and test sets. Generalization is measured by the  $L_{2}$  reconstruction error of the coordinates of the backbone atoms. For hACE2, we are able to achieve sub-Angstrom performance on the test set. In either case, the reconstruction is within experimental resolution of the associated PDB files; 6VXX/6VW1 for the S protein and 6VW1 for hACE2. Additionally, the average error in the length of the pseudobonds is also sub-Angstrom. For reference, the average pseudobond in the datasets has a length of  $3.86\AA$ . Thus, it is evident that ProGAE is able to reconstruct proteins within meaningful resolution.

Utility of the Extrinsic Latent Space With the reconstruction capabilities of ProGAE verified, we consider the benefit of having separate intrinsic and extrinsic latent spaces. First, we explore the statistical relationship between the learned intrinsic latent space and the extrinsic latent space. Canonical correlation analysis (CCA) is a natural approach to study if a linear relationship exists (Hardoon et al., 2004). We include background on CCA in Appendix A.1.

Table 2 includes the leading correlation between the intrinsic and extrinsic latent spaces for each dataset. Note this correlation is very low, implying that there is a negligible linear relationship between the intrinsic and extrinsic latent spaces. Learning a disentangled latent representation is often desired for better interpretability, which is typically measured using a generalization of mutual information such as total correlation as in (Chen et al., 2018). As stated earlier, each trajectory in the dataset corresponds to the S or hACE2 protein bound to a specific drug. Then it is natural to investigate if this distinct drug information is encoded in the two disentangled latent spaces. Table 2 contains the performance of a linear classifier trained on the different latent spaces to classify the drug present in each frame. It is clear that the drug molecule can be almost perfectly classified in the extrinsic latent space, while such classification is random in the intrinsic latent space. Figures 3a and 3b display the embeddings of the test set in the latent spaces, projected to the first two canonical components. In these figures, color denotes the identity of the drug that the protein is bound to. Even in the 2D projection of the extrinsic latent space, clustering by the drug identity is apparent, which is not the case for the intrinsic embedding.

Next, we consider if this linear separation is chemically meaningful. To this end, we train a linear regression model on the extrinsic latent space to predict certain physico-chemical properties of a drug binding to a protein. Table 3 displays the performance of the regression model at predicting the properties of molecular weight, hydrogen bond donor count, and topological polar surface area. For comparison, we train a linear regression model on the first  $n_e$  principal component scores corresponding to the PCA of the extrinsic signal on each element of the test dataset. The latent regression outperforms that of PCA, indicating that the extrinsic latent embedding captures more physico-chemical information about the bound drug.

Geometry of the Generated Dynamics As a generative model, it is important to consider if the dynamics generated via paths in the latent space match our expectations. One way in which we measure this is how the distance matrix of atoms in a generated protein changes as the latent variables change. Specifically, we are interested in the distance between non-bonded atoms.

In Figures 4a and 4b, we plot the norm of the difference between these distance matrices for the generated protein and that of the protein at its initiation. We sample the latent space along two principal components, while setting others to 0. So in these figures 4a and 4b, we see three different cross sections of this space. It is immediately apparent that a change in the extrinsic latent code dominates changes in the non-bonded distance. This is a desired behavior of a disentangled geometric autoencoder, where large-scale changes are captured in the extrinsic latent space, while small-scale changes in local distance are controlled by the intrinsic latent space.

# 5 CONCLUSION

In this work we introduce a novel geometric autoencoder named ProGAE for learning meaningful disentangled representations of the protein structural dynamics. Our model accurately reconstructs the protein dynamics. The autoencoder framework separately encodes intrinsic and extrinsic geometries to ensure better latent space interpretability. The extrinsic latent space can classify the protein structures with respect to the bound drug molecules, as well as can predict the drug properties. We also show that the geometric convolutional filters learned in training can be successfully transferred to trajectories of different protein systems, irrespective of system size, conformational state, or presence of protein-protein interaction. These results on learning, predicting, and generating protein dynamics suggest that the proposed framework can serve as the first step towards bridging geometric deep learning with molecular dynamics.
