# Abstract

Stance detection holds great potential for enhancing the quality of online political discussions, as it has shown to be useful for summarizing discussions, detecting misinformation, and evaluating opinion distributions. Usually, transformer-based models are used directly for stance detection, which require large amounts of data. However, the broad range of debate questions in online political discussion creates a variety of possible scenarios that the model is faced with and thus makes data acquisition for model training difficult. In this work, we show how to leverage LLM-generated synthetic data to train and improve stance detection agents for online political discussions: (i) We generate synthetic data for specific debate questions by prompting a Mistral-7B model and show that fine-tuning with the generated synthetic data can substantially improve the performance of stance detection. (ii) We examine the impact of combining synthetic data with the most informative samples from an unlabelled dataset. First, we use the synthetic data to select the most informative samples, second, we combine both these samples and the synthetic data for fine-tuning. This approach reduces labelling effort and consistently surpasses the performance of the baseline model that is trained with fully labeled data. Overall, we show in comprehensive experiments that LLM-generated data greatly improves stance detection performance for online political discussions.

# 1 Introduction

With the recent advent of powerful generative Large Language Models (LLMs) such as  $\mathrm{ChatGPT}^1$ , Llama [Touvron et al., 2023] and Mistral [Jiang et al., 2023], investigating how synthetic data from LLMs can be leveraged for downstream tasks has become a very important and relevant topic of research. It is well known that Natural Language Processing (NLP) tasks based on single-label or multi-label classification such as stance and sentiment detection benefit immensely from large amounts of labelled data [Mehrafarin et al., 2022]. However, in many cases, labelled data is scarce, especially in the realm of online political discussions: one issue is that gathering labelled data may not be allowed for privacy reasons. Another issue is that especially in stance detection for online political discussions comments and their stances are intrinsically linked to a question. An obvious approach to stance detection might be to apply LLMs directly, which has been tried with mixed results (see Section 2). Furthermore, LLMs are not yet fast enough at inference time for smaller real world interactive deployment in online discussions, unless adequate hardware infrastructure is provided. How exactly synthetic data from an LLM should be combined with a small pool of unlabelled training data will be investigated along the following questions:

Question 1 (Q1): Does fine-tuning with synthetic data improve the stance detection model? Answer: Yes, and the more the better! One problem of stance detection datasets for online political discussions is the lack of (balanced) data for specific questions (sometimes also called issues). While fine-tuning with synthetic data is possible for BERT-based models, it was previously found to be challenging [Mahmoudi et al., 2024]. Using more powerful LLMs, we can show that fine-tuning with synthetic data does improve stance detection performance in online political discussions. This also holds when augmenting existing labelled data.

Question 2 (Q2): Can the labelling effort be reduced by controlling what example to manually label using active learning with synthetic data? Answer: Yes, even if we don't have real true labels initially. Another challenge in stance detection for online political discussions is labelling data, which is expensive and time consuming. Active learning can help us to identify the most informative samples that are then labelled manually, even if there are initially no labelled samples. For this we use the synthetic samples which have labels by construction, to select the most ambiguous (and thus most informative) samples in the unlabelled dataset.

Question (Q3): Can fine-tuning using the most informative samples plus the synthetic data outperform fine-tuning with a completely manually labelled dataset? Answer: Yes, it can! Our study reveals that the most effective way to improve stance detection performance is to combine both

synthetic data and the most informative samples obtained via active learning. Surprisingly, this is even better than using a full dataset that has been completely manually labelled.

# 2 Background

Stance Detection for Online Political Discussions. Stance detection is a sub-task of sentiment analysis [Romberg and Escher, 2023] and opinion mining [ALDayel and Magdy, 2021] that aims to automatically detect the stance (e.g., favor, against, or neutral) of the author of a contribution towards a discussed issue, or target. In online political discussions, stances can be defined as the side that the author is on, e.g., for or against an increase in value added tax. Research has identified stance detection as an important yet challenging task; it is important because the automated detection of stances can, e.g., improve discussion summarisation [Chowanda et al., 2017], facilitate the detection of misinformation [Hardalov et al., 2022], and provide more comprehensive evaluations of opinion distributions in online political discussions and participation processes [Romberg and Escher, 2023]. Stance detection is also implemented as a feature in many recommender systems and discussion platforms [Küçük and Can, 2020]. Still, due to its dependency on context, stance detection is a highly challenging task. In online political discussions, for example, detecting the stance of a contribution towards a question requires a proper identification of both the question under discussion and the position of the contributor towards it. However, users regularly deviate from the original question and discuss multiple topics in the same thread [Ziegele et al., 2014], resulting in a high number of potential questions with often very few instances for training purposes. Fine-tuning transformer-based models [Vaswani et al., 2017] to solve stance detection is a common practice, but training these models requires a large amount of annotated data, which for the large variety of questions in online political discussions is unfeasible to acquire. We therefore in our work, show how to improve stance detection for online political discussions with synthetic data.

Active Learning. The aim of active learning is to minimize the effort of labelling data, while simultaneously maximizing the model's performance. This is achieved by selecting a query strategy that chooses the most interesting samples from a set of unlabelled data points, which we refer to as most informative samples. These samples are then passed to, e.g., a human annotator for labelling. The usual setup consists of iteratively training a model on a small subset of manually labelled data that stems from a pool of unlabelled data. There exist many different query strategies such as Query By Committee (QBC, [Seung et al., 1992]), Minimum Expected Entropy (MEE, Holub et al. [2008] or Contrastive Active Learning (CAL, Margatina et al. [2021]). By actively choosing samples and asking for the correct labelling, the model is able to learn from few labelled data points, which is advantageous especially when annotated datasets are not available. Within the domain of political text analysis, many different tasks lack large amounts of annotated data. It has been already shown in the past that these tasks can benefit from the active learning: e.g., stance detection [Kucher et al., 2017], topic modeling [Romberg and Escher, 2022], speech act classification [Schmidt et al., 2023] or toxic comment classification [Miller et al., 2020]. In this work, we examine how LLM-generated synthetic data can be used instead of real labelled data to select the most informative samples to be manually labelled.

Using LLM-generated synthetic data for fine tuning. Recent work has shown that synthetic data generated from LLMs can be used to improve the performance of a model on downstream tasks. Møller et al. [2023] showed that synthetic data can be used to improve the performance of a model on downstream classification tasks by comparing the performance of a model finetuned on LLM-generated data to crowd annotated data. In many cases the model finetuned on LLM-generated data outperforms the model finetuned on crowd annotated data. Mahmoudi et al. [2024] study the use of synthetic data for data augmentation in stance-detection. The authors use GPT-3 to generate synthetic data for a specific topic with mixed results due to the inability of GPT-3 to generate good data samples. In our work, we use a newer LLM model, Mistral-7B, which generates better synthetic data samples and show that we can generate synthetic data that matches the real data distribution. Veselovsky et al. [2023] analyse in which ways synthetic data is best generated for tasks like sarcasm detection and sentiment analysis. The authors reach the conclusion that grounding the prompts to generate the synthetic data to real samples helps improve the quality of the synthetic data. Similarly, Li et al. [2023] argue that subjectivity in the classification task determines whether synthetic data can be used effectively.

Using LLMs directly for stance detection. It has been shown that LLMs can be used directly for stance detection such as [Cruickshank and Xian Ng, 2023], [Burnham, 2023]. However, the general conclusion of these studies is that while LLMs are competitive with other transformer models such as BERT, especially for edge cases, they exhibit replication issues. [Burnham, 2023] also discuss the possibility of pre-training models on more specific data to improve the generalisation capability of the model. We therefore focus on using LLMs to generate synthetic data to solve key challenges in stance detection such as the lack of available data for specific topics and labelling large amounts of data, rather than using LLMs directly for the task.

# 3 Method

In line with our declared contributions, we present our core ideas to improve the performance of the stance detection model: (i) To fine-tune the model with synthetic data, we first define the synthetic dataset and show our pipeline to generate it. The baseline model is then further fine-tuned on the synthetic data. (ii) We then present our synthetic data-based approach to identify the most informative samples from a batch of unlabelled data. We propose a synthetic extension to the QBC (Query by Committee, [Seung et al., 1992]) method, where the synthetic data act as an ensemble of experts.

# 3.1 Preliminaries

Political discussions are typically centered around questions  $q \in \mathcal{Q}$  (sometimes also called issues or targets). For stance detection, we usually have for each of these questions  $q$  a set of labelled data  $\mathcal{D}^{(q)} = \{(x^{(i)},y^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{I}$  where  $x^{(i)} \in \mathcal{X}$  is a statement (or comment) and  $y^{(i)}$  is the stance of the statement, with  $y^{(i)} \in \{0,1\} = \mathcal{Y}$ . Note, that we use the notation  $\mathcal{D}^{(q)}$  for labelled and for unlabelled datasets (then the labels are ignored). We view the stance detection model as a binary classification function  $f: \mathcal{Q} \times \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ , where we included the question as input to provide context. The classifier  $f$  can be split into two parts,  $f = h \circ g$ : the first part is a pre-trained language model  $g: \mathcal{Q} \times \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{E}$  such as BERT [Devlin et al., 2019] or RoBERTa [Liu et al., 2019]. The second part is a classifier  $h: \mathcal{E} \to \mathcal{Y}$  on top of the pre-trained model  $g$ . The pre-trained language model  $g$  outputs an embedding  $e^{(i)} \in \mathcal{E}$  given a question  $q$  and an input  $x^{(i)}$ . The classifier  $h$  is usually a linear layer or a multi-layer perceptron, which takes the embedding  $e^{(i)}$  from  $g$  as input and outputs a prediction  $\hat{y}^{(i)}$ . The stance detection model is fine-tuned by minimizing the cross-entropy loss between the predicted labels  $\hat{y}^{(i)} = f(q,x^{(i)})$  and the actual labels  $y^{(i)}$ .

# 3.2 Generating Synthetic Data for Stance Detection

To generate synthetic samples, we employ a quantized version of the Mistral-7B-instruct-v0.1 model $^2$  to generate comments on a specific question  $q$ , using the following prompt:

A user in a discussion forum is debating other users about the following question: [q] The person is in favor about the topic in question. What would the person write? Write from the person's first person perspective.

where "[q]" must be replaced with the question  $q$ . Similarly, to generate a negative sample, we replace "is in favor" with "is not in favor". As in the X-Stance dataset [Vamvas and Sennrich, 2020], we assign the two labels 0 and 1. We denote the question-specific synthetic dataset as:

$$
\mathcal {D} _ {\text {s y n t h}} ^ {(q)} = \left\{\left(x _ {\text {s y n t h}} ^ {(m)}, 1\right) \right\} _ {m = 1} ^ {M / 2} \cup \left\{\left(x _ {\text {s y n t h}} ^ {(m)}, 0\right) \right\} _ {m = 1 + M / 2} ^ {M} \tag {1}
$$

where half of the  $M$  synthetic data samples have positive labels, i.e., are comments in favor for the posed question, while the other half is against. Since the dataset is in German, we translate the questions  $q$  with a "NLLB-300M" [NLLB Team et al., 2022] translation model. The English answers from the Mistral-7B model are then translated back to German using the translation model.

We show the translation pipeline in Figure 9 along with translations of the questions chosen for our experiments in Table 2. We also attach the synthetic generated data in both English and German to the paper. In our experiments, this translation pipeline worked well even for complex topics in online political discussions, making our method applicable to any language where a good translation model

exists. Overall, the generated dataset  $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{synth}}^{(q)}$  will be used in two ways: (i) to augment the existing dataset  $\mathcal{D}^{(q)}$  in order to increase the amount of training data, and (ii) to detect the most informative samples in the unlabelled data pool, which is explained next.

# 3.3 Synthetic Data-driven Query By Comittee (SQBC)

In the typical active learning scenario, we have a set of unlabelled data, where the goal is to choose the most informative samples to label. Our approach is inspired by the two methods Query by Committee (QBC) [Seung et al., 1992] and Contrastive Active Learning (CAL, Margatina et al. [2021]): instead of using QBC's ensemble of experts and the KL-divergence based information score in CAL, we directly use the synthetic data and its labels to produce classification scores for the unlabelled data using  $k$  nearest neighbors. The most informative samples are then the data points with the most indecisive scores. Since we query a synthetic dataset instead of an ensemble of experts (as in QBC), we call our method Synthetic data-driven QBC or short SQBC.

Our method performs three steps:

(1) Generate the embeddings. Given some embedding function  $g: \mathcal{Q} \times \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{E}$ , we generate embeddings for the unlabelled data,

$$
E = \left\{e ^ {(i)} \right\} _ {i = 1} ^ {I} = \left\{g (q, x ^ {(i)}) \right\} _ {i = 1} ^ {I} \tag {2}
$$

and for the labelled synthetic data

$$
E _ {\text {s y n t h}} = \left\{e _ {\text {s y n t h}} ^ {(m)} \right\} _ {m = 1} ^ {M} = \left\{g (q, x _ {\text {s y n t h}} ^ {(m)}) \right\} _ {m = 1} ^ {M}. \tag {3}
$$

Note that  $q$  is the question for which we generate the synthetic data and for which we want to detect the most informative samples. If obvious from the context, we often omit the superscript  $(q)$ .

(2) Using the synthetic nearest neighbours as oracles to score the unlabelled data. For the  $i$ -th unlabelled embedding  $e^{(i)}$  let  $\mathrm{NN}(i)$  be the set of indices of the  $k$  nearest neighbours (wrt. to the embeddings using the cosine similarity) among the labelled embeddings  $E_{\mathrm{synth}}$ . The score for each unlabelled data point counts the number of labels  $y_{\mathrm{synth}}^{(m)} = 1$  among the nearest neighbours, i.e.,

$$
s (i) = \sum_ {m \in \mathrm {N N} (i)} y _ {\text {s y n t h}} ^ {(m)} \in \{0, \dots , k \}. \tag {4}
$$

For our experiments, we choose  $k = M / 2$  which worked well across all experiments (other values for  $k$  are possible, but did not lead to significantly better results).

(3) Choosing the most informative samples. The scores take values between 0 and  $k$ . For 0, the synthetic nearest neighbours all have labels  $y_{\mathrm{synth}}^{(m)} = 0$ , for value  $k$ , all have labels  $y_{\mathrm{synth}}^{(m)} = 1$ . The most informative samples have a score around  $k / 2$ . We thus adjust the range of the scores so that values in the middle range have the smallest scores (close to 0). We do this by subtracting  $k / 2$  from the score and taking the absolute value,

$$
s ^ {\prime} (i) = \left| s (i) - k / 2 \right|. \tag {5}
$$

The  $J$  most informative samples  $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{Minf}}^{(q)}\subset \mathcal{D}^{(q)}$  among the unlabelled samples are the  $J$  samples with the smallest scores. In the experiments we vary  $J$  to study the impact of manually labelled most informative samples.

(4) Labelling the most informative samples. This step must be performed manually, i.e., the most informative samples selected in the previous step are labelled by a human expert. Attempting to assign labels automatically using majority vote from the synthetic samples did not work well, since the most informative samples are exactly at the boundary between the classes, leading to random and thus noisy labels.

# 4 Experiments

We now describe the datasets, the experiments and the results. Our experiments follow the structure of the questions Q1, Q2 and Q3 posed in the introduction. Apart from presenting numerical results for these questions, we also provide further insights by visualizing the embeddings of the synthetic and real world datasets and by showing the selection behaviour of the active learning methods.

# 4.1 Datasets

X-Stance dataset. To evaluate our methods, we use the X-Stance dataset [Vamvas and Senrich, 2020], a multilingual stance detection dataset, which contains a total of 67,000 annotated comments on 194 policy-related questions, answered by Swiss election candidates. X-stance includes comments in German (48,600), French (17,200) and Italian (1,400). For this paper we only included the German comments, but as mentioned above, other languages can be easily included by automatic translation. The data has been extracted from smartvote, a Swiss voting advice platform. The comments are labelled either as being in favor (positive) or against (negative) the posed question, which is a typical labelling scheme for stance detection datasets. The X-Stance dataset is split in training and testing questions, i.e., a question in the training dataset does not appear in the test dataset. Furthermore, for each question  $q$  from the training data, there are several annotated comments, which form the dataset  $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}^{(q)}$ . Similarly, for each question  $q$  in the test data, the set of annotated comments is written as  $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}^{(q)}$ . To refer to the whole training dataset we write  $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}} = \cup_{q\in \mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}^{(q)}$ .

To limit computation time, we selected 10 questions from the test dataset to evaluate our method. The selection was done to best reflect the variability of the data (see Appendix D.1): we choose questions with small amount of comments, unbalanced comments and also balanced comments. Furthermore, for 5 of the questions the majority class is favor and for the other 5 the majority class is against.

Synthetic dataset. To test the synthetic data-augmentation and active learning based on SQBC (see Section 3.3) we generate sets of varying sizes  $M = \{200,500,1000\}$  for each of the 10 questions. The synthetic data follows the same structure as the data from the X-stance dataset, where for a specific question  $q$  we have  $M$  comments and  $M$  labels. Each set contains  $M / 2$  positive labels and  $M / 2$  negative labels, i.e., the synthetic data is balanced.

# 4.2 Experimental Setup

General setup. For all experiments, the starting model is the pre-trained BERT base $^3$  model. We adapt the model to the stance detection task by training it on the X-Stance training dataset  $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}$  (across all questions). We call this the Baseline, and it is characteristic for BERT-based stance detection (e.g., Vamvas and Sennrich [2020]). For fine-tuning and testing we evaluate the given model separately on 10 chosen questions from the test dataset of X-Stance for all experiments. For each question  $q$  we split  $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}^{(q)}$  into a 60/40 train/test split (repeated with 5 different seeds to get error bars) and use the train split for fine-tuning to the given question and the test split for evaluation.

Evaluated methods. Next, we briefly describe the different evaluated methods. Table 1 shows an overview of which datasets are used for each method/variant:

Baseline: this is the default model only trained on  $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}$ , (e.g., Vamvas and Senrich [2020]).

True Labels: this variant assumes that all true labels for the train split of  $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}^{(q)}$  are available and thus performs no active learning. This is the case  $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{MInf}}^{(q)} = \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}^{(q)}$  for each  $q$  in the test set.

SQBC: we apply our active learning approach to get the most informative samples  $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{Minf}}^{(q)}$

CAL: we apply Contrastive Active Learning (CAL, Margatina et al. [2021]) to get the most informative samples  $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{MInf}}^{(q)}$ .

Random: as an ablation, we randomly select the most informative samples  $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{Minf}}^{(q)}$

Note that the "+"Synth" variants additionally fine-tune the model on the synthetic data  $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{synth}}^{(q)}$

Evaluation. Our main results report the average F1 score over 10 selected questions from the test dataset evaluated on the comments from the test split. The error bars represent the average standard deviation over the 10 questions for 5 runs with different seeds. More detailed results per question are shown in Appendix E.

# 4.3 Experiments

For each question proposed in Section 1, we designed a specific experiment to test the impact of LLM-generated synthetic data augmentation for stance detection in online political discussions:

- Q1: The two models Baseline and True Labels are augmented with synthetic data of varying sizes.  
- Q2: We compare the 3 active learning strategies to determine the most informative samples: CAL, SQBC and Random. To study reduction in labelling effort, we define 4 different budgets of data samples (75%, 50%, 25% and 10% of  $\mathcal{D}_{\text {test }}^{(q)}$ ) to be selected for manual labelling. These variants are also compared against Baseline and True Labels.  
- Q3: We combine fine-tuning with the synthetic data (Q1) and the (manually labelled) most informative samples (Q2). We apply the same testing procedure as in Q2, where we test the performance for different labelling budgets, while also comparing to Baseline and True Labels.

# 4.5 Interpreting the role of the synthetic data

The synthetic data is at the core of our work. To get a better understanding we analyse the role of synthetic data when using it for data augmentation and for the selection of the most informative samples. To study whether the synthetic data matches the real data, we visualize embeddings of the synthetic data together with the embeddings of the real world data using T-SNE projection [van der Maaten and Hinton, 2008] in Figures 5, and 6. To gain insights into the selection mechanism for different query strategies, we add the T-SNE embeddings of the selected samples in Figures 8 and 7. Again, we discuss our insights along the questions Q1, Q2 and Q3.

Q1: The synthetic data matches the distribution of the real world data. We visualize the positive and negative synthetic data samples together with the means (cluster centers) of the real world data in Figures 5 and 6. We observe that the synthetic data extends the real world data, which we believe is the reason why fine-tuning with synthetic data is effective in online political discussions. Also, the larger the synthetic dataset size, the more the synthetic data matches the distribution of the real world data. Furthermore, the positive and negative samples are well separated, which we attribute to having pre-trained the BERT-model on  $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}$  of the X-Stance dataset, providing prior knowledge about the stance detection task.

Q2: SQBC selects samples that are not contained in the synthetic data distribution. We visualize the selected samples of the SQBC and compare them to the selected samples by CAL and Random in Figures 7 and 8. We see that SQBC selects the unlabelled samples that are mostly in between the two classes of the synthetic data. This is the expected behaviour since we select the samples where the classification score is ambiguous. For random selection, the range of selected samples is broad: some similar samples between the two classes like SQBC are selected, but also within class samples that are not covered by the synthetic data set. This explains why random selection works well with a large synthetic dataset, since it further extends the decision boundary of the model. Finally, CAL selects samples similar to SQBC, but mostly tends to select samples from only one class, resulting in worse performance.

Q3: The synthetic data extends the unlabelled real data, while the most informative samples improve classification by selecting samples not well covered by the synthetic data. Combining the insights from Q1 and Q2, we see that the synthetic data effectively extends the real world data thus providing a better estimate of the underlying distribution. When adding the most informative samples from the active learning methods, we add samples that are not in the more general synthetic data distribution thus extending the decision boundary of the classifier. We argue that this is why the combination of most informative samples and synthetic samples is so effective.

# 5 Discussion

Limitations and future work. One limitation of our approach is the fact that we have to fine-tune a separate model for each question. While this leads to excellent results, a common approach is to fine-tune a single (and thus more general) model for several questions (like Baseline). Having observed that the synthetic data distribution differs (sometimes greatly) for each question, we feel that the extra training is justifiable and necessary. The computing time is not a concern in our case, since fine-tuning the BERT model takes less than a minute even with a synthetic dataset size of  $M = 1000$  on a reasonable GPU (NVIDIA A100). Future work, could study whether the underlying distribution of the synthetic data can serve as a proxy to combine real unlabelled data from different questions

with similar (compatible) underlying distributions. This might enable us to train capable general stance detection models.

Conclusion. In this work, we presented how to improve stance detection models for online political discussions utilizing LLM-generated synthetic data: (i) we showed that fine-tuning with synthetic data related to the question improves the performance of the stance detection model. After analysing the embeddings of the synthetic data, we attribute this to the LLM-generated synthetic data aligning well with the real data for the given question. (ii) We reduce the labelling effort by selecting the most informative samples informed by synthetic data with SQBC. The synthetic data are separated by construction into the positive and negative classes, which makes finding the most informative samples more straightforward, since we can count the labels of the k-nearest synthetic neighbours to determine classification ambiguity. (iii) Fine-tuning with synthetic data can be further enhanced by adding the most informative samples, outperforming the method that uses all true labels. Concluding, our findings demonstrate the potential of synthetic data to significantly improve the efficiency and effectiveness of stance detection in online political discussions.
