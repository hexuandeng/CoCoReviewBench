# Abstract

A recent line of work shows that notions of multigroup fairness imply surprisingly strong notions of omniprediction: loss minimization guarantees that apply not just for a specific loss function, but for any loss belonging to a large family of losses [GKR+22, GHK+23]. While prior work has derived various notions of omniprediction from multigroup fairness guarantees of varying strength, it was unknown whether the connection goes in both directions.

In this work, we answer this question in the affirmative, establishing equivalences between notions of multicalibration and omniprediction. The new definitions that hold the key to this equivalence are new notions of swap omniprediction, which are inspired by swap regret in online learning [FV99, BM07a]. We show that these can be characterized exactly by a strengthening of multicalibration that we refer to as swap multicalibration. One can go from standard to swap multicalibration by a simple discretization; moreover all known algorithms for standard multicalibration in fact give swap multicalibration. In the context of omniprediction though, introducing the notion of swapping results in provably stronger notions, which require a predictor to minimize expected loss at least as well as an adaptive adversary who can choose both the loss function and hypothesis based on the value predicted by the predictor.

Building on these characterizations, we paint a complete picture of the relationship between the various omniprediction notions in the literature by establishing implications and separations between them. Our work deepens our understanding of the connections between multigroup fairness, loss minimization and outcome indistinguishability and establishes new connections to classic notions in online learning.

# 1 Introduction

A number of striking connections between multigroup fairness, indistinguishability and loss minimization have emerged recently. The fairness notions at the heart of these connections are multiaccuracy and multicalibration, which were introduced in the work of [HKRR18], see also [KMR17, KNRW17]. These notions apply to a predictor that assign probabilities to points in a domain. They respectively require the statistical guarantees of accuracy and calibration to hold even conditioned on membership in one of a specified and potentially huge set of subgroups of the domain. This set of subgroups is denoted by  $\mathcal{C}$ , and is assumed to admit succinct representations, such as a bounded size decision tree or a neural net over the domain. There is no reference to loss functions, which are central to the loss minimization paradigm in machine learning.

Yet, it turns out that are deep connections between these notions and loss minimization. This was first realized in the work of  $\left[\mathrm{GKR}^{+}22\right]$ , who introduced the notion of (standard) omniprediction. They define an  $(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C})$ -omnipredictor to be a predictor whose predictions are good enough to compete against the best hypothesis from a class  $\mathcal{C}$ , for any loss function from a family  $\mathcal{L}$ . The class  $\mathcal{C}$  now serves as a benchmark hypothesis class to compare against. The ability to give guarantees for any loss function from a rich class sets this apart from standard loss minimization guarantees, since the predictor can be trained on the dataset once and for all without having to know the specific target loss in advance, beyond the fact that it belongs to  $\mathcal{L}$ . One could use the same predictor to make optimal decisions relative to  $\mathcal{C}$  for every loss in  $\mathcal{L}$ . This is in contrast to the standard practice of optimizing over  $\mathcal{C}$  separately for each loss function.<sup>1</sup>

This raises the question of whether one can efficiently learn omnipredictors for rich classes of loss functions. This was answered affirmatively by  $\left[\mathrm{GKR}^{+}22\right]$ , who show that a  $\mathcal{C}$ -multicalibrated predictor is an  $(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cvx}},\mathcal{C})$ -omnipredictor where  $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cvx}}$  is the family of all convex, Lipschitz loss functions that satisfy some mild boundedness conditions. [HKRR18] showed that one can efficiently learn a  $\mathcal{C}$ -multicalibrated predictor efficiently, given oracle access to a weak agnostic learner for  $\mathcal{C}$ .

Intuitively, omniprediction suggests that a  $\mathcal{C}$ -multicalibrated predictor is indistinguishable from the Bayes optimal predictor as far as convex loss minimization over  $\mathcal{C}$  is concerned. This was formalized by the notion of  $(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C})$ -loss outcome indistinguishability or  $(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C})$ -loss OI for short, introduced in the work of  $\mathrm{[GHK^{+}23]}$ , inspired by the elegant outcome indistinguishability framework of  $\mathrm{[DKR^{+}21]}$ . It requires that labels simulated according to predictions of the predictor be indistinguishable from real labels to certain tests derived from the loss family  $\mathcal{L}$  and hypothesis family  $\mathcal{C}$ .  $\mathrm{[GHK^{+}23]}$  showed that  $(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C})$ -loss OI is a provably stronger notion than being an  $(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C})$ -omnipredictor. They show it is implied by a multigroup fairness condition they call calibrated multiaccuracy, but for a class of hypotheses  $\mathcal{C}'$  that may depend on both  $\mathcal{L}$  and  $\mathcal{C}$ . For a given hypothesis class  $\mathcal{C}$ , calibrated multiaccuracy lies in between multiaccuracy and multicalibration.

We will use the umbrella term omniprediction to refer to such notions that give guarantees for a rich class of loss functions  $\mathcal{L}$ , while only learning a single predictor at training time. This will include the notions of  $(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C})$ -omniprediction from  $\mathrm{[GKR^{+}22]}$ ,  $(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C})$ -loss OI from  $\mathrm{[GHK^{+}23]}$ , and two additional notions that we will introduce in this work. To summarize the state of the art, we knew that various notions of omniprediction can be derived from multigroup fairness guarantees of varying strength. What was unknown was whether this connection goes both ways:

Is multicalibration (or some other multigroup fairness condition) necessary for notions of om-

# niprediction?

A closely related question stems from the observation that multigroup fairness notions above do not explicitly reference loss functions in their formulation. Despite this, can we frame a loss minimization problem whose solution is guaranteed to give the desired multigroup fairness guarantee? Such a result was known for multiaccuracy via the work of  $\left[\mathrm{GHK}^{+}23\right]$ , which showed that the standard convex program used for any generalized linear model (GLM) results in a multiaccurate predictor. But multiaccuracy in itself is a rather weak fairness guarantee, and is not known to guarantee any interesting form of omniprediction. This raises the question:

Can we capture rich multigroup fairness notions like multicalibration in the language of loss minimization?

In this work, we answer the above questions in the affirmative, establishing equivalences between notions of multicalibration and omniprediction. The main conceptual contribution that facilitates this equivalence are the notions of swap loss minimization, swap omniprediction and swap loss-outcome indistinguishability which strengthen existing notions such as agnostic learning, omniprediction and loss-outcome indistinguishability. Our notion is inspired by the notion of swap regret in the online learning literature [FV98, BM07a]. Our main results present exact characterizations of swap analogues of omniprediction and loss OI in terms of a strengthening of standard multicalibration that we call swap multicalibration. We paint a complete picture of the relationships between various omniprediction notions by establishing implications and separations between them. We next describe each of these contributions in detail.

# 1.1 Swap loss minimization and swap omniprediction

We work in the setting of supervised learning where there is an underlying distribution  $\mathcal{D} = (\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{*})$  on points  $\mathbf{x}\in \mathcal{X}$  and  $\mathbf{y}^*\in \{0,1\}$ . A predictor is a function  $\tilde{p}:\mathcal{X}\to [0,1]$  which given a point  $x\in \mathcal{X}$  gives an estimate  $\tilde{p} (x)$  of the probability that the label is 1. Following the outcome indistinguishability framework of  $[\mathrm{DKR}^{+}21]$ , we associate the predictor with the random variable  $\tilde{\mathbf{y}}$  where  $\operatorname *{Pr}[\tilde{\mathbf{y}} = 1|x] = \tilde{p} (x)$ . We view  $\tilde{\mathbf{y}}$  as the predictor's simulation of nature, whereas nature itself corresponds to the true labels  $\mathbf{y}^*$ .

We will consider loss functions  $\ell : \{0,1\} \times \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ , which take a binary label  $y$  and a real-valued action  $t$  and assign to it a loss value. Two common examples are the squared loss  $\ell_2(y,t) = (y - t)^2$  and logistic loss  $\ell_{\lg}(y,t) = \log(1 + \exp((1 - 2y)t))$ . All notions of loss minimization with respect to a hypothesis class  $\mathcal{C} = \{c : \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}\}$  can be viewed as a game with two participants:

- The Predictor  $\mathcal{P}$  who aims to minimize loss based on the predictions of  $\tilde{p}$ , which they believe to be a good (enough) predictor of nature.  
An Adversary  $\mathcal{A}$  who aims to minimize loss using a hypothesis  $c\in \mathcal{C}$

Given a loss function  $\ell$  and a prediction  $\tilde{p}(x) = v$ , since the predictor believes that the labels will be distributed according to  $\tilde{\mathbf{y}} \sim \mathrm{Ber}(v)$ , the Bernoulli distribution with parameter  $v$ , they can compute an action  $k_{\ell}(v) \in \mathbb{R}$  which minimizes the expected loss. For common losses, this post-processing function is simple to compute: for instance  $k_{\ell_2}(v) = v$ , whereas  $k_{\ell_{\lg}}(v) = \log(v / (1 - v))$ . With this setup in hand, we present four models of agnostic loss minimization of increasing strength. The first is the classic notion of agnostic learning, the other three are new notions that we introduce

in this work. For simplicity of exposition, we present the 0-error version of these definitions, the  $\delta$ -error versions will allow some slack in the conditions.

Agnostic Learning: We say that  $\tilde{p}$  is an  $(\ell, \mathcal{C})$ -agnostic learner if the predictor can do as well as the adversary who takes actions based on hypotheses from  $\mathcal{C}$ , in terms of the expected loss  $\ell$  under the distribution  $\mathcal{D}$ . Formally, we require that

$$
\mathbf {E} [ \ell (\mathbf {y}, k _ {\ell} (\tilde {p} (\mathbf {x})) \leq \min  _ {c \in \mathcal {C}} \mathbf {E} [ \ell (\mathbf {y}, c (\mathbf {x})) ]
$$

Swap agnostic Learning: Consider the model where  $(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{*})$  is drawn from  $\mathcal{D}$ . The model makes its prediction  $\mathbf{v} = \tilde{p} (\mathbf{x})$ , this prediction  $\mathbf{v}$  is shown to the adversary. The adversary then picks a hypothesis  $c_{\mathbf{v}}\in \mathcal{C}$ . Their best strategy is to pick for each  $v\in \mathrm{Im}(\tilde{p})$  the hypothesis

$$
c _ {v} = \underset {c \in \mathcal {C}} {\arg \min} \mathbf {E} [ \ell (\mathbf {y}, c (\mathbf {x})) | \tilde {p} (\mathbf {x}) = v ]
$$

We say  $\tilde{p}$  is an  $(\ell, \mathcal{C})$ -swap agnostic learner if the predictor can still do better than the adversary. In other words, for any collection  $\{c_v \in \mathcal{C}\}_{v \in \mathrm{Im}(\tilde{p})}$  of hypothesis that represent the strategy of the adversary, writing  $\mathbf{v} = \tilde{p}(\mathbf{x})$  we have

$$
\mathbf {E} [ \ell (\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, k _ {\ell} (\mathbf {v}) ] \leq \mathbf {E} [ \ell (\mathbf {y}, c _ {\mathbf {v}} (\mathbf {x}) ]
$$

This is clearly a strengthening of the standard notion of agnostic learning where we require the predictor compete with the single best hypothesis  $c \in \mathcal{C}$ . Indeed, it seems a much more stringent condition. Provided the class  $\mathcal{C}$  contains all constant functions, the adversary can always imitate the predictor and choose  $c_{\mathbf{v}} = k_{\ell}(\mathbf{v})$  to be the same constant as the predictor, and hence ensure that their loss is no worse than  $\mathcal{P}$ . For such  $\mathcal{C}$ , swap agnostic learning requires that imitation is indeed the best strategy for  $\mathcal{A}$ .

Swap Omniprediction: We now consider a family  $\mathcal{L}$  of loss functions. As before,  $(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{*})$  is drawn from  $\mathcal{D}$ , the prediction  $\mathbf{v} = \tilde{p} (\mathbf{x})$  is shown to the adversary. The adversary then picks a loss function  $\ell_{\mathbf{v}}\in \mathcal{L}$  and a hypothesis  $c_{\mathbf{v}}\in \mathcal{C}$ . We compare the loss measured under  $\ell_{\mathbf{v}}$  when the predictor takes the action  $k(\mathbf{v}) = k_{\ell_{\mathbf{v}}}(\mathbf{v})$  and the adversary takes the action  $c_{\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{x})$ . We say that  $\tilde{p}$  is an  $(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C})$ -swap omnipredictor if it holds that for every strategy  $\{\ell_v\in \mathcal{L},c_v\in \mathcal{C}\}_{v\in \mathrm{Im}(\tilde{p})}$  of the adversary, we have

$$
\mathbf {E} [ \ell_ {\mathbf {v}} (\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, k _ {\ell_ {\mathbf {v}}} (\mathbf {v})) ] \leq \mathbf {E} [ \ell_ {\mathbf {v}} (\mathbf {y}, c _ {\mathbf {v}} (\mathbf {x})) ] \tag {1}
$$

This implies swap-agnostic learning for every  $\ell \in \mathcal{L}$ , since the adversary can choose the loss function  $\ell$  for all  $v$ , and only vary  $c_{\mathbf{v}}$ . It also implies the standard notion of omniprediction from  $\left[\mathrm{GKR}^{+}22\right]$  which is equivalent to requiring  $(\ell, \mathcal{C})$ -agnostic learning for every  $\ell \in \mathcal{L}$ .

Swap loss outcome-indistinguishability: As before,  $(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{*})$  is drawn from  $\mathcal{D}$ , and the prediction  $\mathbf{v} = \tilde{p} (\mathbf{x})$  is shown to the adversary, who then picks a loss function  $\ell_{\mathbf{v}}\in \mathcal{L}$  and a hypothesis  $c_{\mathbf{v}}\in \mathcal{C}$ . This defines a statistical test

$$
u _ {\mathbf {v}} (\mathbf {x}, \mathbf {y}) = \ell_ {\mathbf {v}} (\mathbf {y}, k _ {\ell_ {\mathbf {v}}} (\mathbf {v})) - \ell_ {\mathbf {v}} (\mathbf {y}, c _ {\mathbf {v}} (\mathbf {x}))
$$

which compares the losses suffered by the predictor's and adversary's actions. Inspired by the loss OI paradigm [DKR $^{+}$ 21, GHK $^{+}$ 23], we require that the expectation of  $u_{\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y})$  is the same whether the labels are  $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{y}^*$  drawn according to nature, or  $\mathbf{y} = \tilde{\mathbf{y}}$  drawn according to the predictors simulation. Formally, the predictor  $\tilde{p}$  is  $(\mathcal{L}, \mathcal{C})$ -swap loss OI if for every strategy  $\{\ell_v \in \mathcal{L}, c_v \in \mathcal{C}\}_{v \in \mathrm{Im}(\tilde{p})}$  of the adversary,

$$
\mathbf {E} [ u _ {\mathbf {v}} (\mathbf {x}, \mathbf {y} ^ {*}) ] = \mathbf {E} [ u _ {\mathbf {v}} (\mathbf {x}, \tilde {\mathbf {y}}) ]. \tag {2}
$$

In the standard notion of loss OI from  $\mathrm{[GHK^{+}23]}$ ,  $\ell, c$  are fixed independent of  $\mathbf{v}$ , thus swap loss OI is a stronger notion. To see that this is indeed a strengthening of swap omniprediction, observe that when labels are generated by  $\tilde{\mathbf{y}} \sim \mathrm{Ber}(\mathbf{v})$ ,  $k_{\ell_{\mathbf{v}}}(\mathbf{v})$  is indeed the optimal action. So we have

$$
\mathbf {E} \left[ u _ {\mathbf {v}} (\mathbf {x}, \tilde {\mathbf {y}}) \right] \leq 0 \tag {3}
$$

since the LHS is negative conditioned on every value of  $\mathbf{v}$ . Assuming swap-loss OI holds, this implies that

$$
\mathbf {E} \left[ u _ {\mathbf {v}} \left(\mathbf {x}, \mathbf {y} ^ {*}\right) \right] \leq 0 \tag {4}
$$

But this inequality is equivalent to Equation (1) defining swap omniprediction.

# 1.2 Swap multicalibration

As a first step towards a characterization of these notions of omniprediction, we consider a technical strengthening of the multicalibration that we call swap multicalibration. The standard definition of  $(\mathcal{C},\alpha)$ -multicalibration where  $\mathcal{C} = \{c:\mathcal{X}\to \mathbb{R}\}$  requires that

$$
\max _ {c \in \mathcal {C}} \underset {\mathbf {v}} {\mathbf {E}} | \mathbf {E} [ c (\mathbf {x}) (\mathbf {y} - \mathbf {v}) | \tilde {p} (\mathbf {x}) = \mathbf {v} ] | \leq \alpha .
$$

Swap multicalibration interchanges the order of max and expectation, requiring that

$$
\underset {\textbf {v} \textit {c} \in \mathcal {C}} {\mathbf {E}} \max _ | c (\mathbf {x}) (\mathbf {y} - \mathbf {v}) | \tilde {p} (\mathbf {x}) = \mathbf {v} ] | \leq \alpha .
$$

Swap multicalibration allows an auditor to use a different choice of  $c_{\mathbf{v}} \in \mathcal{C}$  to audit our predictor, conditioned on each value  $\mathbf{v}$  of the predictions. In this sense, it is reminiscent of our definitions of swap loss minimization. But the key difference is that swap multicalibration is not a significant strengthening of the standard definition of multicalibration. We present two arguments in favor of this view:

- Known algorithms for achieving standard multicalibration [HKRR18, GKR $^{+}$ 22] actually give swap multicalibration. They ensure multicalibration by conditioning on each prediction value, and then using a weak agnostic learner for  $\mathcal{C}$  to audit for a violation of the multicalibration condition. This naturally yields swap multicalibration. Thus the same primitive of weak agnostic learning [BLM01] needed for standard multicalibration also suffices for swap multicalibration.  
- We present a simple transformation that converts any  $(\mathcal{C},\alpha)$ -multicalibrated predictor into one that is  $(\mathcal{C},\alpha^{1/3})$ -swap multicalibrated, and at  $\ell_{\infty}$  distance  $\alpha^{1/3}$  from the original predictor.

# 1.3 Characterizing notions of swap omniprediction

Recall that a loss function is a function  $\ell : \{0,1\} \times \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ . A loss function is nice if it is Lipschitz and satisfies some mild boundedness conditions. The formal definition (Definition 3.1) appears in Section 3.  $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cvx}}$  is the subset all nice loss functions that are convex in their second argument.

Our first main result (Theorem 6.1) shows the following equivalence between swap notions of multicalibration, omniprediction and agnostic learning.

Theorem 1 (Informal statement of Theorem 6.1). For any hypothesis class  $\mathcal{C}$  and predictor  $\tilde{p}:\mathcal{X}\to \{0,1\}$ , the following notions are equivalent:

-  $\tilde{p}$  is  $\mathcal{C}$ -swap multicalibrated.  
-  $\tilde{p}$  is an  $(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cvx}},\mathcal{C})$  -swap omnipredictor.  
-  $\tilde{p}$  is an  $(\ell_2,\mathcal{C})$  -swap agnostic learner.

This strengthens the connection between multicalibration and omniprediction discovered in  $\mathrm{[GKR^{+}22]}$ . We start from a multicalibration assumption of similar strength, and show an equivalence to a stronger omniprediction guarantee. The equivalence to swap agnostic  $\ell_2$ -loss minimization shows that the problem of finding a predictor satisfying multicalibration can be indeed formulated as a loss minimization problem.

In order to state our next result, we recall the following definitions from  $\left[\mathrm{GHK}^{+}23\right]$ .

Definition 1.1. [GHK+23] For a loss function  $\ell : \{0,1\} \times \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ , define  $\partial \ell : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$  as

$$
\partial \ell (t) = \ell (1, t) - \ell (0, t).
$$

For a family of loss functions  $\mathcal{L}$  and a hypothesis class  $\mathcal{C}$ , let  $\partial \mathcal{L} = \{\partial \ell\}_{\ell \in \mathcal{L}}$  and  $\partial \mathcal{L} \circ \mathcal{C} = \{\partial \ell \circ c\}_{\ell \in \mathcal{L}, c \in \mathcal{C}}$ .

Our second main result Theorem 7.1 shows the equivalence of  $(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C})$ -swap loss OI for classes of nice loss functions  $\mathcal{L}$  containing the squared loss and  $\partial L\circ \mathcal{C}$ -swap multicalibration.

Theorem 2 (Informal statement of Theorem 7.1). Let  $\mathcal{L}$  be a family of nice loss functions containing the squared loss. For any hypothesis class  $\mathcal{C}$ , the following notions are equivalent:

-  $(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C})$  -swapresilientlossOI.  
-  $(\partial \mathcal{L} \circ \mathcal{C})$ -swap multicalibration.

The same class  $\partial L\circ \mathcal{C}$  appears in the work of  $[\mathrm{GHK}^{+}23]$ , where they show that  $(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C})$  loss OI is implied by calibration and  $\partial L\circ \mathcal{C}$ -multiaccuracy. We show that the stronger notion of  $\partial L\circ \mathcal{C}$ -multicalibration in fact gives an equivalence to  $(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C})$ -swap loss OI. Along the way, we prove that swap loss-OI for just the  $\ell_{2}$  loss implies calibration.

That loss-OI implies omniprediction (without swap) was proved in  $\left[\mathrm{GHK}^{+}23\right]$ , the remaining implications are proved in Section 4. Theorem 1.2.  $(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C})$  -Loss  $OI$  and  $(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C})$  -swap omniprediction are incomparable.

These separations are proved using the  $\ell_2$  and  $\ell_4$  loss, both of which are convex. This implies that Figure 1 captures all the implications that hold for all families  $\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C}$  (although other implications could hold for specific choices of  $\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C}$ ). # 2 Related work

Independent concurrent work. The notion of strict multicalibration was defined in independent work of Dwork, Lee, Lin and Tankala [DLLT23]. They connect this and other multigroup fairness definitions to various versions of the Szemeredi regularity lemma and its weaker version due to Frieze and Kannan. Their notions bears important similarities to swap multicalibration, but it is different. Like swap multicalibration, strict multicalibration involves switching the order of expectations and max. But they require a statistical closeness guarantee, whereas we only require a first order guarantee, that  $c(\mathbf{x})$  be uncorrelated with  $\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{v}$  conditioned on  $\mathbf{v}$ . For Boolean  $c$ , these notions are essentially equivalent. In the setting of real-valued functions  $\mathcal{C}$  however, they seem rather different. Swap multicalibration is closed under linear/convex combinations of  $\mathcal{C}$ , whereas strict multicalibration might not be. In the real-valued setting, strict multicalibration seems to require a stronger learning primitive than weak agnostic learner for  $\mathcal{C}$ , which suffices for swap multicalibration. The analogous statistical distance notion of multiaccuracy is at least as powerful as the notion of hypothesis OI for arbitrary loss families  $\mathcal{L}$ , which is known to require weak learning for  $\partial \mathcal{L} \circ \mathcal{C}$  [GHK⁺ 23, Theorem 4.9].

The independent work of Globus-Harris, Harrison, Kearns, Roth and Sorrell  $\left[\mathrm{GHHK}^{+}23\right]$  relates multicalibration to real-valued boosting or minimize  $\ell_{2}$  loss. The implication that  $(\ell_{2},\mathcal{C})$  swap-agnostic learning implies multicalibration follows from Part(1) of Theorem 3.2 in their paper. They prove that a violation of multicalibration leads to a better strategy for the adversary in the  $(\ell_{2},\mathcal{C})$ -swap minimization game. They do not consider the notion of swap multicalibration, so their result is not a tight characterization unlike our equivalence of Items (1) and (3) in Theorem 6.1.

Regret minimization and multigroup fairness. Our notions of swap loss minimization and swap omniprediction are inspired by notions of swap regret minimization in online learning [FV98,

FV99, BM07a]. The classic results of [FV98, FV99] relate calibration and swap regret, whereas [BM07a] show a generic way to convert a low external regret algorithm to ones with low internal and swap regret. The study of online learning and regret minimization is extensive, with deep connections to calibration and equilibria in game theory, we refer the readers to [CBL06, BM07b] for comprehensive surveys.

Recent work has established rich connections between online learning and regret minimization on one hand, and multigroup fairness notions on the other: multicalibration in the online setting has been considered in the work of  $\left[\mathrm{GJN}^{+}22\right]$ , while [BL20, GJKR18] relate multigroup fairness to questions in online learning. Our work contributes a new facet to these connections: it introduces new notions motivated by the regret minimization literature to agnostic loss minimization and omniprediction, and shows that these new notions have tight characterizations in terms of multigroup fairness notions.

Multicalibration. The notions of mutliaccuracy and multicalibration were introduced in the work of [HKRR18], see also [KMR17, KNRW17]. The notion has been extended to several other settings including multiclass predictions  $\left[\mathrm{DKR}^{+}22\right]$ , real-valued labels  $\left[\mathrm{GJN}^{+}22, \mathrm{JLP}^{+}21\right]$ , online learning  $\left[\mathrm{GJN}^{+}22\right]$  and importance weights [GRSW22]. Alternate definitions and extensions of the standard notions of multicalibration have been proposed in  $\left[\mathrm{GKR}^{+}22, \mathrm{GKSZ}22, \mathrm{DDZ}23, \mathrm{DLLT}23\right]$ . Multicalibration has also proved to have unexpected connections to many other domains. We have discussed the connections to loss minimization and indistinguishability. The work of  $\left[\mathrm{KKG}^{+}22\right]$  shows an elegant application to domain adaptation. Connections to boosting are explored in the work of  $\left[\mathrm{GKR}^{+}22, \mathrm{GHHK}^{+}23\right]$ .

# 3 Preliminaries

We consider the problem of binary classification over a domain  $\mathcal{X}$ . We are given samples  $(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{*})$  from nature's distribution where  $\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{X}$  and  $\mathbf{y}^{*} \in \{0,1\}$ , and our goal is to predict the label  $\mathbf{y}^{*}$  given  $\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{X}$ . A predictor is a function  $\tilde{p}: \mathcal{X} \to [0,1]$ , where  $\tilde{p}(x)$  represents an estimate for  $\operatorname{Pr}[\mathbf{y}^{*} = 1|\mathbf{x} = x]$ . Given a predictor  $\tilde{p}$ , we associate it with the random variable  $\tilde{\mathbf{y}}$  where  $\mathbf{E}[\tilde{\mathbf{y}}|x] = \tilde{p}(\mathbf{x})$ . The variable  $\tilde{\mathbf{y}}$  can be viewed as  $\tilde{p}$ 's simulation of nature's label  $\mathbf{y}^{*}$ . We define the Bayes optimal predictor  $p^{*}(x) = \operatorname{Pr}[\mathbf{y}^{*} = 1|\mathbf{x} = x]$ . We will use  $\mathcal{D}$  to denote the joint distribution  $(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{*},\tilde{\mathbf{y}})$  where  $\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{y}^{*}|x] = p^{*}(x)$  and  $\mathbf{E}[\tilde{\mathbf{y}}|x] = \tilde{p}(x)$ . We will refer to the marginal distribution of  $(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{*})$  as nature's distribution and  $(\mathbf{x},\tilde{\mathbf{y}})$  as  $\tilde{p}$ 's simulated distribution or simply the simulation when the predictor  $\tilde{p}$  is clear from the context. While the joint distribution of  $(\mathbf{y}^{*},\tilde{\mathbf{y}})$  is not important to us, for simplicity we assume they are independent given  $\mathbf{x} = x$ .

We will sometimes view drawing a sample from  $\mathcal{D}$  in two steps: we first sample a prediction  $\mathbf{v} \in [0,1]$  according to the distribution of  $\tilde{p}(\mathbf{x})$  under  $\mathcal{D}$ . We then draw the random variables  $(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}^*, \tilde{\mathbf{y}})$  according to the conditional distribution  $\mathcal{D}|\tilde{p}(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{v}$ . Accordingly, let  $\mathcal{D}_{\tilde{p}}$  denote the distribution of  $\tilde{p}(\mathbf{x})$  where  $\mathbf{x} \sim \mathcal{D}$ . For each  $v \in \mathrm{Im}(f)$ , let  $\mathcal{D}|_v$  denote the conditional distribution  $\mathcal{D}|\tilde{p}(\mathbf{x}) = v$ . The distribution  $\mathcal{D}|_{\mathbf{v}}$  for  $\mathbf{v} \sim \mathcal{D}_{\tilde{p}}$  is just the distribution  $\mathcal{D}$ .

A concept class is a set of functions  $\mathcal{C}:\{c:\mathcal{X}\to \mathbb{R}\}$ . We assume that  $\mathcal{C}$  is closed under negation, and it contains the constant functions  $0,1$ . We define  $\| \mathcal{C}\|_{\infty} = \max |c(x)|$  over  $c\in \mathcal{C},x\in \mathcal{X}$ . We say that  $\mathcal{C}$  is bounded if  $\| c\|_{\infty}\leq 1$ . We define  $\operatorname {Lin}(\mathcal{C},W)$  to be all functions of the form

$$
h (x) = \sum_ {i} w _ {i} c _ {i} (x), \quad \sum_ {i} | w _ {i} | \leq W.
$$

Note that for bounded  $\mathcal{C}$ ,  $\| \mathrm{Lin}(\mathcal{C}, W) \|_{\infty} \leq W$ . We define  $\mathrm{Lin}(\mathcal{C})$  to be the set of all linear combinations with no restriction on the coefficients.

Loss functions. A loss function is a function  $\ell : \{0,1\} \times \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ , which assigns a real value  $\ell(y,t)$  to a pair of inputs where  $y \in \{0,1\}$  is a binary label, and we refer to  $t \in \mathbb{R}$  as the action. We say the loss function is convex if for  $y \in \{0,1\}$ ,  $\ell(y,t)$  is a convex function of  $t$ . By linearity, this convexity property holds for  $\ell(p,t)$  for all  $p$ .

We define the class of nice loss functions, which obey minimal boundedness and Lipschitzness conditions.

Definition 3.1. For a constant  $B > 0$ , a loss function is  $B$ -nice if there exists an interval  $I_{\ell} \subseteq \mathbb{R}$  such that the following conditions hold:

1. (Optimality) If  $\Pi_{\ell}:\mathbb{R}\to I_{\ell}$  denotes projection onto the interval  $I_{\ell}$ , then  $\ell (p,t)\leq \ell (p,\Pi_{\ell}(t))$  for all  $t\in \mathbb{R}$  and  $p\in [0,1]$ .  
2. (Lipschitzness) For  $y \in \{0,1\}$  and  $t \in I$ ,  $\ell(y,t)$  is 1-Lipschitz as a function of  $t$ .  
3. (Bounded derivative) For  $t \in I_{\ell}$ ,  $|\partial \ell(t)| \leq B$ .

The class of all  $B$ -nice loss functions by  $\mathcal{L}(B)$ . The subset of  $B$ -nice convex loss functions is denoted by  $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cvx}}(B)$ .

The class  $\mathcal{L}$  includes several commonly used loss functions including  $\ell_p$  loss defined by  $\ell_p(y,t) = |y - t|^p / p$  for  $p \geq 1$ . For the  $\ell_p$  losses where  $p \geq 1$ ,  $I_\ell = [0,1]$ .

The optimality property implies that we may assume  $k_{\ell}:[0,1] \to I_{\ell}$  since we do not increase the loss by projection onto  $I_{\ell}$ . Indeed, for convex functions, the natural choice for  $I_{\ell}$  is to take the interval  $[k_{\ell}(0),k_{\ell}(1)]$ . We formalize this in Lemma A.1 in Appendix A.2 where we show that for every convex loss function, it is possible to choose an interval of this form that satisfies optimality. One can then ensure the Lipschitz constant is bounded by a suitable rescaling, which is why we normalize by  $p$  for the  $\ell_p$  loss. We now take  $B = \max_{t\in I}|\partial \ell (t)|$ , so that  $B = 1 / p$  for the  $\ell_p$  loss.

The bounded derivatives condition is weaker than asking that  $\ell$  is bounded on the interval  $I$ , indeed it is invariant under shifting both  $\ell(0, t)$  and  $\ell(1, t)$  by some function of  $t$ . The bounded derivative condition implies that  $\ell$  is Lipschitz in its first argument, a property that will be useful. The proof of this lemma is in Appendix A.2.

Lemma 3.2. For every  $\ell \in \mathcal{L}(B)$  and  $t_0\in I_\ell$ , the function  $\ell (p,t_0)$  is  $B$ -Lipschitz as a function of  $p$ .

We recall the notion of optimal post-processing of predictions for a loss function  $\ell$  (see for instance  $[\mathrm{GKR}^{+}22, \mathrm{GHK}^{+}23]$ ). We extend  $\ell$  linearly to allow the first argument to take values in the range  $[0,1]$  as:

$$
\ell (p, t) = \underset {\mathbf {y} ^ {*} \sim \operatorname {B e r} (p)} {\mathbf {E}} [ \ell (\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, t) ] = p \ell (1, t) + (1 - p) \ell (0, t).
$$

Define the function  $k_{\ell}:[0,1]\to \mathbb{R}$  which specifies the action that minimizes  $\ell (p,t)$ . Formally,

$$
k _ {\ell} (p) = \underset {t \in \mathbb {R}} {\arg \min } \ell (p, t). \tag {5}
$$

If there are multiple optima, we break ties arbitrarily.

# 4 Swap agnostic learning and notions of omniprediction

In order to minimize expected loss under  $\mathcal{D}$ , we learn a function  $f: \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}$  which prescribes an action at every point  $x \in \mathcal{X}$ . We would like  $f$  to be as good as the best hypothesis from some reference hypothesis class  $\mathcal{H}$ , which consists of functions  $h: \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}$  that specify an action for each point. Formally, we say that  $f$  is an  $(\ell, \mathcal{H}, \delta)$ -agnostic learner if

$$
\mathbf {\Delta} _ {\mathcal {D}} ^ {\mathbf {E}} [ \ell (\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, f (\mathbf {x}) ] \leq \min _ {h \in \mathcal {H}} \mathbf {\Delta} _ {\mathcal {D}} ^ {\mathbf {E}} [ \ell (\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, h (\mathbf {x})) ] + \delta .
$$

We will typically consider  $\mathcal{H} = \operatorname{Lin}(\mathcal{C}, W)$  where  $\mathcal{C}$  is a bounded hypothesis class.

# 4.1 Swap agnostic learning

A stronger notion than agnostic learning is to allow for different choices based on the current action  $f(\mathbf{x})$ . This has the natural interpretation that we see the action  $f(\mathbf{x}) = t$  and then switch to the best model  $h = h_t \in \mathcal{H}$  for the conditional distribution  $\mathcal{D}|f(\mathbf{x}) = t$ . This is similar to the notion of swap-regret that is studied in online learning. As before, given a function  $f: \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}$ , we let  $\mathcal{D}_f$  denote the distribution of actions  $\mathbf{t} \in \mathbb{R}$  where  $\mathbf{t} = f(\mathbf{x})$  for  $\mathbf{x} \sim \mathcal{D}$ .

Definition 4.1. We say that  $f: \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}$  is an  $(\ell, \mathcal{H}, \delta)$ -swap agnostic learner if

$$
\mathbf {\underline {{E}}} _ {\mathcal {D}} [ \ell (\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, f (\mathbf {x})) ] \leq \mathbf {\underline {{E}}} _ {\mathbf {t} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {f}} \left[ \min _ {h \in \mathcal {H}} \mathbf {\underline {{E}}} _ {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {t}}} [ \ell (\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, h (\mathbf {x})) ] \right] + \delta .
$$

This is a strengthening of an  $(\ell, \mathcal{H}, \delta)$ -agnostic learner since

$$
\underset {\mathbf {t} \sim f _ {\mathcal {D}}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \min  _ {h \in \mathcal {H}} \underset {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {t}}} {\mathbf {E}} [ \ell (\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, h (\mathbf {x})) ] \right] \leq \min  _ {h \in \mathcal {H}} \underset {\mathcal {D}} {\mathbf {E}} [ \ell (\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, h (\mathbf {x})) ]. \tag {6}
$$

# 4.2 Swap Omniprediction

We now consider the omniprediction scenario introduced in  $\left[\mathrm{GKR}^{+}22\right]$  where the loss can come from a family  $\mathcal{L}$ . We first present the standard notion of omniprediction, which requires the function  $f = k_{\ell} \circ \tilde{p}$  is an  $(\ell, \mathcal{C}, \delta)$ -agnostic learner for every  $\ell \in \mathcal{L}$ .

Definition 4.2.  $[GKR^{+}22]$  The predictor  $\tilde{p}:\mathcal{X}\to [0,1]$  is an  $(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{H},\delta)$  omnipredictor if for every  $\ell \in \mathcal{L}$ ,

$$
\underset {\mathcal {D}} {\mathbf {E}} [ \ell (\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, k _ {\ell} (\tilde {p} (\mathbf {x})) ] \leq \min _ {h \in \mathcal {H}} \underset {\mathcal {D}} {\mathbf {E}} [ \ell (\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, h (\mathbf {x})) ] + \delta .
$$

We strengthen this notion to swap omniprediction, where both the choice of loss  $\ell \in \mathcal{L}$  and hypothesis  $h\in \mathcal{H}$  are allowed to depend on the prediction  $\tilde{p} (x) = v$  . Let  $\operatorname {Im}(\tilde{p})\subseteq [0,1]$  denote the set of values taken by  $\tilde{p} (x)$  . For each  $v\in \mathrm{Im}(\tilde{p})$  , we allow an adversarial choice of loss  $\ell_v\in \mathcal{L}$  . The omnipredictor responds with the action  $k(v) = k_{\ell_v}(v)$  . We compare this with the optimal choice of hypothesis  $h_v$  tailored to the loss  $\ell_v$  and the conditional distribution  $\mathcal{D}|\tilde{p} (\mathbf{x}) = v$

Definition 4.3. The predictor  $\tilde{p}$  is an  $(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{H},\delta)$ -swap omnipredictor if for any choice of loss functions  $\{\ell_v\in \mathcal{L}\}_{v\in \mathrm{Im}(\tilde{p})}$  and hypotheses  $\{h_v\in \mathcal{H}\}_{v\in \mathrm{Im}(\tilde{p})}$ , if we denote  $k(v) = k_{\ell_v}(v)$ , then

$$
\underset {\mathbf {v} \in \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \underset {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \ell (\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, k (\mathbf {v})) \right] \right] \leq \underset {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \underset {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \ell_ {\mathbf {v}} (\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, h _ {\mathbf {v}} (\mathbf {x})) \right] \right] + \delta .
$$

The special case where we use the same loss  $\ell$  for every  $v$  gives swap-agnostic learning for  $\ell$ .

Corollary 4.4. If  $\tilde{p}$  is an  $(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{H},\delta)$ -swap omnipredictor, it is an  $(\ell ,\mathcal{H},\delta)$ -swap agnostic learner for every  $\ell \in \mathcal{L}$ , hence it is an  $(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{H},\delta)$ -omnipredictor.

Proof. We let  $\ell_v = \ell$  for all  $v \in \operatorname{Im}(\tilde{p})$ , so that  $k(v) = k_{\ell}(v)$ . We pick the hypothesis

$$
h_{v} = \operatorname *{arg  min}_{h\in \mathcal{H}}\underset {\mathcal{D}|_{v}}{\mathbf{E}}\left[\ell (\mathbf{y}^{*},h(\mathbf{x}))\right]
$$

The swap omniprediction guarantee reduces to

$$
\underset {\mathbf {v} \in \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \right. \underset {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \ell (\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, k _ {\ell} (\mathbf {v})) \right] = \underset {\mathcal {D}} {\mathbf {E}} [ \ell (\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, k _ {\ell} (\tilde {p} (\mathbf {x}))) ] \leq \underset {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} {\mathbf {E}} \min  _ {h \in \mathcal {H}} \underset {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \ell (\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, h (\mathbf {x})) \right] + \delta .
$$

This implies that  $f = k_{\ell} \circ \tilde{p}$  is a swap agnostic learner for every  $\ell \in \mathcal{L}$  since we allow the choice of  $h$  to depend on  $\tilde{p}(\mathbf{x})$  which is more informative than  $f(\mathbf{x}) = k_{\ell}(\tilde{p}(\mathbf{x}))$ .

Thus swap omniprediction implies swap learning for every  $\ell$ , analogous to how standard omniprediction implies agnostic learning. However, swap omniprediction is stronger since we also allow the loss to be chosen adaptively to the prediction.

# 4.3 Swap Loss-OI

The notion of loss outcome indistinguishability or loss-OI for short was introduced in the recent work of  $\left[\mathrm{GHK}^{+}23\right]$  with the motivation of understanding omniprediction from the perspective of outcome indistinguishability  $\left[\mathrm{DKR}^{+}21\right]$ . Loss-OI gives a strengthening of omniprediction. It requires predictors  $\tilde{p}$  to fool a family  $\mathcal{U}$  of statistical tests  $u: \mathcal{X} \times [0,1] \times \{0,1\}$  that take a point  $\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{X}$ , a prediction  $\tilde{p}(\mathbf{x}) \in [0,1]$  and a label  $\mathbf{y} \in \{0,1\}$  as their arguments. The goal is distinguish between the scenarios where  $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{y}^*$  is generated by nature versus where  $\mathbf{y} = \tilde{\mathbf{y}}$  is a simulation of nature according to the predictor  $\tilde{p}$ . Formally, we require that for every  $u \in \mathcal{U}$ ,

$$
\mathbf {\delta} _ {\mathcal {D}} [ u (\mathbf {x}, \tilde {p} (\mathbf {x}), \mathbf {y} ^ {*}) ] \approx_ {\varepsilon} \mathbf {\delta} _ {\mathcal {D}} [ u (\mathbf {x}, \tilde {p} (\mathbf {x}), \tilde {\mathbf {y}}) ].
$$

Loss OI specializes this to a specific family of tests arising in the analysis of omnipredictors.

Definition 4.5.  $[GHK^{+}23]$  For a loss family  $\mathcal{L} = \{\ell : \{0,1\} \times \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\}$  and a concept class  $\mathcal{C} = \{c : \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}\}$ , define the family of tests  $\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C}) = \{u_{\ell,c}\}_{\ell \in \mathcal{L}, c \in \mathcal{C}}$  where

$$
u _ {\ell , c} (x, v, y) = \ell \left(y, k _ {\ell} (v)\right) - \ell \left(y, c (x)\right). \tag {7}
$$

We say that a predictor  $\tilde{p}:\mathcal{X}\to [0,1]$  is  $(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C},\varepsilon)$ -loss  $OI$  if for every  $u\in \mathcal{U}(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C})$ , it holds that

$$
\left| \underset {(\mathbf {x}, \mathbf {y} ^ {*}) \sim \mathcal {D}} {\mathbf {E}} [ u (\mathbf {x}, \tilde {p} (\mathbf {x}), \mathbf {y} ^ {*}) ] - \underset {(\mathbf {x}, \tilde {\mathbf {y}}) \sim \mathcal {D} (\tilde {p})} {\mathbf {E}} [ u (\mathbf {x}, \tilde {p} (\mathbf {x}), \tilde {\mathbf {y}}) ] \right| \leq \varepsilon . \tag {8}
$$

$\left[\mathrm{GHK}^{+}23\right]$  show that loss-OI implies omniprediction (but that the converse is not always true).

Lemma 4.6. [GHK+23, Proposition 4.5] If the predictor  $\tilde{p}$  is  $(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C},\varepsilon)$ -loss  $OI$ , then it is an  $(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C},\varepsilon)$ -omnipredictor.

Next we introduce swap loss outcome-indistinguishability or swap loss-OI for short.

Definition 4.7. The predictor  $\tilde{p}$  satisfies  $(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C},\alpha)$ -swap loss  $OI$  if for any choice of loss functions  $\{\ell_v\in \mathcal{L}\}_{v\in \mathrm{Im}(\tilde{p})}$  and hypotheses  $\{h_v\in \mathcal{H}\}_{v\in \mathrm{Im}(\tilde{p})}$ , if we denote  $u_{v} = u_{\ell_{v},c_{v}}\in \mathcal{U}(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C})$ , then

$$
\underset {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} {\mathbf {E}} \left| \underset {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ u _ {\mathbf {v}} \left(\mathbf {x}, \mathbf {v}, \mathbf {y} ^ {*}\right) - u _ {\mathbf {v}} \left(\mathbf {x}, \mathbf {v}, \tilde {\mathbf {y}}\right) \right] \right| \leq \alpha .
$$

This notion generalizes both swap omniprediction and loss-OI simultaneously.

Lemma 4.8. If the predictor  $\tilde{p}$  satisfies  $(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C},\alpha)$ -swap loss  $OI$ , then

- it is an  $(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C},\alpha)$ -swap omnipredictor.  
- it is  $(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C},\alpha)$ -loss  $OI$ .

# 5 Swap multicalibration

We say that a predictor  $\tilde{p}:\mathcal{X}\to [0,1]$  is  $\varepsilon$ -calibrated if

$$
\mathsf {C E} _ {\mathcal {D}} (f) := \underset {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} {\mathbf {E}} \left| \underset {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} {\mathbf {E}} [ \mathbf {y} ^ {*} - \mathbf {v} ] \right| \leq \varepsilon .
$$

We refer to  $\mathsf{CE}_{\mathcal{D}}(\tilde{p})$  as the calibration error of  $\tilde{p}$ . We say  $\tilde{p}$  perfectly calibrated if  $\mathsf{CE}_{\mathcal{D}}(\tilde{p}) = 0$ .

Informally, multicalibration introduced in the work of [HKRR18] requires calibration to hold conditioned on every  $c \in \mathcal{C}$  for some family of sets/functions  $\mathcal{C}$ . The original paper only considered Boolean functions  $c$ , this was subsequently extended to real-valued functions in the work of [GKR+22]. The formulation below appears in [GHK+23].

Definition 5.1. [HKRR18, GHK+23] We say that the predictor  $\tilde{p}:\mathcal{X}\to [0,1]$  is  $(\mathcal{C},\alpha)$ -multicalibrated if for all  $c\in \mathcal{C}$

$$
\underset {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \left| \underset {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} {\mathbf {E}} [ c (\mathbf {x}) (\mathbf {y} - \mathbf {v}) ] \right| \right] \leq \alpha . \tag {9}
$$

We define the multicalibration error of  $\tilde{p}$  wrt  $\mathcal{C}$  under  $\mathcal{D}$  as

$$
\mathsf {M C E} _ {\mathcal {D}} (f, \mathcal {C}) = \max _ {c \in \mathcal {C}} \underset {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \left| \underset {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} {\mathbf {E}} [ c (\mathbf {x}) (\mathbf {y} - \mathbf {v}) ] \right| \right].
$$

When  $c:\mathcal{X}\to \{0,1\}$  is Boolean (and has sufficiently large measure), this definition says that conditioned on  $c(\mathbf{x}) = 1$ $\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{v}}[|\mathbf{E}_{\mathcal{D}|_{\mathbf{v}}} [y - \mathbf{v}]|]$  is small, which matches [HKRR18].

We define the stronger notion of swap multicalibration. This definition extends the pseudorandomness perspective on multicalibration developed by  $\mathrm{[DKR^{+}21,GKR^{+}22]}$ . It requires that for the typical prediction  $\mathbf{v}\sim \mathcal{D}_{\tilde{p}}$ , no hypothesis in  $\mathcal{C}$  achieves good correlation with the residual labels  $\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{v}$  under  $\mathcal{D}|_{\mathbf{v}}$ .

Definition 5.2. We say that the predictor  $\tilde{p}:\mathcal{X}\to [0,1]$  is  $(\mathcal{C},\alpha)$ -swap multicalibrated if

$$
\mathbf {E} _ {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} \left[ \max  _ {c \in \mathcal {C}} \left| \mathbf {E} _ {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} [ c (\mathbf {x}) (\mathbf {y} - \mathbf {v}) ] \right| \right] \leq \alpha . \tag {10}
$$

We define the swap multicalibration error of  $\tilde{p}$  wrt  $\mathcal{C}$  under  $\mathcal{D}$  as

$$
\mathsf {s M C E} _ {\mathcal {D}} (\tilde {p}, \mathcal {C}) = \underset {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \max _ {c \in \mathcal {C}} \left| \underset {c \in \mathcal {C}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ c (\mathbf {x}) (\mathbf {y} - \mathbf {v}) \right] \right| \right]
$$

To contrast the two notions, we refer to the notion from definition 5.1 as standard multicalibration. The difference between swap and standard multicalibration is in the order of quantifiers. The standard definition requires that for every  $c$ , the correlation  $|\mathbf{E}_{\mathcal{D}|\mathbf{v}}[c(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{v})]|$  achieved in small in expectation over  $\mathbf{v}\sim \mathcal{D}_{\tilde{p}}$ . Swap multicalibration considers the maximum of  $|\mathbf{E}_{\mathcal{D}|\mathbf{v}}[c(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{v})]|$  over all  $c\in \mathcal{C}$  for each fixing of  $\mathbf{v} = v$  and requires this to be small in expectation over  $\mathbf{v}\sim \mathcal{D}_{\tilde{p}}$ .

It follows that swap multicalibration implies standard multicalibration.

Lemma 5.3. We have

$$
\mathsf {M C E} _ {\mathcal {D}} (\tilde {p}, \mathcal {C}) \leq \mathsf {s M C E} _ {\mathcal {D}} (\tilde {p}, \mathcal {C}).
$$

Hence if  $\tilde{p}$  is  $(\mathcal{C},\alpha)$ -swap multicalibrated, then it is  $(\mathcal{C},\alpha)$ -multicalibrated.

# 5.1 From standard to swap multicalibration

To compare the notions, we define the following sets of predictions.

Definition 5.4. Let  $\beta \in [0,1]$ . Define

$$
\begin{array}{l} \operatorname {B a d} _ {\beta} (c, \tilde {p}) = \left\{v \in [ 0, 1 ]: \left| \mathbf {E} _ {\mathcal {D} | _ {v}} [ c (\mathbf {x}) (\mathbf {y} - v) ] \right| \geq \beta \right\} \\ \operatorname {B a d} _ {\beta} (\tilde {p}, \beta) = \left\{v \in [ 0, 1 ]: \exists c \in \mathcal {C} s. t. \left| \underset {\mathcal {D} | _ {v}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ c (\mathbf {x}) (\mathbf {y} - v) \right] \right| \geq \beta \right\}. \\ \end{array}
$$

Under standard multicalibration, we can bound the probability of  $\mathrm{Bad}_{\beta}(c,\tilde{p})$  under  $\mathcal{D}_{\tilde{p}}$  for each  $c\in \mathcal{C}$  by an application of Markov's inequality. The set  $\mathrm{Bad}_{\beta}(c,\tilde{p})$  depends on  $c$ , and different sets might be bad for different choices of  $c$ . One might hope to take a union bound over all  $\mathcal{C}$  to arrive at a single bad set  $\mathrm{Bad}_{\beta}(\tilde{p})$ , but if the set  $\mathcal{C}$  is large or infinite, then this is not possible. In contrast, swap multicalibration allows us to define a single set  $\mathrm{Bad}_{\beta}(\tilde{p})$  of predictions  $v\subseteq \operatorname {Im}(\tilde{p})$  where the correlation between some  $c\in \mathcal{C}$  and the residue  $\mathbf{y} - v$  can be large conditioned on  $\tilde{p} (\mathbf{x}) = v$ , so that this set has small probability under  $\mathcal{D}_{\tilde{p}}$ .

Lemma 5.5. If  $\tilde{p}$  is  $(\mathcal{C},\alpha)$ -multicalibrated, then for every  $c\in \mathcal{C}$ ,

$$
\operatorname * {P r} _ {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} [ \mathbf {v} \in \mathrm {B a d} _ {\beta} (c, \tilde {p}) ] \leq \frac {\alpha}{\beta}.
$$

If  $\tilde{p}$  is  $(\mathcal{C},\alpha)$ -swap multicalibrated, then

$$
\operatorname * {P r} _ {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} [ \mathbf {v} \in \mathrm {B a d} _ {\beta} (\tilde {p}) ] \leq \frac {\alpha}{\beta}.
$$

While swap multicalibration is nominally a stronger notion than standard multicalibration, it is not too different in terms of the computational complexity of achieving it. We present two arguments in favor of this view.

1. Any predictor  $\tilde{p}$  that guarantees standard multicalibration can be converted into a predictor  $\bar{p}$  that guarantees swap multicalibration, using a simple bucketing procedure. This causes some degradation in the error parameter, but the resulting predictor is close to  $\tilde{p}$  in  $\ell_{\infty}$ .  
2. Known algorithms for standard multicalibration [HKRR18, GKR $^{+}$ 22] are readily seen to guarantee swap multicalibration without any modification.

We show that starting from a  $(\mathcal{C},\alpha)$ -multicalibrated predictor  $\tilde{p}$ , by suitably discretizing its values, one can get a predictor that is close to  $\tilde{p}$  and  $(\mathcal{C},\alpha')$ -swap multicalibrated, at the cost of a some degradation in the value of  $\alpha$ .

Definition 5.6. Let  $\delta \in [0,1]$  so that  $m = 1 / \delta \in \mathbb{Z}$ . Define  $B_{j} = [(j - 1)\delta ,j\delta)$  for  $j\in [m - 1]$  and  $B_{m} = [1 - \delta ,1]$ . Define the predictor  $\bar{p}$  where for every  $x$  such that  $\tilde{p} (x)\in B_j$ ,  $\bar{p} (x) = j\delta$ .

For two functions  $f, g: \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}$ , define their  $\ell_{\infty}$  distance as  $\| f, g \|_{\infty} = \max_{x \in \mathcal{X}} |f(x) - g(x)|$ .

Theorem 5.7. Let  $\tilde{p}:\mathcal{X}\to [0,1]$  be a  $(\mathcal{C},\alpha)$ -multicalibrated. For all  $\delta \geq 0$  where  $1 / \delta \in \mathbb{Z}$ , the predictor  $\bar{p}$  is  $(\mathcal{C},2\sqrt{\alpha / \delta} +\delta)$ -swap multicalibrated, and  $\| f,\bar{p}\|_{\infty}\leq \delta$ .

While the bound of the theorem is valid for all  $\delta$ , the swap multicalibration guarantee is only meaningful when  $\delta \geq c\alpha$ . In particular, taking  $\delta = \alpha^{1/3}$  yields a predictor  $\bar{p}$  so that  $\| \bar{p}, \tilde{p} \|_{\infty} \leq \alpha^{1/3}$ ,  $\mathsf{sMCE}_{\mathcal{D}}(\bar{p}, \mathcal{C}) \leq \alpha^{1/3}$ .

Algorithms for multicalibration: Both the algorithms we are aware of for achieving standard multicalibration, the original [HKRR18] algorithm and the branching program based algorithm from  $\left[\mathrm{GKR}^{+}22\right]$ , are easily seen to give the stronger guarantee of swap multicalibration. Both algorithms work by conditioning on each (sufficiently likely) prediction  $v \in \operatorname{Im}(f)$ , and ensuring that no hypothesis  $c \in \mathcal{C}$  is sufficiently well correlated with  $\mathbf{y} - v$ . Indeed, this requires them to run the weak agnostic learner for  $\mathcal{C}$  separately for every  $v \in \operatorname{Im}(f)$ . But the upshot is that conditioned each  $v$ , they guarantee that  $\max_{c} |\mathbf{E}_{\mathcal{D}|v}[c(x)(\mathbf{y} - v)]|$  is small, rather than just guaranteeing the smallness in average for every  $c$ . The total probability of the unlikely predictions can be added to the swap multicalibration error.

# 6 Characterizing swap omniprediction

Our main result is an equivalence between swap multicalibration and swap omniprediction for the class of nice loss functions.

Theorem 6.1. Let  $\tilde{p}$  be a predictor,  $\mathcal{C}$  be a bounded hypothesis class and  $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cvx}}(B)$  be the class of  $B$ -nice convex loss functions. The following are equivalent:3

1.  $\tilde{p}$  is  $(\mathcal{C},\alpha_{1})$  -swap multicalibrated.  
2.  $\tilde{p}$  is an  $(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cVX}}(B),\mathrm{Lin}(\mathcal{C},W),O((W + B)\alpha_{2}))$  -swap omnipredictor, for all  $W\geq 1,B\geq 0$  
3.  $\tilde{p}$  is an  $(\ell_2,\mathrm{Lin}(\mathcal{C},2),\alpha_3)$ -swap agnostic learner.

In preparation for proving this, we first prove some preliminary results. We define a function  $\alpha : \mathrm{Im}(\tilde{p}) \to [-1, 1]$  which measures the maximum correlation between  $c \in \mathcal{C}$  and  $\mathbf{y} - v$ , conditioned on a prediction value  $v \in \mathrm{Im}(\tilde{p})$ . Let

$$
\alpha (v) = \left| \max  _ {c \in \mathcal {C}} \underset {\mathcal {D} | _ {v}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ c (\mathbf {x}) \left(\mathbf {y} ^ {*} - v\right) \right] \right|.
$$

$(\mathcal{C}, \alpha_0)$ -swap multicalibration can be written as

$$
\mathbf {E} _ {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} [ \alpha (\mathbf {v}) ] \leq \alpha_ {0}.
$$

We observe that swap multicalibration is closed under bounded linear combinations of  $\mathcal{C}$ , like with standard multicalibration.

Claim 6.2. 1. For every  $h \in \operatorname{Lin}(\mathcal{C}, W)$  and  $v \in \operatorname{Im}(\tilde{p})$ , we have

$$
\max  _ {h \in \operatorname {L i n} (\mathcal {C}, W)} \left| \mathbf {E} _ {\mathcal {D}} [ h (\mathbf {x}) (\mathbf {y} ^ {*} - v) | \tilde {p} (\mathbf {x}) = v ] \right| \leq W \alpha (v).
$$

2. Let  $p_v^* = \mathbf{E}[\mathbf{y}^* |\tilde{p} (\mathbf{x}) = v]$ . Then

$$
\left| p _ {v} ^ {*} - v \right| \leq \alpha (v). \tag {11}
$$

Claim (2) follows since  $1 \in \mathcal{C}$ .

We use the notation  $(\tilde{p}(\mathbf{x}), \mathbf{y}^*) = (v, y)$  to indicate  $\tilde{p}(\mathbf{x}) = v$  and  $\mathbf{y}^* = y$ .

Claim 6.3. For  $h \in \operatorname{Lin}(\mathcal{C}, w)$ ,  $v \in \operatorname{Im}(\tilde{p})$  and  $y \in \{0,1\}$ , define the following conditional expectations:

$$
\mu (h: v) = \mathbf {E} [ h (\mathbf {x}) | \tilde {p} (\mathbf {x}) = v ]
$$

$$
\mu (h: v, y) = \mathbf {E} [ h (\mathbf {x}) | (\tilde {p} (\mathbf {x}), \mathbf {y} ^ {*}) = (v, y) ].
$$

Then for each  $y\in \{0,1\}$

$$
\Pr [ \mathbf {y} ^ {*} = y | \tilde {p} (\mathbf {x}) = v ] \left| \mu (h: v, y) - \mu (h: v) \right| \leq (W + 1) \alpha (v). \tag {12}
$$

Proof. Recall that  $\operatorname{Cov}[\mathbf{y}^*, \mathbf{z}] = \mathbf{E}[\mathbf{y}^*\mathbf{z}] - \mathbf{E}[\mathbf{y}^*]\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{z}]$ . For any  $h \in \operatorname{Lin}(\mathcal{C}, W)$  we have

$$
\begin{array}{l} | \operatorname {C o v} [ \mathbf {y} ^ {*}, h (\mathbf {x}) | \tilde {p} (\mathbf {x}) = v ] | = | \mathbf {E} [ h (\mathbf {x}) (\mathbf {y} ^ {*} - \mathbf {E} [ \mathbf {y} ^ {*} ]) | \tilde {p} (\mathbf {x}) = v ] | \\ = | \mathbf {E} [ h (\mathbf {x}) (\mathbf {y} ^ {*} - v) | \tilde {p} (x) = v ] | + | \mathbf {E} [ (v - \mathbf {y} ^ {*}) | \tilde {p} (\mathbf {x}) = v ] | \\ \leq (W + 1) \alpha (v) \\ \end{array}
$$

where we use the fact that  $h \in \operatorname{Lin}(\mathcal{C}, W)$  and  $1 \in \mathcal{C}$ . Since  $\mathbf{y}^* \in \{0, 1\}$ , this implies the claimed bounds by standard properties of covariance (see [GKR+22, Corollary 5.1])

Next we show the following lemma, which shows that one can replace  $h(\mathbf{x})$  by the constant  $\Pi_{\ell}(\mu(h:v))$  without a large increase in the loss.

Lemma 6.4. For all  $h \in \operatorname{Lin}(\mathcal{C}, W)$ ,  $v \in \operatorname{Im}(\tilde{p})$  and loss  $\ell \in \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CVX}}(B)$ , we have

$$
\underset {\mathcal {D} | _ {v}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \ell \left(\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, \Pi_ {\ell} (\mu (h: v)) \right] \leq \underset {\mathcal {D} | _ {v}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \ell \left(\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, h (\mathbf {x})\right) \right] + 2 (W + 1) \alpha (v). \right. \tag {13}
$$

Proof. For any  $y \in \{0,1\}$ ,

$$
\begin{array}{l} \underset {\mathcal {D} | _ {v}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \ell (\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, h (\mathbf {x})) | (\tilde {p} (\mathbf {x}), \mathbf {y} ^ {*}) = (v, y) \right] = \underset {\mathcal {D} | _ {v}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \ell (y, h (\mathbf {x})) | (\tilde {p} (\mathbf {x}), \mathbf {y} ^ {*}) = (v, y) \right] \\ \geq \ell (y, \mathbf {E} [ h (\mathbf {x}) | (\tilde {p} (\mathbf {x}), \mathbf {y} ^ {*}) = (v, y) ]) (14) \\ = \ell (y, \mu (h: v, y)) \\ \geq \ell (y, \Pi_ {\ell} (\mu (h: v, y))). (15) \\ \end{array}
$$

where Equation (14) uses Jensen's inequality, and Equation (15) uses the optimality of projection for nice loss functions. Further, by the 1-Lipschitzness of  $\ell$  on  $I_{\ell}$ , and of  $\Pi_{\ell}$  on  $\mathbb{R}$

$$
\begin{array}{l} \ell (y, \Pi_ {\ell} (\mu (h: v, y))) - \ell (y, \Pi_ {\ell} (\mu (h: v))) \leq | \Pi_ {\ell} (\mu (h: v, y)) - \Pi_ {\ell} (\mu (h: v)) | \\ \leq | \mu (h: v, y) - \mu (h: v) | \tag {16} \\ \end{array}
$$

Hence we have

$$
\begin{array}{l} \left. \mathbf {E} _ {\mathcal {D} | v} \left[ \ell \left(\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, \Pi_ {\ell} (\mu (h: v))\right) \right] - \mathbf {E} _ {\mathcal {D} | v} \left[ \ell \left(\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, h (\mathbf {x})\right) \right] \right] \\ = \sum_ {y \in \{0, 1 \}} \Pr [ \mathbf {y} ^ {*} = y | \tilde {p} (\mathbf {x}) = v ] \left(\ell (y, \Pi_ {\ell} (\mu (h: v))) - \mathbf {E} [ \ell (y, h (\mathbf {x})) | (\tilde {p} (\mathbf {x}), \mathbf {y} ^ {*}) = (v, y) ]\right) \\ \leq \sum_ {y \in \{0, 1 \}} \Pr [ \mathbf {y} ^ {*} = y | \tilde {p} (\mathbf {x}) = v ] \left(\ell \left(y, \Pi_ {\ell} \left(\mu (h: v)\right)\right) - \ell \left(y, \Pi_ {\ell} \left(\mu (h: v, y)\right)\right)\right) \quad (\text {B y E q u a t i o n (1 5)}) \\ \leq \sum_ {y \in \{0, 1 \}} \Pr [ \mathbf {y} ^ {*} = y | \tilde {p} (\mathbf {x}) = v ] | \mu (h: v, y) - \mu (h: v) | \quad \text {(b y E q u a t i o n (1 6))} \\ \leq 2 (W + 1) \alpha (v). \quad (\text {B y}) \\ \end{array}
$$

Next we compare  $\Pi_{\ell}(\mu(h:v))$  with  $k_{\ell}(v)$ . It is clear that the latter is better for minimizing loss when  $\mathbf{y} \sim \mathrm{Ber}(v)$ , by definition. We need to compare the losses when  $\mathbf{y}^* \sim \mathrm{Ber}(p_v^*)$ . But  $p_v^*$  and  $v$  are at most  $\alpha(v)$  apart by Equation (11). Hence, by using Lipschitzness, one can infer that  $k_{\ell}(v)$  is better than  $\Pi_{\ell}(\mu(h:v))$  and hence  $h(\mathbf{x})$ . This is formalized in the following lemma and its proof.

Lemma 6.5. For all  $v \in \operatorname{Im}(\tilde{p})$ ,  $\ell \in \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cvx}}(B)$  and  $h \in \operatorname{Lin}(\mathcal{C}, W)$ , we have

$$
\underset {\mathcal {D} | _ {v}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \ell \left(\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, k _ {\ell} (\tilde {p} (\mathbf {x}))\right) \right] \leq \underset {\mathcal {D} | _ {v}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \ell \left(\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, h (\mathbf {x})\right) \right] + 2 (W + B + 1) \alpha (v). \tag {17}
$$

Proof. By the definition of  $k_{\ell}$ ,  $k_{\ell}(v)$  minimizes expected loss when  $\mathbf{y} \sim \mathrm{Ber}(v)$ , so

$$
\ell (v, k _ {\ell} (v)) \leq \ell (v, \Pi_ {\ell} (\mu (h: v)) \tag {18}
$$

On the other hand,

$$
\underset {\mathcal {D} | _ {v}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \ell (\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, t) \right] = \ell \left(p _ {v} ^ {*}, t\right), \text {w h e r e} p _ {v} ^ {*} = \underset {\mathcal {D} | _ {v}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \mathbf {y} ^ {*} \right].

Thus our goal is to compare the losses  $\ell(p_v^*, t)$  for  $t = k_\ell(v)$  and  $t = \Pi_\ell(\mu(h : v))$ . Hence, applying Lemma 3.2 gives

$$
\begin{array}{l} \ell \left(p _ {v} ^ {*}, k _ {\ell} (v)\right) \leq \ell (v, k _ {\ell} (v)) + \alpha (v) B \\ - \ell \left(p _ {v} ^ {*}, \Pi_ {\ell} (\mu (h: v)) \leq - \ell (v, \Pi_ {\ell} (\mu (h: v)) + \alpha (v) B \right. \\ \end{array}
$$

Subtracting these inequalities and then using Equation (18) gives

$$
\begin{array}{l} \ell \left(p _ {v} ^ {*}, k _ {\ell} (v)\right) - \ell \left(p _ {v} ^ {*}, \Pi_ {\ell} (\mu (h: v)) \leq \ell (v, k _ {\ell} (v)) - \ell (v, \Pi_ {\ell} (\mu (h: v)) + 2 B \alpha (v) \right. (19) \\ \leq 2 \alpha (v) B. (20) \\ \end{array}
$$

We can now write

$$
\begin{array}{l} \underset {\mathcal {D} | _ {v}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \ell \left(\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, k _ {\ell} (v)\right) \right] = \ell \left(p _ {v} ^ {*}, k _ {\ell} (v)\right) \\ \leq \ell \left(p _ {v} ^ {*}, \Pi_ {\ell} (\mu (h: v)) + 2 \alpha (v) B \right. \quad \text {(b y E q u a t i o n (2 0))} \\ = \underset {\mathcal {D} | _ {v}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \ell \left(\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, \Pi_ {\ell} (\mu (h: v))\right) \right] + 2 \alpha (v) B \\ \leq \underset {\mathcal {D} | _ {v}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \ell \left(\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, h (\mathbf {x})\right) \right] + 2 (W + 1) \alpha (v) + 2 B \alpha (v). \quad \text {(b y E q u a t i o n (1 3))} \\ \end{array}
$$

We now complete the proof of Theorem 6.1.

Proof of Theorem 6.1. (1)  $\Longrightarrow$  (2) Fix a  $(\mathcal{C},\alpha_{1})$ -swap multicalibrated predictor  $\tilde{p}$ . Fix a choice of loss functions  $\{\ell_v\in \mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{cvx}}\}_{v\in \mathrm{Im}(f)}$  and hypotheses  $\{h_v\in \mathcal{H}\}_{v\in \mathrm{Im}(f)}$ . since  $c(\mathbf{x}),y - v\in [-1,1]$ . Hence  $h^\prime (x) = w_1\cdot 1 + w_2c(v)$  where  $|w_{1}| + |w_{2}|\leq 2$ . This contradicts the definition of an  $(\ell_2,\mathrm{Lin}(\mathcal{C},2),\alpha_3)$ -swap agnostic learner if  $\alpha_{3} < \alpha_{1}^{2}$ .

# 7 Characterizing swap loss OI

We show that  $(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C})$  swap loss OI and  $(\mathcal{L}\circ \mathcal{C})$  -swap multicalibration are equivalent for nice loss functions.

Theorem 7.1. Let  $\mathcal{L} \subseteq \mathcal{L}(B)$  be a family of  $B$ -nice loss functions such that  $\ell_2 \in \mathcal{L}$ . Then  $(\partial \mathcal{L} \circ \mathcal{C}, \alpha_1)$ -swap multicalibration and  $(\mathcal{L}, \mathcal{C}, \alpha_2)$ -swap loss  $OI$  are equivalent.<sup>4</sup>

In preparation for this, we start with the following simple claim from  $\left[\mathrm{GHK}^{+}23\right]$ .

Claim 7.2.  $[GHK^{+}23$ , Lemma 4.8] For random variables  $\mathbf{y}_1, \mathbf{y}_2 \in \{0,1\}$  and  $t \in \mathbb{R}$ ,

$$
\mathbf {E} [ \ell (\mathbf {y} _ {1}, t) - \ell (\mathbf {y} _ {2}, t) ] = \mathbf {E} [ (\mathbf {y} _ {1} - \mathbf {y} _ {2}) \partial \ell (t) ]. \tag {21}
$$

We record two corollaries of this claim. These can respectively be seen as strengthenings of the two parts of Theorem  $\left[\mathrm{GHK}^{+}23\right.$ , Theorem 4.9], which respectively characterized hypothesis OI in terms of multiaccuracy and decision OI in terms of calibration. We generalize these to the swap setting.

Corollary 7.3. For every choice of  $\{\ell_v, c_v\}_{v \in \mathrm{Im}(\tilde{p})}$ , we have

$$
\mathbf {E} _ {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} \left[ \left| \mathbf {E} _ {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} [ \ell_ {\mathbf {v}} (\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, c _ {\mathbf {v}} (\mathbf {x})) - \ell_ {\mathbf {v}} (\tilde {\mathbf {y}}, c _ {\mathbf {v}} (\mathbf {x})) ] \right| \right] = \mathbf {E} _ {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} \left[ \left| \mathbf {E} _ {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} [ (\mathbf {y} ^ {*} - \tilde {\mathbf {y}}) \partial \ell_ {\mathbf {v}} \circ c _ {\mathbf {v}} (\mathbf {x}) ] \right| \right]. \tag {22}
$$

Hence if  $\tilde{p}$  is  $(\partial \mathcal{L} \circ \mathcal{C}, \alpha)$ -swap multicalibrated, then

$$
\underset {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \left| \underset {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \ell_ {\mathbf {v}} (\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, c _ {\mathbf {v}} (\mathbf {x})) - \ell_ {\mathbf {v}} (\tilde {\mathbf {y}}, c _ {\mathbf {v}} (\mathbf {x})) \right] \right| \right] \leq \alpha .
$$

Proof. Equation (22) is derived by applying Equation (21) to the LHS. Assuming that  $\tilde{p}$  is  $(\partial \mathcal{L} \circ \mathcal{C}, \alpha)$ -swap multicalibrated, we have

$$
\underset {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \left| \underset {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ (\mathbf {y} ^ {*} - \tilde {\mathbf {y}}) \partial \ell_ {\mathbf {v}} \circ c _ {\mathbf {v}} (\mathbf {x}) \right] \right| \right] \leq \underset {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \left| \underset {c ^ {\prime} \in \partial \mathcal {L} \circ \mathcal {C}} {\max} \underset {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ (\mathbf {y} ^ {*} - \tilde {\mathbf {y}}) c ^ {\prime} (\mathbf {x}) \right] \right| \right] \leq \alpha .
$$

Corollary 7.4. Let  $\{\ell_v\}_{v\in \mathrm{Im}(f)}$  be a collection of loss  $B$ -nice loss functions. Let  $k(v) = k_{\ell_v}(v)$ . If  $\tilde{p}$  is  $\alpha$ -calibrated then

$$
\left. \right. \mathbf {E} _ {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} \left[\left| \mathbf {E} _ {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} \left[ \ell_ {\mathbf {v}} \left(\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, k (\mathbf {v})\right) - \ell_ {\mathbf {v}} \left(\tilde {\mathbf {y}}, k (\mathbf {v})\right)\right]\right|\right] \leq B \alpha . \tag {23}
$$

Proof. We have

$$
\begin{array}{l} \underset {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \left| \underset {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} {\mathbf {E}} [ \ell_ {\mathbf {v}} (\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, k (\mathbf {v})) - \ell_ {\mathbf {v}} (\tilde {\mathbf {y}}, k (\mathbf {v})) ] \right| \right] = \underset {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \left| \underset {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} {\mathbf {E}} [ (\mathbf {y} ^ {*} - \mathbf {v}) \partial \ell_ {\mathbf {v}} (k (\mathbf {v})) ] \right| \right] \\ = \mathbf {E} _ {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} \left[ | \partial \ell_ {\mathbf {v}} (k (\mathbf {v})) | \left| \mathbf {E} _ {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} [ \mathbf {y} ^ {*} - \mathbf {v} ] \right| \right] \\ \leq B \underset {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \left| \underset {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} {\mathbf {E}} [ \mathbf {y} ^ {*} - \mathbf {v} ] \right| \right] \\ \leq B \alpha . \\ \end{array}
$$

where we use the fact that  $k(v)\in I_{\ell}$  , and so  $|\partial \ell_v(k(v))|\leq B$

Finally, we show the following key technical lemma which explains why the  $\ell_2$  loss has a special role.

Lemma 7.5. If  $\tilde{p}$  is  $(\{\ell_2\}, \mathcal{C}, \alpha)$ -swap  $OI$ , then it is  $\alpha$ -calibrated.

Proof. Observe that  $\ell_2(y,v) = (y - v)^2 / 2$  so  $k_{\ell_2}(v) = v$ . Hence,

$$
\begin{array}{l} u _ {\ell_ {2}, 0} (x, v, y) = \ell_ {2} (y, k _ {\ell} (v)) - \ell_ {2} (y, 0) \\ = ((y - v) ^ {2} - y ^ {2}) / 2 \\ = - v y + v ^ {2} / 2. \tag {24} \\ \end{array}
$$

Recall that  $\{0,1\} \subset \mathcal{C}$ . The implication of swap loss OI when we take  $c_{v} = 0$  for all  $v$  is that

$$
\underset {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \left| \underset {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ u _ {\ell_ {2}, 0} (\mathbf {x}, \mathbf {v}, \mathbf {y} ^ {*}) - u _ {\ell_ {2}, 0} (\mathbf {x}, \mathbf {v}, \tilde {\mathbf {y}}) \right] \right| \right] \leq \alpha .
$$

We can simplify the LHS using Equation (24) to derive

$$
\begin{array}{l} \underset {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \left| \underset {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ (- \mathbf {v y} ^ {*} + \mathbf {v} ^ {2} / 2) - (- \mathbf {v} \tilde {\mathbf {y}} + \mathbf {v} ^ {2} / 2) \right] \right| \right] = \underset {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \left| \underset {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \mathbf {v} (\mathbf {y} ^ {*} - \tilde {\mathbf {y}}) \right] \right| \right] \\ = \mathbf {E} _ {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} \left[ \mathbf {v} \left| \mathbf {E} _ {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} [ \tilde {\mathbf {y}} - \mathbf {y} ^ {*} ] \right| \right] \leq \alpha . \tag {25} \\ \end{array}
$$

Considering the case where  $c_v = 1$  for all  $v$  gives

$$
\begin{array}{l} u _ {\ell_ {2}, 1} (x, v, y) = \ell_ {2} (y, k _ {\ell} (v)) - \ell_ {2} (y, 1) \\ = ((y - v) ^ {2} - (1 - y) ^ {2}) / 2 \\ = (1 - v) y + (v ^ {2} - 1) / 2. \\ \end{array}
$$

We derive the following implication of swap loss OI by taking  $c_v = 0$  for all  $v$ :

$$
\mathbf {E} _ {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} \left[ \left| \mathbf {E} _ {\left. \mathcal {D} \right| _ {\mathbf {v}}} \left[ u _ {\ell_ {2}, 1} (\mathbf {x}, \mathbf {v}, \mathbf {y} ^ {*}) - u _ {\ell_ {2}, 1} (\mathbf {x}, \mathbf {v}, \tilde {\mathbf {y}}) \right] \right| \right] = \mathbf {E} _ {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} \left[ (1 - \mathbf {v}) \left| \mathbf {E} _ {\left. \mathcal {D} \right| _ {\mathbf {v}}} \left[ \tilde {\mathbf {y}} - \mathbf {y} ^ {*} \right] \right| \right] \leq \alpha \tag {26}
$$

Adding the bounds from Equations (25) and (26) we get

$$
\underset {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \left| \underset {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} {\mathbf {E}} [ \mathbf {v} - \mathbf {y} ^ {*} ] \right| \right] = \underset {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \left| \underset {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} {\mathbf {E}} [ \tilde {\mathbf {y}} - \mathbf {y} ^ {*} ] \right| \right] \leq \alpha
$$

Proof of Theorem 7.1. We first show the forward implication, that swap multicalibration implies swap loss  $\mathrm{OI}$ .

Since  $\ell_2\in \mathcal{L}$  and  $1\in \mathcal{C}$ , we have  $\partial \ell_{2}\circ 1 = 1\in \partial \mathcal{L}\circ \mathcal{C}$ . This implies that  $\tilde{p}$  is  $\alpha$ -multicalibrated, since

$$
\mathbf {E} _ {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} \left[ \left| \mathbf {E} _ {\left. \mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}} \right.} [ 1 (\mathbf {y} - \mathbf {v}) ] \right| \right] \leq \mathbf {E} _ {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} \left[ \max  _ {c \in \mathcal {C}} \left| \mathbf {E} _ {\left. \mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}} \right.} [ c (\mathbf {x}) (\mathbf {y} - \mathbf {v}) ] \right| \right] \leq \alpha .
$$

Consider any collection of losses  $\{\ell_v\}_{v\in \operatorname {Im}(\tilde{p})}$ . Applying Corollary 7.4, we have

$$
\left. \right. \mathbf {E} _ {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} \left[\left| \mathbf {E} _ {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} \left[ \ell_ {\mathbf {v}} \left(\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, k (\mathbf {v})\right) - \ell_ {\mathbf {v}} \left(\tilde {\mathbf {y}}, k (\mathbf {v})\right)\right]\right|\right] \leq B \alpha .
$$

On the other hand, by Corollary 7.3, we have for every choice of  $\{\ell_v,c_v\}_{v\in \mathrm{Im}(\tilde{p})}$

$$
\left. \right. \mathbf {E} _ {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} \left[\left| \mathbf {E} _ {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} \left[ \ell_ {\mathbf {v}} \left(\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, c _ {\mathbf {v}} (\mathbf {x})\right) - \ell_ {\mathbf {v}} \left(\tilde {\mathbf {y}}, c _ {\mathbf {v}} (\mathbf {x})\right)\right]\right|\right] \leq \alpha .
$$

Hence for any choice of  $\{u_v\}_{v \in \operatorname{Im}(\tilde{p})}$  we can bound

$$
\begin{array}{l} \underset {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} {\mathbf {E}} \left| \underset {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ u _ {\mathbf {v}} (\mathbf {x}, \mathbf {v}, \mathbf {y} ^ {*}) - u _ {\mathbf {v}} (\mathbf {x}, \mathbf {v}, \tilde {\mathbf {y}}) \right] \right| \\ \leq \mathbf {E} _ {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} \left[ \right.\left| \right. \mathbf {E} _ {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} \left[ \right. \ell_ {\mathbf {v}} \left(\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, k (\mathbf {v})\right) - \ell_ {\mathbf {v}} \left( \right.\tilde {\mathbf {y}}, k (\mathbf {v}) \left. \right]\left. \right| + \left| \mathbf {E} _ {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} \left[ \ell_ {\mathbf {v}} \left(\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, c _ {\mathbf {v}} (\mathbf {x})\right) - \ell_ {\mathbf {v}} \left(\tilde {\mathbf {y}}, c _ {\mathbf {v}} (\mathbf {x})\right)\right]\right|\left. \right] \\ \leq (B + 1) \alpha \\ \end{array}
$$

which shows that  $\tilde{p}$  satisfies swap loss OI with  $\alpha_{2} = (D + 1)\alpha_{1}$ .

Next we show the reverse implication: if  $\tilde{p}$  satisfies  $(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C},\alpha_{2})$ -swap loss OI, then it satisfies  $(\partial \mathcal{L}\circ \mathcal{C},\alpha_{1})$ -swap multicalibration. The first step is to observe that by lemma 7.5, since  $\ell_2\in \mathcal{L}$ , the predictor  $\tilde{p}$  is  $\alpha_{2}$  calibrated. Since any  $\ell \in \mathcal{L}$  is  $B$ -nice, we have

$$
\mathbf {E} _ {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} \left[ \left| \mathbf {E} _ {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} [ \ell_ {\mathbf {v}} (\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, k (\mathbf {v})) - \ell_ {\mathbf {v}} (\tilde {\mathbf {y}}, k (\mathbf {v})) ] \right| \right] = \mathbf {E} _ {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} \left[ \left| \mathbf {E} _ {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} [ (\mathbf {y} ^ {*} - \tilde {\mathbf {y}}) k (\mathbf {v}) ] \right| \right] \leq B \alpha_ {2}.
$$

For any  $\{\ell_v, c_v\}_{v \in \operatorname{Im}(f)}$ , since

$$
u _ {v} (x, v, y) = \ell_ {v} (y, k _ {\ell} (v)) + \ell_ {v} (y, c _ {v} (x))
$$

we can write

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathbf {E} _ {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} \left[ \left| \mathbf {E} _ {\left. \mathcal {D} \right| _ {\mathbf {v}}} \left[ \ell_ {\mathbf {v}} \left(\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, c (\mathbf {x})\right) - \ell_ {\mathbf {v}} \left(\tilde {\mathbf {y}}, c (\mathbf {x})\right) \right] \right| \right] \\ \leq \mathbf {E} _ {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} \left[ \left| \mathbf {E} _ {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} [ u _ {\mathbf {v}} (\mathbf {x}, \mathbf {v}, \mathbf {y} ^ {*}) - u _ {\mathbf {v}} (\mathbf {x}, \mathbf {v}, \tilde {\mathbf {y}}) ] \right| + \left| \mathbf {E} _ {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} [ \ell_ {\mathbf {v}} (\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, k (\mathbf {v})) - \ell_ {\mathbf {v}} (\tilde {\mathbf {y}}, k (\mathbf {v})) ] \right| \right] \\ \leq (B + 1) \alpha_ {2}. \\ \end{array}
$$

But by Equation (22), the LHS can be written as

$$
\mathbf {E} _ {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} \left[ \left| \mathbf {E} _ {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} \left[ \ell_ {\mathbf {v}} \left(\mathbf {y} ^ {*}, c (\mathbf {x})\right) - \ell_ {\mathbf {v}} \left(\tilde {\mathbf {y}}, c (\mathbf {x})\right) \right] \right| \right] = \mathbf {E} _ {\mathbf {v} \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} \left[ \left| \mathbf {E} _ {\mathcal {D} | _ {\mathbf {v}}} \left[ \partial \ell_ {\mathbf {v}} \circ c _ {\mathbf {v}} (\mathbf {x}) \left(\mathbf {y} ^ {*} - \mathbf {v}\right) \right] \right| \right]
$$

This shows that  $\tilde{p}$  is  $(\partial \mathcal{L} \circ \mathcal{C}, (B + 1)\alpha_{2})$ -swap multicalibrated.

# 8 Relating notions of omniprediction

In this work, we have discussed four notions of omniprediction for a given  $(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C})$  pair:

00) Omniprediction form  $\left[\mathrm{GKR}^{+}22\right]$ .  
01) Loss OI from  $\left[\mathrm{GHK}^{+}23\right]$ .  
10) Swap omniprediction.  
11) Swap Loss OI.

In order to compare them, we ask which of these notions implies the other for every choice of  $\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C}$ ? The relation between them is given in Figure 1.

Let us recap why the above implications are known to hold.

- Loss OI implies omniprediction by  $\left[\mathrm{GHK}^{+}23\right.$ , Proposition 4.5].  
- Swap omniprediction implies omniprediction by Corollary 4.4.  
- Swap loss OI implies both loss OI and swap multicalibration by Lemma 4.8.

Further, this picture captures all the implications that hold for all  $(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C})$ , any implications not implied by this diagram will be shown to not hold for some choice of  $(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C})$ . We will prove Theorem 1.2 which states that neither loss OI nor swap omniprediction implies the other for all  $(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C})$ . This separates these notions from swap loss OI, since swap loss OI implies both these notions<sup>5</sup> By similar reasoning, it separates omniprediction from both these loss OI and swap omnipredicton, since omniprediction is implied by either of them.

$\left[\mathrm{GHK}^{+}23, \text { Theorem 4.6 }\right]$  already showed that omniprediction does not imply loss OI, indeed we will observe that the same example in fact shows that swap omniprediction does not imply loss OI. In their example, we have  $\mathcal{D}$  on  $\{\pm 1\}^{3} \times [0,1]$  where the marginal on  $\{\pm 1\}^{3}$  is uniform, and  $p^{*}(x) = (1 + x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}) / 2$ , whereas  $\tilde{p}(x) = 1 / 2$  for all  $x$ . We take  $\mathcal{C} = \{1, x_{1}, x_{2}, x_{3}\}$ . Since  $\tilde{p} = 1 / 2$  is

constant, it is easy to check that  $\tilde{p} - p^{*} = -x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}/2$  is uncorrelated with  $\mathcal{C}$ . Hence  $\tilde{p}$  satisfies swap multicalibration (which is the same as multicalibration or even multiaccuracy in this setting where  $\tilde{p}$  is constant). Hence by Theorem 6.1,  $\tilde{p}$  is an  $(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cvx}}(1), \mathrm{Lin}_{\mathcal{C}}, 0)$ -swap omnipredictor. [GHK+23, Theorem 4.6] prove that  $\tilde{p}$  is not loss-OI for the  $\ell_{4}$  loss. Hence we have the following result.

Lemma 8.1. The predictor  $\tilde{p}$  is  $(\mathcal{C},0)$ -swap multicalibrated and hence it is a  $(\{\ell_4\},\mathrm{Lin}(\mathcal{C}),0)$ -swap omnipredictor. But it is not  $(\{\ell_4\},\mathrm{Lin}(\mathcal{C},1),\varepsilon)$ -loss  $OI$  for  $\varepsilon < 4 / 9$ .

We remark that the construction extends to all  $\ell_p$  losses for even  $p > 2$ . Hence even for convex losses, the notions of swap omniprediction are loss-OI seem incomparable.

Next we construct an example showing that loss OI need not imply swap omniprediction. We will consider the set of all GLM losses defined below, which contain common losses including the squared loss and the logistic loss.

Definition 8.2. Let  $g: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$  be a convex, differentiable function such that  $[0,1] \subseteq \operatorname{Im}(g')$ . Define its matching loss to be  $\ell_g = g(t) - yt$ . Define  $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GLM}} = \{\ell_g\}$  be the set of all such loss functions.

$\left[\mathrm{GHK}^{+}23\right]$  show a general decomposition result that reduces achieving loss-OI to a calibration condition and a multiaccuracy condition. Whereas arbitrary losses might require multiaccuracy for the more powerful class  $\partial \mathcal{L}\circ \mathcal{C}$ , for  $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GLM}}$ ,  $\partial \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GLM}}\circ \mathcal{C} = \mathcal{C}$ . This is formalized in the following result.

Lemma 8.3.  $[GHK^{+}23$ , Theorem 5.3] If  $\tilde{p}$  is  $\varepsilon_{1}$ -calibrated and  $(\mathcal{C},\varepsilon_2)$ -multiaccurate, then it is  $(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GLM}},\operatorname {Lin}(\mathcal{C},W),\delta)$ -loss  $OI$  for  $\delta = \varepsilon_{1} + W\varepsilon_{2}$ .

We now show that that loss-OI does not imply swap-multicalibration. In light of the above result, it suffices to find a predictor that is calibrated and multiaccurate (and hence satisfies loss-OI), but not multicalibrated, hence not swap multicalibrated. By Theorem 6.1 it is not an  $(\{\ell_2\}, \mathrm{Lin}_{\mathcal{C}}, \delta)$ -swap omnipredictor for  $\delta$  less than some constant.

Let us define the predictors  $p^*, \tilde{p} : \{\pm 1\}^2 \to [0,1]$  as below. We use these to show a separation between loss-OI and swap-resilient omniprediction.

Lemma 8.4. Consider the distribution  $\mathcal{D}$  on  $\{\pm 1\}^2\times \{0,1\}$  where the marginal on  $\{\pm 1\}^2$  is uniform and  $\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{y}|x] = p^{*}(x)$ . Let  $\mathcal{C} = \{1,x_1,x_2\}$ .

1.  $\tilde{p} \in \operatorname{Lin}(\mathcal{C}, 1)$ . Moreover, it minimizes the squared error over all hypotheses from  $\operatorname{Lin}(\mathcal{C})$ .  
2.  $\tilde{p}$  is perfectly calibrated and  $(\mathcal{C},0)$ -multiaccurate. So it is  $(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GLM}},\mathrm{Lin}(\mathcal{C}),0)$ -loss-  $OI$ .  
3.  $\tilde{p}$  is not  $(\mathcal{C},\alpha)$ -multicalibrated for  $\alpha < 1/8$ . It is not  $(\ell_2, \operatorname{Lin}(\mathcal{C}), \delta)$ -swap agnostic learner for  $\delta < 1/64$ .

Proof. We compute Fourier expansions for the two predictors:

$$
p ^ {*} (x) = \frac {1}{8} \left(4 + 3 x _ {2} - x _ {1} x _ {2}\right) \tag {27}
$$

$$
\tilde {p} (x) = \frac {1}{8} (4 + 3 x _ {2}) \tag {28}
$$

This shows that  $\tilde{p} \in \mathrm{Lin}(\mathcal{C})$ , and moreover that it is the optimal approximation to  $p^*$  in  $\mathrm{Lin}(\mathcal{C})$ , as it is the projection of  $p^*$  onto  $\mathrm{Lin}(\mathcal{C})$ . This shows that  $\tilde{p}$  is an  $(\ell_2, \mathrm{Lin}(\mathcal{C}), 0)$ -agnostic learner.

It is easy to check that  $\tilde{p}$  is perfectly calibrated. It is  $(\mathcal{C},0)$ -multiaccurate, since it is the projection of  $p^*$  onto  $\operatorname{Lin}(\mathcal{C})$ , so  $\tilde{p} - p^*$  is orthogonal to  $\operatorname{Lin}(\mathcal{C})$ . Hence we can apply Lemma 8.3 to conclude that it is  $(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GLM}},\operatorname{Lin}(C),0)$ -loss-OI, where  $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GLM}}$  which contains the squared loss.

To show that  $\tilde{p}$  is not swap-agnostic, we observe that conditioning on the value of  $\tilde{p}(\mathbf{x}) = (4 + 3x_2)/8$  is equivalent to conditioning on  $x_2 \in \{\pm 1\}$ . For each value of  $x_2$ , the restriction of  $p^*$  which is now linear in  $x_1$  belongs to  $\operatorname{Lin}(\mathcal{C})$ . Indeed if we condition on  $\tilde{p}(x) = 1/8$  so that  $x_2 = -1$ , we have

$$
p ^ {*} (x) = \frac {1}{2} - \frac {3}{8} + \frac {1}{8} x _ {1} = \frac {1 + x _ {1}}{8}.
$$

Conditioned on  $\tilde{p}(x) = 7/8$  so that  $x_2 = 1$ , we have

$$
p ^ {*} (x) = \frac {1}{2} + \frac {3}{8} - \frac {1}{8} x _ {1} = \frac {7 - x _ {1}}{8}.
$$

Hence we have

$$
\underset {v \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\tilde {p}}} {\mathbf {E}} \left[ \left| \min _ {h \in \mathrm {L i n} (\mathcal {C})} \mathbf {E} [ (\mathbf {y} - h (\mathbf {x})) ^ {2} | f (\mathbf {x}) = v ] \right| \right] = \mathbf {E} [ (y - p ^ {*} (x)) ^ {2} ] = \mathrm {V a r} [ \mathbf {y} ],
$$

whereas the variance decomposition of squared loss gives

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathbf {E} \left[ (\mathbf {y} - \tilde {p} (\mathbf {x})) ^ {2} \right] = \mathbf {E} \left[ (\mathbf {y} - p ^ {*} (\mathbf {x})) ^ {2} \right] + \mathbf {E} \left[ (p ^ {*} (\mathbf {x}) - \tilde {p} (\mathbf {x})) ^ {2} \right] \\ = \operatorname {V a r} [ \mathbf {y} ] + \frac {1}{6 4} \mathbf {E} \left[ \left(x _ {1} x _ {2}\right) ^ {2} \right] \\ = \operatorname {V a r} [ \mathbf {y} ] + \frac {1}{6 4}. \\ \end{array}
$$

Hence  $\tilde{p}$  is not  $(\ell_2,\mathrm{Lin}(\mathcal{C}),\delta)$ -swap agnostic for  $\delta < 1 / 64$

To see that  $f$  is not multicalibrated for small  $\alpha$ , observe that conditioned on  $x_2 \in \{\pm 1\}$ , the correlation between  $x_1$  and  $\tilde{p} - p^*$  is  $1/8$ .

Note that item (1) above separates swap-resilient omniprediction from omniprediction/ agnostic learning. This separation can also be derived from  $\left[\mathrm{GKR}^{+}22\right.$ , Theorem 7.5] which separated (standard) omniprediction from agnostic learning, since swap-resilient omniprediction implies standard omniprediction.

Comparing notions for  $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GLM}}$ : In Section 1.4, we compared notions of omniprediction for  $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cvx}}$ . Here we consider the class  $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GLM}}$ . The key observation here is that  $\partial \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GLM}} \circ \mathcal{C} = \mathcal{C}$ , which leads to the following collapse.

Lemma 8.5. The notions of  $(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GLM}},\mathcal{C},\alpha_1)$ -swap loss  $OI$  and  $(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GLM}},\mathcal{C},\alpha_2)$ -swap omniprediction are equivalent.

We know that loss OI implies omniprediction for  $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GLM}}$ , since this holds true for all  $\mathcal{L}$ . We do not know if these notions are infact equivalent for  $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GLM}}$ , since the construction in Lemma 8.1 used the  $\ell_4$  loss which does not belong to  $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GLM}}$ .
