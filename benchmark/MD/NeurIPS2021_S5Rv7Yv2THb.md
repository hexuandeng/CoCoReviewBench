# Abstract

Numerous models for supervised, unsupervised, and reinforcement learning benefit from combining discrete and continuous components. The resulting discrete-continuous models are end-to-end learnable and are compositional, tend to generalize better, and are more interpretable. A popular approach to build discrete-continuous computation graphs is that of integrating discrete probability distributions into neural networks using stochastic softmax tricks. Prior work has focused on computation graphs with a single discrete component on each of the graph's execution paths. We analyze the behavior of more complex stochastic computations graphs with multiple sequential discrete components. We show that it is challenging to optimize the parameters of these models, mainly due to vanishing gradients. We then propose two new strategies to overcome these challenges. First, we propose dropout residual connections specifically tailored to discrete-continuous computation graphs. Second, we show that matching the temperature parameters of the Gumbel-Softmax and increasing the scale parameter of the Gumbel noise perturbations drastically improves the learning behavior. With an extensive set of experiments, we show that we can train complex discrete-continuous models which one cannot train with standard stochastic softmax tricks. We also show that using complex discrete-stochastic models allows us to generalize better than their continuous counterparts on several benchmark datasets.

# 1 Introduction

Neuro-symbolic learning systems aim to combine discrete and continuous operations. The majority of recent research has focused on integrating neural network components into probabilistic logics [21, 15], that is, making logic-based reasoning approaches more amenable to noisy and high-dimensional input data. On the other end of the spectrum are the data-driven approaches which aim to learn a modular and discrete program structure in an end-to-end neural network based system. The broader vision followed by proponents of these approaches are systems capable of assembling the required modular operations to solve a variety of tasks with heterogeneous input data. Reinforcement learning, program induction, and neural module networks [1] are particular instances of such data-driven discrete-continuous learning systems.

We take a more focused look at learning systems comprised of both discrete and continuous (differentiable) operations where the discrete operations are modeled as discrete probability distributions. The resulting systems can be described by their stochastic computation graphs a formalism recently introduced to unify several related approaches [23]. Figure 1 illustrates two instances of such stochastic computation graphs. For a given high-dimensional input such as a set of images or a list of symbols, a computation graph, consisting of stochastic (discrete) and continuous (neural) nodes, is created to solve a particular task. With this paper we address the problem of learning such discrete-continuous computation graphs. # Efficient Learning of Discrete-Continuous Computation Graphs

Any deterministic neural network can be represented by a composition of basic differentiable functions. These networks, therefore, can be fully described by a directed acyclic graph (the computation graph) that determines the operations executed during the forward and backward passes. Schulman et al. [23] proposed stochastic computation graphs as an extension of neural networks that combine deterministic and stochastic operations - a node in the computation graph can be either a differentiable function or a probability distribution. A stochastic node  $X$  is typically a random variable with a parameterized probability distribution  $p_{\theta(x)}$  with parameters  $\pmb{\theta}$ . Suppose that  $f$  is a smooth function (such as the loss function of a learning problem), then the gradient of  $f$  at the stochastic node is  $\nabla_{\pmb{\theta}} \mathbb{E}_{p_{\theta}(x)}[f(x)]$ . Kingma and Welling [12] proposed the reparameterization trick to overcome the problem of computing gradients with respect to the parameters of a distribution. The idea is to find a function  $g$  and distribution  $\rho$  such that one can replace  $x \sim p_{\theta}(x)$  by  $x = g(z, \pmb{\theta})$  with  $z \sim \rho(z)$ . If this is possible, then one can write

$$
\nabla_ {\boldsymbol {\theta}} \mathbb {E} _ {x \sim p _ {\boldsymbol {\theta}} (x)} [ f (x) ] = \mathbb {E} _ {z \sim \rho (z)} [ \nabla_ {\boldsymbol {\theta}} f (g (z, \boldsymbol {\theta})) ] \approx \frac {1}{S} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {S} \nabla_ {\boldsymbol {\theta}} f (g (z _ {i}, \boldsymbol {\theta})) \text {w i t h} z _ {i} \sim \rho (z). \tag {1}
$$

With this paper we address the problem of training stochastic computation graphs where the stochastic nodes are based on categorical (discrete) distributions approximated using stochastic softmax

tricks [11, 20]. More specifically, we consider discrete-continuous components modeling a categorical variable  $X$  with  $k$  possible values  $\mathbf{z}_1,\ldots ,\mathbf{z}_k$ . Each of the  $\mathbf{z}_i$  is the one-hot encoding of the category  $i$ . We consider discrete-continuous functions  $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to \mathbb{R}^n$  with  $\boldsymbol {v} = f(\boldsymbol {u})$  defined as follows

Let  $\mathrm{Gumbel}(0,\beta)$  be the Gumbel distribution with location 0 and scale  $\beta$ . Using the Gumbel-max trick, we can sample  $z\sim p(z;\theta)$  as follows:

$$
\boldsymbol {z} := \boldsymbol {z} _ {i} \quad \text {w i t h} \quad i = \underset {j \in \{1, \dots , k \}} {\arg \max } \left[ \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {j} + \epsilon_ {j} \right] \text {w h e r e} \epsilon_ {j} \sim \operatorname {G u m b e l} (0, 1). \tag {3}
$$

The Gumbel-softmax trick is a relaxation of the Gumbel-max trick (relaxing the argmax into a softmax with a scaling parameter  $\lambda$ ) that allows one to perform standard backpropagation:

$$
\boldsymbol {z} _ {i} = \frac {\exp \left(\left(\boldsymbol {\theta} _ {i} + \epsilon_ {i}\right) / \tau\right)}{\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {k} \exp \left(\left(\boldsymbol {\theta} _ {j} + \epsilon_ {j}\right) / \tau\right)} \text {w h e r e} \epsilon_ {i} \sim \operatorname {G u m b e l} (0, 1). \tag {4}
$$

Hence, instead of sampling a discrete  $z$ , the Gumbel-Softmax trick computes a relaxed  $z$  in its place.

We are concerned with the analysis of the behavior of the Gumbel-softmax trick in more complex stochastic computation graphs. In these computation graphs, multiple sequential Gumbel-softmax components occur on a single execution path. Throughout the remainder of the paper, we assume that during training we use the Softmax-trick as outlined above while at test time we sample discretely using the Gumbel-max trick.

Let us first take a look at  $\frac{\partial v}{\partial u}$ , that is, the derivative of the output of the discrete-continuous function  $f$  with respect to its input. Using the chain rule (and with a slight abuse of notation to simplify the presentation), we can write

$$
\frac {\partial \boldsymbol {v}}{\partial \boldsymbol {u}} = \frac {\partial \boldsymbol {v}}{\partial \boldsymbol {z}} \frac {\partial \boldsymbol {z}}{\partial \boldsymbol {\theta}} \frac {\partial \boldsymbol {\theta}}{\partial \boldsymbol {u}}.
$$

By assumption  $\frac{\partial v}{\partial z}$  and  $\frac{\partial \theta}{\partial u}$  exist and are efficiently computable. Using the derivative of the softmax function and setting  $Z := \sum_{\ell=1}^{k} \exp \left( \left( \theta_{\ell} + \epsilon_{\ell} \right) / \tau \right)$  (the partition function) we have

$$
\frac {\partial \boldsymbol {z} _ {i}}{\partial \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {j}} = \left\{ \begin{array}{c l} \frac {1}{\tau} \left(\frac {\exp \left((\boldsymbol {\theta} _ {i} + \epsilon_ {i}) / \tau\right)}{Z}\right) \left(1 - \frac {\exp \left((\boldsymbol {\theta} _ {j} + \epsilon_ {j}) / \tau\right)}{Z}\right) & \text {i f} i = j \\ - \frac {1}{\tau} \left(\frac {\exp \left((\boldsymbol {\theta} _ {i} + \epsilon_ {i}) / \tau\right)}{Z}\right) \left(\frac {\exp \left((\boldsymbol {\theta} _ {j} + \epsilon_ {j}) / \tau\right)}{Z}\right) & \text {i f} i \neq j. \end{array} \right. \tag {5}
$$

A closer inspection of these formulas points to the problem of vanishing gradients especially when components are connected sequentially. Similar to the problem of sigmoid activation units in standard deep neural networks, which have been replaced by ReLUs and similar modern activation functions but which, unfortunately, are not applicable to our setting, the Gumbel-Softmax-based components lead to vanishing gradients whenever the probability saturates. We hypothesize that it is crucial for the neural networks operating on the continuous (and often high-dimensional) input features to receive more direct gradient information through the discrete-continuous components. Indeed, we observe in several cases that the Gumbel-softmax distributions saturate and, as a result, that sufficient gradient information cannot reach the parameters of upstream neural network components.

# 2.1 TEMPMATCH: Temperature Matching and Inverse Scale Annealing

In this section we explore the behavior of two interdependent parameters: the Gumbel-softmax temperature  $\tau$  and the Gumbel scale parameter  $\beta$ . First, we have the temperature parameter  $\tau$  from the Gumbel-softmax trick (see Equation 4). The purpose of this parameter is to make the output of the softmax function more or less discrete, that is, more or less concentrated on one of the categories. Second, and this is a new insight, we can change the scale parameter  $\beta$  of the Gumbel distribution. For scale parameter  $\beta$ , we sample the noise perturbation iid as  $\epsilon_{i} \sim \mathrm{Gumbel}(0, \beta)$ . If we use the Gumbel-max trick of Equation 3 we implicitly generate samples from the distribution  $p(z_{i}; \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \exp(\theta_{i} / \beta) / \sum_{j=1}^{k} \exp(\theta_{j} / \beta)$ . Increasing the scale parameter  $\beta$ , therefore, makes the Gumbel-max samples more uniform and less concentrated. When using the Gumbel-softmax trick instead of the Gumbel-max trick we obtain  $z_{i}$  that are more uniform and discrete. Indeed, in the limit of  $\beta \to \infty$  we obtain discrete samples uniformly distributed over the categories, independent of the logits. For  $\beta \to 0$ , we obtain the standard softmax distribution with temperature 1. Now, the problem of vanishing gradients caused by Gumbel-softmax components can be mitigated by increasing the scale parameter (decreasing or annealing the inverse scale parameter)  $\beta$  relative to the temperature parameter  $\tau$ . Intuitively, increasing  $\beta$  increases the probability of generating more uniform (relative to the current parameters  $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ ) samples and, therefore, allowing the model to avoid saturation.

To illustrate the effect of matching the two parameters, we conducted the toy experiment depicted in Figure 3. The model here computes  $\mathrm{Softmax}(\theta +\epsilon)$  with parameters  $\pmb{\theta}^{\intercal} = (\theta_{1},\theta_{2})$  and  $\epsilon \sim$  Gumbel(0,β). The learning problem is defined through a cross-entropy loss between the output probabilities of the Gumbel-Softmax and a constant target vector  $(1.0,0.0)^{\intercal}$ . We observe that early during training (before the probabilities saturate) lower values for  $\beta$  work well and exhibit less variance than higher values. However, once the probabilities and, therefore, the gradients start to saturate, a higher value for  $\beta$  enables the continued training of the model. Indeed, even though the softmax probability is close to 1, we observe a continuous increase of the parameter  $\theta_{1}$  and a high enough gradient signal for  $\beta \geq 2$ . It also shows that annealing the inverse scale parameter, that is, increasing the scale parameter relative to  $\tau$  over time, should be beneficial.

In summary, the two parameters  $\tau$  and  $\beta$  should be matched, that is, optimized relative to each other, to obtain the desired behavior during training. We show in later experiments that for more realistic learning problems choosing a higher value for  $\beta$  (and/or increasing  $\beta$  over time) is highly beneficial.

Residual (or shortcut) connections are crucial when training very deep neural networks [9]. A standard residual connection for the discrete-continuous components we consider here would be achieved by replacing Equation (2d) with  $\pmb{v} = (1 - \alpha)\pmb{u} + \alpha h_{\pmb{w}'}(\pmb{z})$ . At training time, one could use several strategies to balance the residual and model connections by, for instance, setting  $\alpha > 0$  to be

We show empirically that models with residual connections tend to strongly overfit even if the parameter  $\alpha$  is close to 1, and completely fail to perform well at test time. To mitigate the problem of overfitting to the existence of residual connections while at the same time reducing vanishing gradients in expectation, we propose DROPRES connections. These are residual connections sampled from a Bernoulli distribution with parameter  $\alpha$ . We replace Equation (2d) by

$$
\boldsymbol {v} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \boldsymbol {u} + h _ {\boldsymbol {w} ^ {\prime}} (\boldsymbol {z}) & \text {w i t h p r o b a b i l i t y} 1 - \alpha \\ h _ {\boldsymbol {w} ^ {\prime}} (\boldsymbol {z}) & \text {w i t h p r o b a b i l i t y} \alpha , \end{array} \right. \tag {6}

that is, we drop out the residual connection with probability  $\alpha$ . This way, the model is exposed to both purely discrete and discrete-continuous computation paths. Hence, with probability  $(1 - \alpha)$  we obtain a shortcut connection between the neural components, effectively bypassing the Gumbel-softmax. With probability  $\alpha$  the training path goes exclusively through the Gumbel-softmax distribution. The expectation of the gradients, taken over a Bernoulli random variable with parameter  $\alpha$ , is now

$$
\mathbb {E} \left[ \frac {\partial \boldsymbol {v}}{\partial \boldsymbol {u}} \right] = (1 - \alpha) \mathbf {1} + \frac {\partial \boldsymbol {v}}{\partial \boldsymbol {z}} \frac {\partial \boldsymbol {z}}{\partial \boldsymbol {\theta}} \frac {\partial \boldsymbol {\theta}}{\partial \boldsymbol {u}}. \tag {7}
$$

Hence, the model obtains a stronger gradient signal for upstream models in expectation while not being able to overfit using the residual connections.

To illustrate the impact of dropout residual connections, we analyzed the gradients of message passing steps for the ListOps problem (see experiments) in a discrete-continuous computation graph of the type depicted in Figure 1 (bottom). As we can observe in Figure 4, if we do not use dropout residual connections, the greater the distance of a discrete-continuous operation to the loss function (the lower the value on the x-axis), the smaller is the mean absolute value of the gradients reaching it. This illustrates the vanishing gradient problem. When using dropout residual connections, on the other hand, the mean absolute values of the gradients do not vanish proportional to their distance to the loss and are more evenly distributed.

# 3 Related Work

Various stochastic gradient estimators have been proposed to tackle the problem of backpropagating through stochastic nodes. The REINFORCE algorithm [29] utilizes the log derivative trick to estimate score functions. More recent approaches are based on reparameterization tricks [12] that enable the gradient computation by removing the dependence of the density on the input parameters. Maddison et al. [14] and Jang et al. [11] proposed the Gumbel-Softmax distribution, a continuous (but biased) reparameterization trick for categorical distributions. Tucker et al. [27] and Grathwohl et al. [7] introduce parameterized control variates to lower the variance and obtain unbiasedness for these gradient estimators. Recently, Paulus et al. [20] propose a framework that generalizes the Gumbel-Softmax trick for various combinatorial distributions. In contrast to the above, we do not focus on further improving gradient estimators for a single stochastic node but explore the capabilities and difficulties of backpropagating through compositional chains of stochastic nodes. The arithmetic syntax of each training example induces a directed rooted in-tree, from now on called arborescence, which is the tree along which the message-passing steps of a GNN operate. We use the same bi-LSTM encoder as Paulus et al. [20] to compute the logits of all possible edges and the same directed GNN architecture. The original model uses a directed version of Kirchoff's matrix-tree theorem as introduced by Koo et al. [13] to induce the arborescence prior. We simplified this idea by taking, for each node, the Gumbel-softmax over all possible parent nodes. Here we make use of the fact that in an arborescece, each node (with the exception of the root) has exactly one parent. As in Paulus et al. [20], we exclude edges from the final closing bracket since its edge assignment cannot be learned from the task objective.

In contrast to Paulus et al. [20], where only the edges of the latent graph are modeled with categorical variables, we also modelled the nodes of the latent graphs with discrete-continuous components (see Figure 1 (bottom)). Let  $\mathrm{Num} \in \mathbb{R}^{10 \times \dim}$  be the embedding layer that maps the 10 numeral tokens to their respective embeddings and let  $\mathrm{Pred}: \mathbb{R}^{\dim} \to \mathbb{R}^{10}$  be the classification layer that maps the embedding  $\pmb{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{\dim}$  of the final output to the logits of the 10 classes:  $\mathrm{Pred}(\pmb{x}) \coloneqq \mathrm{Lin}^{10 \times \dim}(\mathrm{ReLU}(\mathrm{Lin}_{B}^{\dim \times \dim}(\pmb{x})))$ . After each message-passing operation of the GNN, we obtain an embedding  $\pmb{u} \in \mathbb{R}^{\dim}$  for each node. By choosing  $g_{w} \coloneqq \mathrm{Pred}$ , we obtain the 10 class logits  $\pmb{\theta} = g_{\pmb{w}}(\pmb{u}) = \mathrm{Pred}(\pmb{u}) \in \mathbb{R}^{10}$  for the numerals  $0, \dots, 9$ . Using the Gumbel-softmax trick with logits  $\pmb{\theta}$ , scale  $\beta$ , and temperature  $\tau$ , we obtain  $z \in \mathbb{R}^{10}$ . We then compute  $\pmb{v} = h_{\pmb{w}'}(z) = z^{\top} \mathrm{Num}$ . Multi-Hop Reasoning over Knowledge Graphs Here we consider the problem of answering multi-hop (path) queries in knowledge graphs (KGs) [8]. The objective is to find the correct object (subject) given the subject (object) and a sequence of relations (the path) without knowing the intermediate entities. We evaluate various approaches on the standard benchmarks for path queries [8]. Further experiments on a newly created dataset based on FB15K-237 can be found in the Appendix.

We model each application of a relation type to an (intermediate) entity as a discrete-continuous component that discretely maps to one of the KG entities. The discrete distribution  $p(\boldsymbol{z}; \boldsymbol{\theta})$ , therefore, has as many categories as there are entities in the KG. Without loss of generality, let  $\text{score}(\boldsymbol{s}, \boldsymbol{r}, \boldsymbol{o}) : \mathbb{R}^{3 \times \dim} \to \mathbb{R}$  be a scoring function that maps the embeddings of a triple to the logit (unnormized probability) of the triple being true. Given the current (intermediate) entity embedding  $s$  and relation type embedding  $r$  we compute the logits  $\boldsymbol{\theta}$  for all possible entities. Hence, the function  $g$  computes the logits for all possible entities. Using the Gumbel-softmax trick with parameter  $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ , scale  $\beta$ , and temperature  $\tau$ , we obtain the sample  $z \in \mathbb{R}^n$ . The function  $h$  now computes  $z^\top \mathbf{E}$  where  $\mathbf{E}$  be the matrix whose rows are the entity embeddings. We use dropout residual connection between the input and output vectors of the discrete-continuous component during training. [22], i.e., we choose a dimension and batchsize of 512, a learning rate of 0.001, and a L3 regularization of 0.01. End-to-End Learning of MNIST Addition The MNIST addition task addresses the learning problem of simultaneously (i) recognizing digits from images and (ii) performing the addition operation on the digit's numerical values [15]. The dataset was introduced with DeepProbLog, a system that combines neural elements with a probabilistic logic programming language. In DeepProbLog the program that adds two numbers is provided. We adopt their approach of using a convolutional neural network (CNN) to encode the MNIST images. Contrary to their approach, we learn the operation without any prior knowledge about adding numbers in a data-driven manner.

The addition operation is modeled as  $\mathrm{Add}(\pmb{x},\pmb{y}) = \mathrm{Lin}_B^{\dim \times 2\dim}(\mathrm{ReLU}(\mathrm{Lin}_B^{2\dim \times 2\dim}([x;\pmb{y}]))$  and we use a single linear layer to compute the logits. Given  $\pmb{u}\in \mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{dim}}$ , the encoding of the digit obtained from the CNN, the function  $g$  computes  $\pmb{\theta} = \mathrm{Lin}(\pmb{u})$ , that is, the 19 logits for all possible numbers. Using the Gumbel-softmax trick with parameter  $\pmb{\theta}$ , scale  $\beta$ , and temperature  $\tau$ , we obtain  $\pmb{z}\in \mathbb{R}^{19}$ . The function  $h$  now computes  $\pmb{v} = \pmb{z}^{\intercal}\mathrm{P}\in \mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{dim}}$  where  $\mathrm{P}$  is the weight matrix of Lin. We discretize the two outputs of the CNN, execute the addition layer and use the single linear layer  $\mathrm{P}$  for the classification. This results into a model that has in total less parameters than the baseline in [15]. For the experiments with 3 summands, see Figure 1 and Figure 5, we use the same model with 2 separate addition layers and choose  $\mathrm{P}\in \mathbb{R}^{28\times \dim}$ . Here, we discretize after each CNN encoding as well as after the first addition. We train this model with  $\tau = 8$  and a learning rate of 0.0001.

Table 5 lists the results on this task. Our proposed model, even though trained without a given logic program and in a data-driven manner, achieves the same accuracy as DeepProbLog. Since we use hard sampling during test time, we can discretely read out the outputs of the internal CNN layer. Furthermore, the learned operation is modular and likely able to extrapolate to multiple sequential addition operations.

# 5 Conclusion

We introduced two new strategies to overcome the challenges of learning complex discrete-continuous computation graphs. Such graphs can be effective links between neural and symbolic methods but suffer from vanishing gradients. We propose dropout residual connections and a new temperature matching schema for mitigating the vanishing gradient problem. Note that even with our proposed methods, learning models with discrete nodes is still considerably more challenging than models without. In experiments across a diverse set of domains, we demonstrate that our methods enable the training of complex discrete-continuous models, which could not be learned before. These models beat prior state of the art models without discrete components on several benchmarks and show a remarkable gain in generalization behaviour.
