# Abstract

In this work, we study the problem of robustly estimating the mean/location parameter of distributions without moment bounds. For a large class of distributions satisfying natural symmetry constraints we give a sequence of algorithms that can efficiently estimate its location without incurring dimension-dependent factors in the error. More specifically, suppose an adversary can arbitrarily corrupt an  $\varepsilon$ -fraction of the observed samples. For every  $k \in \mathbb{N}$ , we design an estimator using time and samples  $\tilde{O}(d^k)$  such that the dependence of the error on the corruption level  $\varepsilon$  is an additive factor of  $O(\varepsilon^{1 - \frac{1}{2k}})$ . The dependence on other problem parameters is also nearly optimal. In particular, our class contains products of arbitrary symmetric one-dimensional distributions as well as elliptical distributions, a vast generalization of the Gaussian distribution. Examples include product Cauchy distributions and multi-variate  $t$ -distributions. We stress that we do not make any assumptions on the moments of the distribution and even the first moment might not exist.

We provide the first efficient algorithms for this class of distributions. Previously, such results where only known under boundedness assumptions on the moments of the distribution and in particular, are provably impossible in the absence of symmetry [KSS18, CTBJ22]. For the class of distributions we consider, all previous estimators either require exponential time or incur error depending on the dimension. Our algorithms are based on a generalization of the well-known filtering technique [DK22]. More specifically, we show how this machinery can be combined with Huber-loss-based techniques to work with projections of the noise that behave more nicely than the initial noise. Moreover, we show how sum-of-squares proofs can be used to obtain algorithmic guarantees even for distributions without first moment. We believe that this approach may find other application in future works.

# 1 Introduction

In recent years, there has been a flurry of works proposing efficient algorithms, i.e., whose running time is polynomial in the dimension and all relevant problem parameters, for a variety of different estimation problems [MSS16a, DKK $^{+}$ 19, HL18, KSS18, dKNS20, DKK $^{+}$ 22b, DKK $^{+}$ 22a]. Arguably one of the most fundamental problems in this class is robustly estimating the mean of a multivariate distribution. As a starting point: In the special case of the multi-variate Gaussian distribution the problem is by now very well understood from an algorithmic point of view [DKK $^{+}$ 19, KMZ22, DKS17]. There has been a substantial line of work extending these results to more heavy-tailed settings, in which only a limited number of moments exist. In particular, prior works have achieved this when making assumptions on the boundedness of moments of the distributions or when the distribution is fixed to be, e.g., the multi-variate Gaussian distribution [HL18, KSS18, CDG19, DHL19, DKP20, CTBJ22]. Extending these results to larger classes of distributions is an important open problem. In particular, the above works crucially rely on the bounded moment assumptions and show that in absence of those there are statistical lower bounds strictly separating the achievable error from that in the Gaussian case [KSS18, CTBJ22]. Contrary to this intuition, in this work we consider even more heavy-tailed distributions for which potentially not even the first moment exists. Surprisingly, we are able to show that we can circumvent the above lower bounds by making natural symmetry assumptions on our data, e.g., that the distribution is elliptical. In fact, we show that in this case we can achieve guarantees approaching those of a Gaussian distribution even when no moments exist! We achieve this by proposing a generalization of the well-known filtering technique<sup>1</sup> that achieves close-to-optimal guarantees under bounded moment assumptions. Our generalization can be thought of as suitably truncating the noise distribution via the Huber loss [Hub92]. Moreover, we show how to use sum-of-squares certificates in order to obtain algorithmic guarantees for symmetric distributions without any moment assumptions.

Symmetric distributions are a well-studied class of distributions in statistics with many applications to, e.g., mathematical finance and risk management [Lin75, OR83, Cha83]. A large sub-family of symmetric distributions that are of particular interest are elliptical distributions [Kel70, CHS81, Fan18], which are transformations of spherically symmetric distributions. This is a vast generalization of the multi-variate Gaussian distribution. It includes (very) heavy-tailed distributions which might have no moments. For example, multi-variate  $t$ -distributions, in particular, including multi-variate Cauchy distributions, multi-variate Laplace distributions, and  $\alpha$ -stable sub-Gaussian distributions. For a detailed overview on the topic we refer to, e.g., [Fan18]. For such distributions the mean might not exist. The appropriate generalization is to consider the location parameter  $\mu^{*}$  of the distribution. For us, the following parametrization will be convenient: We can describe a distribution  $D_{\mu^{*}}$  with location parameter  $\mu^{*}$  as  $\mu^{*} + \eta$ , where  $\eta \sim D$  for some distribution  $D$  which is symmetric about 0 in a sense made precise below. In particular, when  $D$  does have a first moment, this would be 0 and  $D_{\mu^{*}}$  will have mean  $\mu^{*}$ . We are interested in designing computationally efficient estimators for estimating  $\mu^{*}$  that are robust against adversarial corruptions of the observed sample. While non-robust estimators achieving statistically optimal guarantees are extremely simple to describe (e.g. the coordinate-wise median works), to the best of our knowledge, all previously known robust estimators either incur error depending on the dimension or require

computation time exponential in the dimension. In this work, we show that assuming symmetry, we can achieve error guarantees paralleling in many ways those which are efficiently achievable when assuming the distribution has bounded moments up to some order.

Problem Set-up and Symmetric Noise Models. On a high level, our setting is the following: Let  $\mu^{*}$  and  $D$  be as above and consider  $n$  i.i.d. samples  $\mathbf{y}_1^* = \boldsymbol{\mu}^* + \boldsymbol{\eta}_1^*, \dots, \mathbf{y}_n^* = \boldsymbol{\mu}^* + \boldsymbol{\eta}_n^*$ , where  $\boldsymbol{\eta}_i^* \sim D$ . In the robust setting we consider, we observe an  $\varepsilon$ -corruption of  $\{\mathbf{y}_1^*, \dots, \mathbf{y}_n^*\}$ . That is, an adversary with full knowledge of  $\mu^{*}, D$  and our algorithm, can inspect  $\{\mathbf{y}_1^*, \dots, \mathbf{y}_n^*\}$  an arbitrarily exchange an  $\varepsilon$ -fraction of the points. We observe the resulting data set  $\{y_1, \dots, y_n\}$  and our goal is to faithfully recover  $\mu^{*}$ . Note that without corruptions estimating  $\mu^{*}$  would indeed be simple: Under the mild assumption that, along each direction,  $D$  has at least some probability mass around 0, a simple Chernoff bound argument shows that the coordinate-wise median estimator can estimate  $\mu^{*}$  up to error  $\sqrt{\frac{d + \log(1 / \delta)}{n}}$  with probability at least  $1 - \delta$ . This is information-theoretically optimal even for the special case of the standard Gaussian distribution. However, it is known that in the robust setting, this estimate incurs an error of  $\Omega(\varepsilon \sqrt{d})$  in the worst case [CGR18].

We consider two classes of symmetric distributions: For intuition, it might help to set the parameter  $\rho = 1$  in the definition below. The first one, is a generalization of product distributions whose components are symmetric about 0. Note that it contains distributions which are not elliptical and very heavy-tailed, e.g., the product Cauchy distribution which does not even have a first moment.

Definition 1.1. Let  $0 < \alpha < 1, 0 < \rho$ , and  $\mu^{*} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ . We say a distribution  $D$  over  $\mathbb{R}^{d}$  is an  $(\alpha, \rho)$ -semi-product distribution, if for  $\eta \sim D$  it holds that

1. For all  $j \in [d]$ , the distribution of  $\pmb{\eta}_j$  is symmetric about 0,  
2. for all  $j \in [d]$ ,  $\mathbb{P}\big(|\pmb{\eta}_j| \leqslant \rho \big) \geqslant \alpha$ ,  
3. the random variables  $\mathrm{sign}(\eta_1),\ldots ,\mathrm{sign}(\eta_d)$  are mutually independent and independent from the vector  $\left(|\pmb {\eta}_j|\right)_{j = 1}^d$

Property 2 quantifies the amount of probability mass close to the origin. Note that without this, estimating  $\mu^{*}$  up to small accuracy is impossible. Property 3 is a weakening of assuming that the coordinates of  $\eta$  are independent.

The second one is the class of elliptical distributions [Kel70, CHS81, Fan18] which in particular can have more complicated dependence structures than semi-product distributions. For intuition, it will be instructive to keep in mind the centered Gaussian distribution.

Definition 1.2. We say that a distribution  $D$  over  $\mathbb{R}^d$  is elliptical, if for  $\eta \sim D$  the following holds: Let  $U$  be uniformly distributed over the  $d$ -dimensional unit sphere. There exists a positive random variable  $R$ , independent of  $U$ , and a positive semi-definite matrix  $\Sigma$ , such that the following holds

$$
\boldsymbol {\eta} = R \Sigma^ {1 / 2} \boldsymbol {U} \quad \mathrm {a n d} \quad \mathbb {P} \Big (R \leqslant \sqrt {2 d} \Big) \geqslant \frac {1}{2}.
$$

We make a couple of remarks: We call  $\Sigma$  the scatter matrix of the distribution<sup>2</sup>. The first property is the standard definition of a random vector following an elliptical distribution. Note however,

that without further restrictions this only identifies  $R$  and  $\Sigma$  up to scale. The second property fixes this scaling. In particular, with this scaling, for the Gaussian distribution the scatter matrix is equal to the covariance matrix. For example, the Gaussian distribution satisfies the above definition: Indeed, we can decompose  $X \sim N(0, \Sigma)$  as  $X = R\Sigma^{1/2}U$ , where  $R$  is distributed as the square root of a  $\chi^2$ -distribution with  $d$  degrees of freedom and  $U$  is independent of  $R$  and distributed uniformly over the unit sphere. Then by Markov's Inequality, it holds that  $\mathbb{P}\big(X \leqslant \sqrt{2d}\big) = \mathbb{P}\big(X^2 \leqslant 2d\big) \geqslant \frac{1}{2}$ . Note that similar scalings have been used in the literature before [CGR18].

Results. We remark that both of our main theorems will follow from an instantiation of the same framework discussed in Section 2. We believe that it is likely that it can also be applied to other kind of symmetric noise distributions as defined above. For semi-product distributions we obtain

Theorem 1.3. Let  $C > 0$  be a large enough absolute constant. Let  $k \in \mathbb{N}$ ,  $\alpha, \rho, \varepsilon, \delta > 0$ ,  $\mu^{*} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$  such that  $\varepsilon^{1 - \frac{1}{2k}} \leqslant \frac{\alpha}{C}$  and assume  $D$  is an  $(\alpha, \rho)$ -semi-product distribution. Also, let  $n \geqslant C \cdot \frac{1}{\alpha^{2}} \cdot d^{k} \cdot \log(d / \delta)$ . There is an algorithm running in time  $n^{O(1)} d^{O(k)}$  that given an  $\varepsilon$ -corruption of samples  $\mathbf{y}_{1}^{*} = \boldsymbol{\mu}^{*} + \boldsymbol{\eta}_{1}^{*}, \ldots, \mathbf{y}_{n}^{*} = \boldsymbol{\mu}^{*} + \boldsymbol{\eta}_{n}^{*}$  and  $\rho, \gamma$  with probability at least  $1 - \delta$  outputs  $\hat{\mu} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$  satisfying

$$
\left\| \hat {\mu} - \mu^ {*} \right\| \leqslant O \left(\rho \cdot \left(\sqrt {\frac {d + \log (1 / \delta)}{\alpha^ {2} n}} + \frac {\varepsilon^ {1 - 1 / (2 k)}}{\alpha}\right)\right).
$$

And for elliptical distributions, we obtain

Theorem 1.4. Let  $C > 0$  be a large enough absolute constant. Let  $k \in \mathbb{N}$ ,  $\varepsilon, \delta > 0$ ,  $\mu^{*} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$  such that  $\varepsilon \leqslant \frac{1}{C}$  and assume  $D$  is an elliptical distribution with scatter matrix  $\Sigma$ . Also, let  $n \geqslant C \cdot d^{k} \cdot \log(d / \delta)$ . There is an algorithm running in time  $n^{O(1)} d^{O(k)}$  that given an  $\varepsilon$ -corruption of samples  $\pmb{y}_{1}^{*} = \mu^{*} + \pmb{\eta}_{1}^{*}, \dots, \pmb{y}_{n}^{*} = \mu^{*} + \pmb{\eta}_{n}^{*}$  with probability at least  $1 - \delta$  outputs  $\hat{\mu} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$  satisfying

$$
\left\| \hat {\mu} - \mu^ {*} \right\| \leqslant O \left(\sqrt {\frac {\mathrm {T r} (\Sigma) \cdot \log (d / \delta)}{n}} + \sqrt {k} \cdot \varepsilon^ {1 - 1 / (2 k)} \cdot \sqrt {\| \Sigma \|}\right).
$$

Note, that in this case, we do not assume that the scatter matrix  $\Sigma$  is known to the algorithm. All parameters relevant to the algorithm can be estimated from the corrupted sample (cf. Section 8.1).

Comparison with Statistical Lower Bounds. Some remarks are in order. We first compare our results to information-theoretic lower bounds. Regarding semi-product distributions: Note that  $N(0,\rho \cdot I_d)$  is a  $(\Theta (1),\rho)$ -semi-product distribution. Hence, lower bounds for the Gaussian distribution apply. In fact, it is known that in this case, the statistically optimal error is  $\Omega \left(\rho \cdot \left(\sqrt{\frac{d + \log(1 / \delta)}{n}} +\varepsilon\right)\right)$  [CGR18]. We give a sequence of polynomial-time algorithms (nearly) approaching this for all such semi-product distributions, and for  $k = \log (1 / \varepsilon)$  we get a quasi-polynomial-time algorithm that achieves it exactly. In fact, there is evidence that super-polynomial-time is inherent for achieving optimal error [DKS17]. We also remark that our dependence on the failure probability is optimal (up to constant factors).

Concerning elliptical distributions, we note that even for the special case when  $D = N(0,\Sigma)$ , the information-theoretically optimal rate is  $O\left(\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{Tr}\Sigma + \|\Sigma\|\log(1 / \delta)}{n}} + \varepsilon \cdot \sqrt{\|\Sigma\|}\right)$  [CGR18]. Again, considering the robust part of the error, we give a sequence of (quasi)-polynomial-time algorithms (nearly) approaching the optimal robust error. In fact, for  $k = \log(1 / \varepsilon)$ , our error scales additively with  $O\left(\varepsilon \sqrt{\log(1 / \varepsilon)} \cdot \sqrt{\|\Sigma\|}\right)$ , which is optimal up to the factor of  $\sqrt{\log(1 / \varepsilon)}$ . We match the non-robust error term up to a factor of  $\log(d / \delta)$  which likely can be improved. In particular, we believe that the dependence on the failure probability can be improved. Hence, we give a sequence of polynomial-time algorithms with guarantees approaching those possible for the Gaussian but which work for a much larger class of distributions.

We also remark that for the semi-product case, the relation between  $\alpha$  and  $\varepsilon$  is close to optimal. In fact, for each coordinate of the observed samples, for roughly an  $(1 - \alpha)$ -fraction the noise introduced by  $\eta_i^*$  can be arbitrarily large. Hence, when  $\varepsilon \geqslant \alpha$ , the adversary could corrupt the remaining  $\alpha$ -fraction of the samples and completely erase all information about this coordinate.

Comparison with Inefficient Algorithms. To the best of our knowledge, the following results about computationally inefficient estimators are known. [CGR18] shows that the Tukey median achieves error  $O\left(\sqrt{\frac{d + \log(1 / \delta)}{n}} + \varepsilon\right)$  for elliptical distributions as long as the spectral norm of  $\Sigma$  is bounded by a constant. Second [PBR20] show how to combine one-directional medians to obtain a robust high-dimensional estimator for a large class of symmetric distributions including the ones studied in this paper. However, already for the multi-variate Gaussian distribution  $N(0, \Sigma)$  they have a sub-optimal dependence on some parameters. There estimator incurs error  $O\left(\sqrt{\|\Sigma\|} \cdot \left(\sqrt{d + \log(1 / \delta)} + \varepsilon\right)\right)$ . Note that while the robust error is indeed optimal, the term  $O\left(\sqrt{\frac{\|\Sigma\|d}{n}}\right)$  is potentially much larger than  $O\left(\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{Tr}\Sigma}{n}}\right)$ . Both algorithms rely on brute force techniques for which it is unclear how to make them efficient.

Comparison to Other Results Employing the Filtering Technique. Our main technical contribution will be to work in a region where the noise distribution is suitably transformed and in particular, has bounded moments of all kinds. Using this transformation, we can access existing techniques for robust mean estimation under bounded moment assumptions (cf. Section 2 for more details). We summarize the main existing results here (for more detail, we refer the reader to [DK22]). In what follows we follow mainly on how the error guarantees depend on  $\varepsilon$  and put other parameters, such as the dependence on the failure probability aside. In particular, under the assumption that  $D$  has  $2k$ -th moment bounded by some  $\sigma_{2k}^{2k}$  in every direction<sup>5</sup>, i.e., for all unit vectors  $v$  it holds that<sup>6</sup>  $\mathbb{E}_{\eta \sim D}\langle \eta ,v\rangle^{2k} \leqslant \sigma_{2k}^{2k}$ . Then it is known how to recover  $\mu^*$  up to error  $\left\| (\hat{\mu} -\mu^{*})\right\| \leqslant O\left(\sigma_{k}\varepsilon^{1 - 1 / (2k)}\right)$  [HL18, DK22]. Under the stronger assumption that  $D$  has covariance  $\Sigma$  and satisfies for every unit vector  $v$  it holds that  $\mathbb{E}_{\eta \sim D}\langle \eta ,v\rangle^{2k} \leqslant O(k\cdot \mathbb{E}_{\eta \sim D}\langle \eta ,u\rangle^{2})^{k}$  it is possible to achieve the stronger error guarantee  $\left\| \Sigma^{-1 / 2}(\hat{\mu} -\mu^{*})\right\| \leqslant O\left(\sqrt{k}\varepsilon^{1 - 1 / (2k)}\right)$  [KSS18, ST21]. We remark

that additionally one needs a certificate of this moment bound in form of a sum-of-squares proof, we discuss this in more detail in Section 6. We remark that for the class of Gaussian distributions  $N(0,\Sigma)$ , it is known how to achieve (nearly) optimal error of  $\| \Sigma^{-1 / 2}(\hat{\mu} -\mu^{*})\| \leqslant O(\varepsilon \sqrt{\log(1 / \varepsilon)})$  [DKK+19, KMZ22]. Further, for sub-gaussian distribution with covariance identity, it is known how to achieve error  $O(\varepsilon \sqrt{\log(1 / \varepsilon)})$ , in  $\ell_2$ -norm, in polynomial time [DHL19].

As in Theorems 1.3 and 1.4, we can achieve stronger guarantees if we are willing to invest more time and samples. Most notably, we are able to quantitatively match the above results in the dependence on  $\varepsilon$  even when the first moment might not exist. In fact, an error of  $\omega (\varepsilon^{1 - 1 / (2k)})$  is unavoidable if we do not have bounded  $2k$ -th moments [KSS18]. While this holds true in all generality, we show that if the distribution is symmetric, you can achieve guarantees all the way up to the (near) optimal error. We remark that our guarantees only hold in the standard  $\ell_2$ -norm and not in the stronger norm scaled by  $\Sigma^{-1 / 2}$ .

For the special case of bounded covariance distributions, there have been a sequence of works which designed estimators achieving statistically (near-) optimal guarantees and with fast running times [CDG19, DHL19]. In contrast, in this paper we focussed on designing the first polynomial time algorithm for our settings. We leave it as an open problem to improve the running time. In particular, we believe the techniques of [DHL19] should be extendable to this setting as well.

Comparison to Other Known Results about Robust Mean Estimation. [CTBJ22] considers the set-up in which the second moment of  $D$  might not exist. More specifically, they assume that for some  $\gamma \in [0,1]$  and all unit vectors  $v$  it holds that  $\mathbb{E}_{\eta \sim D}|\langle \pmb{\eta},v\rangle|^{1 + \alpha} \leqslant 1$ . Under this condition, they give an efficient algorithm estimating the mean up to error  $O\left(\left(\frac{d}{n}\right)^{\alpha /(1 + \alpha)} + \left(\frac{\log(1 / \delta)}{n}\right)^{\alpha /(1 + \alpha)} + \varepsilon^{\alpha /(1 + \alpha)}\right)$  and give a matching lower bound. As an application of their algorithm they mention multi-variate-  $t$  distributions, which in fact are also elliptical. Hence, already for  $k = 2$ , Theorem 1.4 achieves a superior error in terms of the dependence on  $\varepsilon$ .

Other Results On Symmetric Noise Distributions. We remark that there has been a line of work studying estimation problems with very similar noise models to our semi-product distributions [dNS21, dLN+21, dNNS23]. These works are also based on the Huber loss.

# 2 Techniques

Before describing our algorithm, we first recall the formal set-up: Let  $D$  be some distribution over  $\mathbb{R}^d$  which has mean, or location if the mean does not exist, equal to  $0$ ,  $\mu^{*}\in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ , and  $\varepsilon >0$  (all unknown to the algorithm designer). Let  $\eta_1^*,\ldots ,\eta_n^*$  be  $n$  i.i.d. samples from  $D$  and let  $y_{i}^{*} = \mu^{*} + \eta_{i}^{*}$ . An adversary, with full knowledge of our algorithm, can arbitrarily change an  $\varepsilon$ -fraction of  $y_{1}^{*},\ldots ,y_{n}^{*}$  and we observe the resulting vectors  $y_{1},\ldots ,y_{n}$ . Our goal is to recover  $\mu^{*}$ . At the core, robust mean estimation algorithms [HL18, KSS18, ST21, DK22] relying on bounded moment assumptions use the following statement of identifiability: Suppose  $D$  and  $D^{\prime}$  are two distributions which have total variation distance at most  $\varepsilon$  and both have  $2k$ -th moments bounded by some  $\sigma_{2k}$  in every direction. I.e., for every unit vector  $v\in \mathbb{R}^d$  it holds that  $\mathbb{E}_{\eta \sim D}\langle \eta ,v\rangle^k\leqslant \sigma_{2k}^{2k}$ . and analogously for  $D^{\prime}$ . Then their means can be at most  $O\left(\sigma_{2k}\cdot \varepsilon^{1 - 1 / (2k)}\right)$  far apart. This remains true, when considering

empirical distribution over samples. It is instructive to consider the case when the corruptions by the adversary have not increased these directional moments of the empirical distribution. Then, by the above argument, there is nothing to be done and we can just return the sample mean of the corrupted points! If this is not the case, we can iteratively filter our points so that the empirical moments satisfy the boundedness condition.<sup>7</sup>

In this work, we show that a very similar identifiability statement still holds true if we consider distributions which might not even have a first moment. In fact, we show that if we replace the standard moments by the moments of a suitably chosen bounded transformation of the noise distribution, there exists a vector  $\hat{\mu}$ , efficiently computable from the data, that is close to  $\mu^{*}$ . This allows us to use the very same ideas from the above filtering algorithm but applied to the transformed moments. Both Theorems 1.3 and 1.4 will follow by this approach. We next describe the general idea on an abstract level and instantiate it with semi-product and elliptical distributions after.

# 2.1 Proof of Identifiability

We consider the setting in which  $D$  might not have any moments, but satisfies symmetry conditions (cf. Definitions 1.1 and 1.2). Our starting point is the following simple observation: Suppose we apply a bounded transformation  $f\colon \mathbb{R}^d\to \mathbb{R}^{d8}$ , e.g., a projection to a compact set, to the noise distribution  $D$ , then moments up to all orders would exist. Hence, if we would instead of corruptions of  $y_{i} = \mu^{*} + \eta_{i}$  observe corruptions of  $y_{i} = \mu^{*} + f(\eta_{i})$ , we could apply the standard algorithms based on moment bounds. Of course, it is not clear how such transformed samples can be obtained. In fact, this might be just as hard as estimating  $\mu^{*}$ . However, we can still try to apply the same ideas from the proof of identifiability in the bounded moment case.

For this, we first revisit this proof in more detail. For simplicity, we describe the setting in which  $D$  has mean  $\mu^{*}$  and bounded second moments but need not satisfy any symmetry constraints. The extension to higher-order moments will be discussed in the next section. We assume that the distributions correspond to empirical distributions over  $\{y_1^*,\ldots ,y_n^*\}$  and  $\{y_{1},\dots ,y_{n}\}$ . Let  $\hat{\mu} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i = 1}^{n}y_{i}$  and

$$
\tilde {\Sigma} = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(\boldsymbol {\mu} ^ {*} - \boldsymbol {y} _ {i} ^ {*}\right) \left(\boldsymbol {\mu} ^ {*} - \boldsymbol {y} _ {i} ^ {*}\right) ^ {\top} = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {*} \boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {* \top} \quad \mathrm {a n d} \quad \hat {\Sigma} = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(\hat {\boldsymbol {\mu}} - y _ {i}\right) \left(\hat {\boldsymbol {\mu}} - y _ {i}\right) ^ {\top}.
$$

Further, define  $\tilde{g} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_i^*$ . Our goal will be to show that for  $\gamma = 1$

$$
\frac {\gamma}{2} \Big \| \mu^ {*} - \hat {\mu} \Big \| \leqslant O (\| \tilde {g} \| + \sqrt {\varepsilon} \cdot (\sqrt {\| \tilde {\Sigma} \|} + \sqrt {\| \hat {\Sigma} \|})).
$$

Note that  $\| \tilde{g} \|$  can be upper bounded by  $\sqrt{\frac{d\log d}{n}}$  using standard concentration bounds. We choose this formulation since it will be easier to generalize. Note, that if both  $\tilde{\Sigma}$  and  $\hat{\Sigma}$  had bounded spectral norm, we would recover the guarantees of algorithms exploiting bounded second moments. In fact,

the obtained guarantees depend heavily on the bound of these spectral norms and how quickly  $\left\| \tilde{g}\right\|$  goes to 0 when  $n$  goes to infinity.

In what follows we will give a very abstract proof of the above statement. As we will see, this approach will allow us to extend the same ideas also to cases in which no moments exist. In particular, let  $F\colon \mathbb{R}^d\to \mathbb{R}$  and  $f = \nabla F$  and define

$$
\tilde {\Sigma} _ {f} = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} f (\boldsymbol {\mu} ^ {*} - \boldsymbol {y} _ {i} ^ {*}) f (\boldsymbol {\mu} ^ {*} - \boldsymbol {y} _ {i} ^ {*}) ^ {\top} = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} f (\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {*}) f (\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {*}) ^ {\top} \quad \mathrm {a n d} \quad \hat {\Sigma} _ {f} = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} f (\hat {\boldsymbol {\mu}} - \boldsymbol {y} _ {i}) f (\hat {\boldsymbol {\mu}} - \boldsymbol {y} _ {i}) ^ {\top}.
$$

Similarly, let  $\tilde{g}_f = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n f(\boldsymbol{\eta}_i^*)$ . We will choose  $F$  and  $f$  based on the class of distributions at hand. At this stage, when second moment exists, we set  $F(x) = \frac{1}{2}\|x\|^2$  and hence  $f(x) = x$ . It follows that  $\tilde{\Sigma}_f = \tilde{\Sigma}$  and  $\hat{\Sigma}_f = \hat{\Sigma}$ . For distributions where no moments exist, we will later choose  $F$  such that  $f$  is a projection onto a compact set allowing us to control the spectral norm of  $\tilde{\Sigma}_f$ .

Observe, that with these definitions, we can describe  $\hat{\mu}$  as the least-squares solution, i.e., for

$$
\mathcal {L} (\mu) := \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} F (\mu - y _ {i}),
$$

it holds that  $\hat{\mu} = \min_{\mu \in \mathbb{R}^d}\mathcal{L}(\mu)$ . Observe, that since  $F$  is strongly convex with parameter  $\gamma = 1$ , so is  $\mathcal{L}$ . Hence, if we let  $u = \mu^{*} - \hat{\mu}$  it holds that

$$
\frac {\gamma}{2} \| u \| ^ {2} \leqslant \left\langle \nabla \mathcal {L} \left(\mu^ {*}\right), u \right\rangle + \mathcal {L} (\hat {\mu}) - \mathcal {L} \left(\mu^ {*}\right) \leqslant \left\langle \nabla \mathcal {L} \left(\mu^ {*}\right), u \right\rangle .
$$

Hence, our goal will be to upper bound this inner product.

Note that  $\nabla \mathcal{L}(\mu) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(\mu - y_i)$ . Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality twice and the fact that at most an  $\varepsilon$ -fraction of the observed samples has been corrupted, we continue our proof

$$
\begin{array}{l} \left\langle \nabla \mathcal {L} \left(\mu^ {*}\right), u \right\rangle = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left\langle f \left(\mu^ {*} - y _ {i}\right), u \right\rangle \\ = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left\langle f \left(\mu^ {*} - \boldsymbol {y} _ {i} ^ {*}\right), u \right\rangle + \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \mathbf {1} \left\{y _ {i} \neq \boldsymbol {y} _ {i} ^ {*} \right\} \cdot \left\langle f \left(\mu^ {*} - y _ {i}\right) - f \left(\mu^ {*} - \boldsymbol {y} _ {i} ^ {*}\right), u \right\rangle \\ \leqslant \left\| \tilde {g} _ {f} \right\| \cdot \| u \| + \sqrt {\varepsilon} \cdot \sqrt {\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left\langle f \left(\mu^ {*} - y _ {i}\right) - f \left(\tilde {\mu} - y _ {i} ^ {*}\right) , u \right\rangle^ {2}}. \tag {2.1} \\ \end{array}
$$

We next bound using the triangle inequality

$$
\begin{array}{l} \sqrt {\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left\langle f \left(\mu^ {*} - y _ {i}\right) - f \left(\mu^ {*} - \mathbf {y} _ {i} ^ {*}\right) , u \right\rangle^ {2}} \\ \leqslant \sqrt {\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left\langle f \left(\mu^ {*} - y _ {i} ^ {*}\right) , u \right\rangle^ {2}} + \sqrt {\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left\langle f \left(\hat {\mu} - y _ {i}\right) , u \right\rangle^ {2}} + \sqrt {\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left\langle f \left(\mu^ {*} - y _ {i}\right) - f \left(\hat {\mu} - y _ {i}\right) , u \right\rangle^ {2}} \\ \leqslant \| u \| \cdot \left[ \sqrt {\| \tilde {\Sigma} _ {f} \|} + \sqrt {\| \hat {\Sigma} _ {f} \|} + \sqrt {\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left\| f (\mu^ {*} - y _ {i}) - f (\hat {\mu} - y _ {i}) \right\| ^ {2}} \right]. \\ \end{array}
$$

Notice that since  $f$  is 1-Lipschitz, we can upper bound the last square root in the bracket by  $\| u \|$ . Putting all of the above together, we have shown that

$$
\frac {\gamma}{2} \| u \| ^ {2} \leqslant \| u \| \cdot \left[ \left\| \tilde {g} _ {f} \right\| + \sqrt {\varepsilon} \cdot \left(\sqrt {\| \tilde {\Sigma} _ {f} \|} + \sqrt {\| \hat {\Sigma} _ {f} \|} + \| u \|\right) \right].
$$

Hence, rearranging yields the desired conclusion.

Extension to Distributions Without Moments. In this section, we will extend the proof above to the setting when  $D$  is either a semi-product or elliptical distribution (cf. Definitions 1.1 and 1.2). In particular, our goal will be to cleverly choose  $F, f$  such that the above proof applies to these settings. In order to do this, we first give a short list of all the properties the above proof of identifiability used. We then identify suitable choices for the semi-product and elliptical setting.

1.  $\mathcal{L}$  is strongly convex with parameter  $\gamma^{10}$ ,  
2. the spectral norm of  $\tilde{\Sigma}_f$  and  $\hat{\Sigma}_f$  is bounded,  
3. an upper bound for  $\| \tilde{g}_f\| = \left\| \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i = 1}^n f(\pmb {\eta}_i^*)\right\|$  
4.  $f$  is Lipschitz continuous.

A priori, Properties 1 and 2 seem to be in conflict with each other: By strong convexity,  $\mathcal{L}$  and hence also  $F$  must have an at least quadratic lower bound. Hence,  $f$  grows at least linearly. But since our distribution might not even have first moments, this seems to contradict Property 2. One key idea for us is that we do not need strong convexity to hold globally, but in fact it is enough if it holds in a bounded region around  $\mu^{*}$ . Then we only need to argue that the minimizer  $\hat{\mu}$  also falls in this region. Hence, we can choose  $f$  such that it is linear near the origin and sub-linear in the tails. We remark that similar ideas have been used in, e.g., [dNS21]. Indeed, we will choose transformations based on the Huber loss [Hub92]. Let  $h > 0$  then we define  $\Phi_h\colon \mathbb{R}\to \mathbb{R}$  and its derivative  $\phi_h$

$$
\Phi_ {h} (x) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \frac {1}{2} x ^ {2}, & \text {i f} | x | \leqslant h  , \\ h \cdot \left(| x | - \frac {1}{2} h\right)  , & \text {o t h e r w i s e .} \end{array} \right. \quad \text {a n d} \quad \phi_ {h} (x) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} x  , & \text {i f} | x | \leqslant h  , \\ h \cdot \operatorname {s i g n} (x)  , & \text {o t h e r w i s e .} \end{array} \right.
$$

Semi-Product Distributions. First, let  $D$  be a semi-product distributions (cf. Definition 1.1) with parameters  $(\alpha, \rho)$ . For simplicity, we assume that the distribution is indeed an actual product distribution. All arguments readily generalize to the general case without difficulty. Based on the intuition of the preceding paragraph, we choose  $f$  as the projection to the  $\ell_{\infty}$ -ball of radius  $2\rho$ . I.e., for  $j \in [d]$ , we define  $f(x)_j = \phi_{2\rho}(x_j)$ . Note that in this case  $F(x) = \sum_{j=1}^{d} \Phi_{2\rho}(x_j)$  and  $\mathcal{L}(\mu) = \sum_{j=1}^{d} \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \Phi_{2\rho}(\mu_j - (y_i)_j)$ .

Clearly,  $f$  is 1-Lipschitz and hence Property 4 is satisfied. Also note, that we can upper bound

$$
\tilde {\Sigma} _ {f} = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} f \left(\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {*}\right) f \left(\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {*}\right) ^ {\top} \approx \underset {\boldsymbol {\eta} \sim D} {\mathbb {E}} f (\boldsymbol {\eta}) f (\boldsymbol {\eta}) ^ {\top} \preceq \rho \cdot I _ {d}.
$$

Also note that by independence of the coordinates and that each one is symmetric around 0, it follows using a Chernoff bound that  $\left\| \tilde{g}_f\right\| = \left\| \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i = 1}^{n}f(\boldsymbol{\eta}_i^*)\right\| \leqslant O\left(\rho \cdot \sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\right)$ .

Lastly, we argue that  $\mathcal{L}$  is locally  $\alpha$ -strongly convex in an  $\ell_{\infty}$ -ball of radius  $\rho$  around  $\mu^{*}$ . In Section 7, we show that the minimizer falls indeed into this radius. We want to show that for  $u$  such that  $\| u \|_{\infty} \leqslant \rho$  it holds that  $\mathcal{L}(\mu^{*} + u) - \mathcal{L}(\mu^{*}) - \left\langle \nabla \mathcal{L}(\mu^{*}), u \right\rangle \geqslant \frac{\alpha}{2} \cdot \| u \|^{2}$ . In fact, rewriting the above, we obtain

$$
\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {d} \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \Phi_ {2 \rho} \Big (\mu_ {j} ^ {*} + u _ {j} - (y _ {i}) _ {j} \Big) - \Phi_ {2 \rho} \Big (\mu_ {j} ^ {*} - (y _ {i}) _ {j} \Big) - u _ {j} \cdot \phi_ {2 \rho} (\mu_ {j} ^ {*}) \geqslant \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {d} \frac {\alpha}{2} \cdot u _ {j} ^ {2}.
$$

Hence, it is sufficient to show that for all  $j \in [d]$ , the 1-dimensional functions  $x_{j} \mapsto \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i = 1}^{n}\Phi_{\rho}\big(x_{j} - (y_{i})_{j}\big)$  are  $\alpha$ -strongly convex as long as  $|u_{j}| \leqslant \rho$ . For  $j \in [d]$ , let  $M_{j}$  be the set of uncorrupted indices  $i$ , such that  $|(\eta_i)_j| \leqslant \rho$ . It follows that for  $i \in M_j$  all relevant quantities fall into the quadratic part of the Huber loss. By Property 2 of semi-product distribution and a Chernoff bound it follows that  $M_{j}$  has size roughly  $\alpha n$ . Since  $\Phi_{\rho}$  is convex, we can lower bound

$$
\begin{array}{l} \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left[ \Phi_ {2 \rho} \left(\mu_ {j} ^ {*} + u _ {j} - (y _ {i}) _ {j}\right) - \Phi_ {2 \rho} \left(\mu_ {j} ^ {*} - (y _ {i}) _ {j}\right) - u _ {j} \cdot \phi_ {2 \rho} \left(\mu_ {j} ^ {*} - (y _ {i}) _ {j}\right) \right] \\ \geqslant \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i \in M _ {j}} \left[ \frac {1}{2} \left((\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {*}) _ {j} + u _ {j}\right) ^ {2} - \frac {1}{2} (\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {*}) _ {j} ^ {2} - u _ {j} (\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {*}) _ {j} \right] = \frac {| M _ {j} |}{2 n} \cdot u _ {j} ^ {2} \approx \frac {\alpha}{2} \cdot u _ {j} ^ {2}. \\ \end{array}
$$

It follows that  $\hat{\mu} = \min_{\mu \in \mathbb{R}^d}\mathcal{L}(\mu)$  satisfies  $\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\| \mu^{*} - \hat{\mu}\right\| \leqslant O\left(\rho \cdot \sqrt{\frac{d}{n}} +\sqrt{\varepsilon}\cdot \left(\rho +\| \hat{\Sigma}_f\|\right)\right)$  which looks already very close to the guarantees of Theorem 1.3 for  $k = 1$

Elliptical Distributions. Note that the proof above does not extend to elliptical distributions (cf. Definition 1.2), since it is unclear to bound the spectral norm of  $\hat{\Sigma}_f$ . In fact if  $\eta$  follows an elliptical distribution, it is unclear how to bound the spectral norm of the simpler object  $\mathbb{E}\mathrm{sign}(\eta)\mathrm{sign}(\eta)^{\top}$  as a function of  $\Sigma$ . However, fortunately for us, the spatial sign, i.e.,  $\frac{\eta}{\|\eta\|}$  is much better understood [DTV16a, DFV16]. In fact, it holds that  $\| \mathbb{E}\frac{\eta\eta^{\top}}{\|\eta\|^{2}}\| \leqslant \frac{\|\Sigma\|}{\operatorname{Tr}\Sigma}$  [DTV16a]. For simplicity, assume that  $h = \sqrt{\operatorname{Tr}\Sigma}$  is known. In Section 8.1, we show to estimate this based on the observed corrupted samples. Instead of the projection to the  $\ell_{\infty}$ -ball, we project to the  $\ell_2$ -ball of radius  $h$ . That is, we define  $f(x) = \phi_h(\| x\|)\cdot \frac{x}{\|x\|}$ . Hence, in this case  $F(x) = \Phi_h(\| x\|)$  and  $\mathcal{L}(\mu) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i = 1}^{n}\Phi_h\left(\left\| \mu -y_i\right\|\right)$ .

Also in this case, it is easy to see that  $f$  is 1-Lipschitz. Further, denote by  $P_{h} \colon \mathbb{R}^{d} \to \mathbb{R}^{d}$  the projection onto the sphere of radius  $h$ . Then it is not hard to verify that

$$
\tilde {\Sigma} _ {f} = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} f \left(\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {*}\right) f \left(\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {*}\right) ^ {\top} \approx \underset {\boldsymbol {\eta} \sim D} {\mathbb {E}} f (\boldsymbol {\eta}) f (\boldsymbol {\eta}) ^ {\top} \preceq \underset {\boldsymbol {\eta} \sim D} {\mathbb {E}} P _ {h} (\boldsymbol {\eta}) P _ {h} (\boldsymbol {\eta}) ^ {\top} \preceq h ^ {2} \cdot \frac {\| \Sigma \|}{\operatorname {T r} \Sigma} \cdot I _ {d} = \| \Sigma \| \cdot I _ {d}.
$$

Hence  $\| \tilde{\Sigma}_f\| \leqslant O(\| \Sigma \|)$ . Further, since by symmetry  $\mathbb{E}_{\eta}f(\eta) = 0$  and  $\| f(\pmb {\eta})\| \leqslant h$  if follows by standard concentration results (cf. Fact A.2) that  $\left\| \tilde{g}_f\right\| \leqslant O(h\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{n}}) = O(\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{Tr}\Sigma\cdot\log d}{n}})$ .

Lastly, it is not hard to verify that for at least a constant fraction of the samples,  $\left\| \boldsymbol{\eta}_i^*\right\|$  falls in the radius of strong convexity. Hence, a similar argument as in the semi-product case, implies that  $\mathcal{L}$

is  $\Theta(1)$ -strongly convex in an  $\ell_2$ -ball of radius  $h$  around  $\mu^*$ . We refer to Section 8 for a full argument. Hence, it follows that  $\left\| \mu^* - \hat{\mu} \right\| \leqslant O\left(\sqrt{\frac{\operatorname{Tr}\Sigma \cdot \log d}{n}} + \sqrt{\varepsilon} \cdot \left(\sqrt{\|\Sigma\|} + \sqrt{\|\hat{\Sigma}_f\|}\right)\right)$ .

Using Higher-Order Moments. Note that, as in the bounded moment setting, the proof of identifiability we gave naturally extends to using higher-order  $f$ -moments. Indeed, if we apply Hölder's Inequality instead of Cauchy-Schwarz in Eq. (2.1), the following kind of terms appear  $\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle f(\eta_i^*), u \rangle^{2k}\right)^{1/(2k)}$ . Which is the empirical analogue of the  $2k$ -th moment tensor, again with respect to the transformed moments of the noise. We will give a full proof in Section 4. For  $(\alpha, \rho)$ -semi-product distributions, this can be upper bounded by  $O(\rho)$  and for elliptical distributions by  $\sqrt{\|\Sigma\|}$ . Hence, we will eventually be able to conclude the bounds stated in Theorems 1.3 and 1.4.

# 2.2 Filtering Algorithm for Bounded Second Transformed Moments

In this section, we make the above statement of identifiability algorithmic and prove Theorems 1.3 and 1.4. In fact, it allows us to design a generalization of a simple yet effective iterative filtering algorithm that has found wide success for robust mean estimation under bounded moment assumptions. Our exposition closely follows [Li19]. The extension to higher-order moments follows [DK22, Chapter 6.5]. In both cases adapted to use our transformed moments.

On a high level, the idea is as follows: We keep a weight for each data point indicating how confident we are that it is an inlier, i.e., it has not been corrupted. We iteratively update these weights making sure that with each update, we remove more mass from points which are outliers than from inliers. Since the total mass of corrupted points is bounded, the above variant ensures termination and further, that we only removed at most an  $\varepsilon$ -fraction of the mass from the good points. Keeping in mind our proof of identifiability of the previous section, for the update step, we want to downweigh points which introduce a large eigenvalue in  $\hat{\Sigma}_f$ . For simplicity, we assume that the spectral norm of  $\tilde{\Sigma}_f = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} f(\eta_i^*) f(\eta_i^*)^\top$  is known. This assumption can be removed using, e.g., the argument in [DKP20, Appendix A.2].

We first introduce some notation. In what follows, we can assume  $f, F, \mathcal{L}$  to be any functions that satisfy conditions in the previous section. For an entry-wise non-negative vector  $w \in \mathbb{R}^n$ , let  $\mathcal{L}^w(\mu) = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i F(\mu - y_i)$ ,  $\Sigma_f(w, \mu) = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i f(\mu - y_i) f(\mu - y_i)^\top$ . Concretely, we will use the following algorithm

Algorithm 2.1 (Filtering Algorithm).

Input: Corrupted points  $y_{1}, \ldots, y_{n}$ .

Output: Estimate  $\hat{\mu}$  of the location parameter.

- Let  $t = 0$ ,  $w^{(0)} = \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}_n$ ,  $\hat{\mu}^{(0)} = \min_{\mu \in \mathbb{R}^d}\mathcal{L}^{w^{(0)}}(\mu)$ , and  $\Sigma_f^{(0)} = \Sigma_f(w^{(0)},\hat{\mu}^{(0)})$  
- while  $\left\| \Sigma_f^{(t)}\right\| > 100 \cdot \left\|\tilde{\Sigma}_f\right\|$  do

- Compute  $v^{(t)}$  the top eigenvector of  $\Sigma_f^{(t)}$ .  
- For  $i \in [n]$ , compute  $\tau_i^{(t)} = \left\langle v^{(t)}, f(\hat{\mu}^{(t)} - y_i) \right\rangle^2$  and  $\tau_{\max}^{(t)} = \max_{i \in [n]} \tau_i^{(t)}$ .  
- For  $i \in [n]$ , set  $w_i^{(t+1)} = \left( 1 - \frac{\tau_i^{(t)}}{\tau_{\max}^{(t)}} \right) w_i^{(t)}$ .  
- Compute  $\hat{\mu}^{(t + 1)} = \min_{\mu \in \mathbb{R}^d}\mathcal{L}^{w^{(t + 1)}}(\mu)$  and  $\Sigma_f^{(t + 1)} = \Sigma_f(w^{(t + 1)},\hat{\mu}^{(t + 1)})$  
- Assign  $t \gets t + 1$ .

Output  $\hat{\mu}^{(t + 1)}$

We formalize the invariant that we remove more mass from outliers than inliers as the following claim. We give a proof sketch later in this section.

Proposition 2.2. Denote by  $S_g \subseteq [n]$  the set of inliers and by  $S_b$  the set of outliers. Let  $t \geqslant 0$  and assume that Algorithm 2.1 did not terminate after  $t - 1$  iterations, i.e.,  $\left\| \Sigma_f^{(t)} \right\| > 100 \cdot \left\| \tilde{\Sigma}_f \right\|$ . Then it holds that  $\sum_{i \in S_g} w_i^{(t + 1)} - w_i^t < \sum_{i \in S_b} w_i^{(t + 1)} - w_i^t$ .

We first show how Proposition 2.2 implies Theorems 1.3 and 1.4. First note, that since in each step we set the weight of at least one point to 0. Hence, after  $\lceil 2\varepsilon n\rceil$  iterations, we have reduced the total weight by at least  $2\varepsilon$ . Since in each step we remove more weights from the outliers and the total mass of outliers is at most  $\varepsilon$ , it follows that after this number of iterations, we have removed the weight of all outliers. By our assumption on the spectral norm of the empirical matrix, we will terminate in the next iteration. We next prove correctness. Let  $T$  be the index of the last iteration. Note, that we output the mean of the empirical distribution indicated by  $w^T$ . By the above discussion  $w^T$  is at most  $2\varepsilon$ -far from the vector indicating the uniform distribution over the uncorrupted samples. Hence, these two distributions are  $2\varepsilon$ -close in total variation distance. Further, it follows that  $\Sigma_f^{(T)}$  has spectral norm bounded by  $100\cdot \left\| \tilde{\Sigma}_f\right\|$  and hence, it follows by the identifiability proof in the previous section that if  $\mathcal{L}$  is locally  $\gamma$ -strongly convex that  $\frac{\gamma}{2}\cdot \|\hat{\mu}^{(t)} - \mu^{*}\| \leqslant O(\| \tilde{g}_f\| +\sqrt{\varepsilon}\cdot \sqrt{\|\tilde{\Sigma}_f\|})$ . Since for  $(\alpha ,\rho)$ -semi-product distributions  $\gamma = \alpha$ ,  $\| \tilde{g}_f\| \leqslant O(\rho \cdot \sqrt{\frac{d}{n}})$ ,  $\| \tilde{\Sigma}_f\| \leqslant O(\rho)$  Theorem 1.3 follows. Similarly, for elliptical distributions, we have  $\gamma = \Theta (1)$ ,  $\| \tilde{g}_f\| \leqslant O(\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{Tr}\Sigma\cdot\log d}{n}})$ ,  $\| \tilde{\Sigma}_f\| \leqslant O(\| \Sigma \|)$  which implies Theorem 1.4.

Next, we prove Proposition 2.2. For simplicity, denote  $w = w^{(t)}$  and  $w' = w^{(t+1)}$  and drop the superscript from all other variables (it is  $t$  in all remaining cases). Recall that  $v$  is the top eigenvector of  $\Sigma_f$  and  $\tau_i = \langle v, f(\hat{\mu} - y_i) \rangle^2$ . Hence, to prove Proposition 2.2, it is enough to show that

$\sum_{i\in S_g}\tau_iw_i < \sum_{i\in S_b}\tau_iw_i.$  Since  $S_{g}$  and  $S_{b}$  partition  $[n]$  and  $\begin{array}{r}\sum_{i = 1}^{n}\tau_{i}w_{i} = \sum_{i = 1}^{n}w_{i}\langle v,f(\hat{\mu} -y_{i})\rangle^{2} = \left\| \Sigma_{f}\right\| , \end{array}$  it is sufficient to verify that  $\begin{array}{r}\sum_{i\in S_g}\tau_iw_i <   \frac{\|\Sigma_f\|}{2} \end{array}$  . Indeed, we observe

$$
\sum_ {i \in S _ {g}} \tau_ {i} w _ {i} = \sum_ {i \in S _ {g}} w _ {i} \langle v, f (\hat {\mu} - y _ {i}) \rangle^ {2} \leqslant \frac {2}{n} \sum_ {i \in S _ {g}} \langle v, f (\boldsymbol {\mu} ^ {*} - \boldsymbol {y} _ {i} ^ {*}) \rangle^ {2} + \frac {2}{| S _ {g} |} \sum_ {i \in S _ {g}} \langle v, f (\hat {\mu} - y _ {i}) - f (\boldsymbol {\mu} ^ {*} - \boldsymbol {y} _ {i} ^ {*}) \rangle^ {2}.
$$

The first term is at most  $2\|\tilde{\Sigma}_f\|$  and the second term is at most  $\|\hat{\mu} - \mu^*\|^2$  by Cauchy-Schwarz and Lipschitzness of  $f$ . Hence, combining this with our statement of identifiability implies

$$
\sum_ {i \in S _ {g}} \tau_ {i} w _ {i} \leqslant O \left(\| \tilde {\Sigma} _ {f} \| + \| \tilde {g} _ {f} \| + \sqrt {\varepsilon} \cdot \left(\| \tilde {\Sigma} _ {f} \| + \| \Sigma_ {f} \|\right)\right) <   \frac {\left\| \Sigma_ {f} \right\|}{2},
$$

where we also used that  $\| \Sigma_f\| >100\cdot \| \tilde{\Sigma}_f\|$  and  $\| \Sigma_f\| \gg \| \tilde{g}_f\|$ .

# 2.3 Extension to Higher-Order Transformed Moments

One approach to extend the algorithm from the previous section to utilize higher-order transformed moments would be to instead of checking  $\Sigma_{f}$  in each iteration, to check a higher-order moment tensor. The relevant quantity is  $\max_{u\in \mathbb{R}^d}\sum_{i = 1}^{n}w_i\langle f(\hat{\mu} -y_i),u\rangle^{2k}$ . which is known as the injective tensor norm. Unfortunately, this is NP-hard to compute in the worst case. However, for the case of bounded moments, we can get around this by assuming that the injective norm has an efficiently verifiable certificate in the form of an SoS proof. In general, this can be non-trivial to obtain. A key observation is that for our transformed moments these certificates are straightforward to obtain since we can exploit boundedness of the transformation. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time that some transformation is applied in order to make a distribution certifiably bounded. This approach is interesting in its own right and might be useful in other settings. We refer to Section 6 for more detail.

# 3 Preliminaries

Notation We use bold-font for random variables. Further, we use  $\gtrsim$  and  $\lesssim$  to suppress absolute constants which do not depend on any other problem parameters. For a (pseudo)-distribution  $\mu$ , we denote by  $\mathrm{supp}(\mu)$  its support.

Sum-of-Squares Proofs and Pseudo-Expectations In this section, we will introduce sum-of-squares proofs and their convex duals, so-called pseudo-distributions.

Let  $p \colon \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$  be a polynomial in formal variables  $X = (X_1, \ldots, X_n)$ . We say the inequality  $p(X) \geqslant 0$  has a sum-of-squares proof (short SoS proof) if we can write  $p(X)$  as a sum of squares in  $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ . Further, if every polynomial in this decomposition has degree at most  $t$ , we say that the sum-of-squares proof has degree-  $t$  and write  $\left| \frac{X}{t} \right| p \geqslant 0$ . We write  $\mathcal{A} \left| \frac{X}{t} \right| p \geqslant p'$  if  $\mathcal{A} \left| \frac{X}{t} \right| p - p' \geqslant 0$  and  $\mathcal{A} \left| \frac{X}{t} \right| p = p'$  if  $\mathcal{A} \left| \frac{X}{t} \right| p \leqslant p'$  and  $\mathcal{A} \left| \frac{X}{t} \right| p \geqslant p'$ . It is not hard to verify that the following composition rule holds: If  $\left| \frac{X}{t} \right| p \geqslant p'$  and  $\left| \frac{X}{t'} \right| p' \geqslant p''$ , then it also holds that  $\left| \frac{X}{t''} \right| p \geqslant p''$ , where  $t'' = \max\{t, t'\}$ .

Next, we introduce so-called pseudo-distributions, the convex duals of sum-of-squares proofs: For  $d \in \mathbb{N}_{\geqslant 1}$ , a degree- $d$  pseudo-distribution is a finitely-supported function  $\mu \colon \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$  such that

$\sum_{x \in \operatorname{supp}(\mu)} \mu(x) = 1$  and  $\sum_{x \in \operatorname{supp}(\mu)} \mu(x)f^2(x) \geqslant 0$  for all polynomials  $f$  of degree at most  $d/2$ . The pseudo-expectation corresponding to a pseudo-distribution  $\mu$  is defined as the linear operator mapping a degree- $d$  polynomial  $f$  to  $\tilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}f \coloneqq \sum_{x \in \operatorname{supp}(\mu)} \mu(x)f(x)$ . We say that a pseudo-distribution  $\tilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}$  satisfies a set of inequalities  $\{q_1 \geqslant 0, \dots, q_m \geqslant 0\}$  at degree  $r$ , if for all  $S \subseteq [m]$  and  $h$  a sum-of-squares polynomial such that  $\deg(h \cdot \Pi_{j \in S}) \leqslant r$  it holds that

$$
\tilde {\mathbf {E}} _ {\mu} h \cdot \Pi_ {j \in S} \geqslant 0.
$$

We relate pseudo-distributions and sum-of-squares proofs using the following facts.

Fact 3.1. Let  $\mu$  be a degree- $d$  pseudo-distribution that satisfies  $\mathcal{A}$  and suppose that  $\mathcal{A} \Big|_{t} p \geqslant 0$ . Let  $h$  be an arbitrary sum-of-squares polynomial, if  $\deg h + t \leqslant d$  we have  $\tilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu} h \cdot p \geqslant 0$ . In particular, we have  $\tilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu} p \geqslant 0$  as long as  $t \leqslant d$ . If  $\mu$  only approximately satisfies  $\mathcal{A}$  it holds that  $\tilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu} p \geqslant -\eta \| h \|_2$  for  $\eta = 2^{-n^{\Omega(d)}}$ . Further, if there is no degree- $d$  sum-of-squares proof that  $\mathcal{A} \Big|_{t} p \geqslant 0$ , then for there exists a degree- $d$  pseudo-distribution  $\tilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}$  satisfying  $\mathcal{A}$  and  $\varepsilon > 0$  such that  $\tilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu} p < 0$ .

An essential fact is that pseudo-distributions approximately satisfying a system of polynomial inequalities can be found efficiently as long as the constraints have bit-complexity at most  $(m + n)^{O(1)}$ .

Fact 3.2. Given any feasible system of polynomial constraints  $\mathcal{A} = \left\{q_1 \geqslant 0, \dots, q_m \geqslant 0\right\}$  over  $\mathbb{R}^n$  whose bit-complexity satisfies the constraints mentioned above and  $\eta = 2^{-n^{\Theta(d)}}$  we can in time  $(n + m)^{O(d)}$  find a degree- $d$  pseudo-distribution that satisfies  $\mathcal{A}$  up to error  $\eta$ .<sup>13</sup>

# 4 Proof of Identifiability

In this section, we give a formal proof of the statement of identifiability sketch in Section 2. We will give the proof assuming an abstract set of assumptions and verify that they indeed hold for semi-product and elliptical distributions. We first recall the set-up and introduce some notation. We use similar notation as in [Li19]. Let  $\mu^{*} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$  and  $\eta_{1}^{*}, \ldots, \eta_{n}^{*}$  be samples from a distribution  $D$ . We denote the uncorrupted sample as  $y_{1}^{*}, \ldots, y_{n}^{*}$  and the  $\varepsilon$ -corrupted one as  $y_{1}, \ldots, y_{n}$ . Further, let  $S_{g} = \left\{i \mid y_{i} = y_{i}^{*}\right\}$  be the set of uncorrupted indices and  $S_{b} = [n] \setminus S_{g}$  be the set of corrupted ones. Let

$$
\mathcal {W} _ {\varepsilon} = \left\{w \in \mathbb {R} ^ {n} \big | 0 \leqslant w \leqslant \frac {1}{n} \mathbb {1}, \left\| w - \frac {1}{n} \mathbb {1} \right\| _ {1} \leqslant \varepsilon \right\},
$$

where  $\mathbb{1}$  is the all-ones vector and the inequalities between vectors are entry-wise. Throughout the note we assume that  $\varepsilon \lesssim \alpha$ , note that this is implied by the assumptions in Theorems 1.3 and 1.4. Note, that otherwise faithfully recovering  $\mu^{*}$  is impossible: For each coordinate, with high probability there is only  $\Theta (\alpha)$ -fraction of the samples where the noise is small. If  $\varepsilon \gtrsim \alpha$ , the adversary could corrupt precisely this fraction and erase all information about this coordinate.

Further, let  $F \colon \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$  and denote by  $f = \nabla F$  its gradient. Further assume that  $F$  and  $f$  are such that  $f$  is  $L$ -Lipschitz. For  $w \in \mathbb{R}^n$  with non-negative entries, we define  $\mathcal{L}^w \colon \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$  as

$$
\mathcal {L} ^ {w} (\mu) := \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} F \left(\mu - y _ {i}\right).
$$

It follows that  $\nabla \mathcal{L}^w (\mu) = \sum_{i = 1}^n w_if\left(\mu -y_i\right)$ . Let  $2k\in \mathbb{N}_{\geqslant 2}$  and define  $\hat{\mu} (w)\coloneqq \min_{\mu \in \mathbb{R}^d}\mathcal{L}^w (\mu)$  as well as

$$
\bar {\mathfrak {m}} _ {2 k} := \max _ {v \in \mathbf {S} ^ {d - 1}} \left[ \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \big <   f (\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {*}), v \big > ^ {2 k} \right] ^ {\frac {1}{2 k}} \quad \text {a n d} \quad \hat {\mathfrak {m}} _ {2 k} ^ {w} := \max _ {v \in \mathbf {S} ^ {d - 1}} \left[ \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} \big <   f (\hat {\mu} - y _ {i}), v \big > ^ {2 k} \right] ^ {\frac {1}{2 k}}.
$$

We will prove the following theorem.

Theorem 4.1. Let  $\alpha \in (0,1)$  and let  $k$  be a positive integer and  $w \in \mathcal{W}_{2\varepsilon}$ . Suppose that  $\varepsilon^{1 - \frac{1}{2k}} \leqslant 0.01 \cdot (\alpha / L)$  and

$$
\alpha \| \hat {\mu} ^ {w} - \mu^ {*} \| ^ {2} \leqslant 1 0 \left\langle \nabla \mathcal {L} ^ {w} \left(\mu^ {*}\right), \mu^ {*} - \hat {\mu} ^ {w} \right\rangle .
$$

Then

$$
\left\| \hat {\mu} ^ {w} - \mu^ {*} \right\| \leqslant \frac {1 0 0}{\alpha} \cdot \left(\left\| \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} f (\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {*}) \right\| + \varepsilon^ {1 - \frac {1}{2 k}} \cdot \left(\hat {\mathfrak {m}} _ {2 k} ^ {w} + \bar {\mathfrak {m}} _ {2 k}\right)\right).
$$

This theorem easily follows from the following lemma.

Lemma 4.2. Let  $k$  be a positive integer and  $w \in \mathcal{W}_{2\varepsilon}$ . Then

$$
\left\langle \nabla \mathcal {L} ^ {w} (\boldsymbol {\mu} ^ {*}), u \right\rangle \leqslant 5 \cdot \| u \| \cdot \left(\left\| \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} f (\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {*}) \right\| + \varepsilon^ {1 - \frac {1}{2 k}} \cdot \left(\hat {\mathfrak {m}} _ {2 k} ^ {w} + \bar {\mathfrak {m}} _ {2 k} + L \| u \|\right)\right),
$$

where  $u = \hat{\mu} (w) - \mu^{*}$

We first give the proof of Theorem 4.1.

Proof of Theorem 4.1. Let  $u = \hat{\mu}(w) - \mu^*$ . Using Lemma 4.2 and  $\varepsilon^{1 - \frac{1}{2k}} \leqslant 0.01 \cdot (\alpha / L)$ , cancelling the common factor of  $\| u \|$  and rearranging now yields

$$
\| u \| \leqslant \frac {1 0 0}{\alpha} \cdot \left(\left\| \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} f (\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i}) \right\| + \varepsilon^ {1 - \frac {1}{2 k}} \cdot \left(\hat {\mathfrak {m}} _ {2 k} ^ {w} + \bar {\mathfrak {m}} _ {2 k}\right)\right).
$$

Next, we prove Lemma 4.2.

Proof of Lemma 4.2. Again, let  $u = \hat{\mu}(w) - \mu^*$ . We can decompose  $\left\langle \nabla \mathcal{L}^w(\mu^*), u \right\rangle$  as

$$
\begin{array}{l} \left\langle \nabla \mathcal {L} ^ {w} \left(\mu^ {*}\right), u \right\rangle = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} \left\langle f \left(\mu^ {*} - y _ {i}\right), u \right\rangle \\ = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} \left\langle f (\boldsymbol {\mu} ^ {*} - \boldsymbol {y} _ {i} ^ {*}), u \right\rangle + \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \mathbf {1} \{i \in S _ {b} \} \cdot w _ {i} \left\langle f (\boldsymbol {\mu} ^ {*} - y _ {i}) - f (\boldsymbol {\mu} ^ {*} - \boldsymbol {y} _ {i} ^ {*}), u \right\rangle . \\ \end{array}
$$

For the first sum,

$$
\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} \left\langle f \left(\mu^ {*} - \boldsymbol {y} _ {i} ^ {*}\right), u \right\rangle = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} \left\langle f \left(\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {*}\right), u \right\rangle \leqslant \left\| \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} f \left(\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {*}\right) \right\| \cdot \| u \| + \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(w _ {i} - \frac {1}{n}\right) \left\langle f \left(\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {*}\right), u \right\rangle .
$$

By Hölder's Inequality,

$$
\begin{array}{l} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(w _ {i} - \frac {1}{n}\right) \left\langle f \left(\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {*}\right), u \right\rangle \leqslant \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left| w _ {i} - \frac {1}{n} \right|\right) ^ {1 - \frac {1}{2 k}} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left| w _ {i} - \frac {1}{n} \right| \cdot \left\langle f \left(\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {*}\right), u \right\rangle^ {2 k}\right) ^ {\frac {1}{2 k}} \\ \leqslant 4 \cdot \varepsilon^ {1 - \frac {1}{2 k}} \cdot \bar {\mathfrak {m}} _ {2 k} \cdot \| u \|. \\ \end{array}
$$

For the second sum, we bound using Hölder's Inequality

$$
\begin{array}{l} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \mathbf {1} \{i \in S _ {b} \} \cdot w _ {i} \cdot \left\langle f \left(\mu^ {*} - y _ {i}\right) - f \left(\mu^ {*} - \boldsymbol {y} _ {i} ^ {*}\right), u \right\rangle \leqslant \varepsilon^ {1 - \frac {1}{2 k}} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} \cdot \left\langle f \left(\mu^ {*} - y _ {i}\right) - f \left(\mu^ {*} - \boldsymbol {y} _ {i} ^ {*}\right), u \right\rangle^ {2 k}\right) ^ {\frac {1}{2 k}} \\ = \varepsilon^ {1 - \frac {1}{2 k}} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} \cdot \left\langle f \left(\mu^ {*} - y _ {i}\right) - f \left(\mu^ {*} - \boldsymbol {y} _ {i} ^ {*}\right) - f (\hat {\mu} - y _ {i}) + f (\hat {\mu} - y _ {i}), u \right\rangle^ {2 k}\right) ^ {\frac {1}{2 k}} \\ \leqslant \varepsilon^ {1 - \frac {1}{2 k}} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} \left\langle f \left(\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {*}\right), u \right\rangle^ {2 k}\right) ^ {\frac {1}{2 k}} + \varepsilon^ {1 - \frac {1}{2 k}} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} \left\langle f \left(\hat {\mu} - y _ {i}\right), u \right\rangle^ {2 k}\right) ^ {\frac {1}{2 k}} \\ + \varepsilon^ {1 - \frac {1}{2 k}} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} \left\langle f \left(\mu^ {*} - y _ {i}\right) - f \left(\hat {\mu} - y _ {i}\right), u \right\rangle^ {2 k}\right) ^ {\frac {1}{2 k}}. \\ \end{array}
$$

We bound the three terms in the square bracket one by one.

The first term can be bounded as follows

$$
\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} \left\langle f \left(\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {*}\right), u \right\rangle^ {2 k} \leqslant \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \frac {1}{n} \left\langle f \left(\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {*}\right), u \right\rangle^ {2 k} \leqslant \bar {\mathbf {m}} _ {2 k} ^ {2 k} \| u \| ^ {2 k}.
$$

The second one is at most  $\bar{\mathfrak{m}}_{2k}\| u\|$  by definition of  $\hat{\mathfrak{m}}_{2k}^{w}$ . For the third, we use that  $f$  is  $L$ -Lipschitz to bound

$$
\left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} \cdot \left\langle f \left(\mu^ {*} - y _ {i}\right) - f \left(\hat {\mu} - y _ {i}\right), u \right\rangle^ {2 k}\right) ^ {\frac {1}{2 k}} \leqslant \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} \cdot \left\| f \left(\mu^ {*} - y _ {i}\right) - f \left(\hat {\mu} - y _ {i}\right) \right\| ^ {2 k} \| u \| ^ {2 k}\right) ^ {\frac {1}{2 k}} \leqslant L \| u \| ^ {2}.
$$

It follows that

$$
\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \mathbf {1} \{i \in S _ {b} \} \cdot w _ {i} \cdot \left\langle f \left(\mu^ {*} - y _ {i}\right) - f \left(\mu^ {*} - \boldsymbol {y} _ {i} ^ {*}\right), u \right\rangle \leqslant \varepsilon^ {1 - \frac {1}{2 k}} \| u \| \cdot \left(\hat {\mathfrak {m}} _ {2 k} ^ {w} + \bar {\mathfrak {m}} _ {2 k} + L \| u \|\right).
$$

Putting all the above together, we obtain the desired bound.

# 5 Efficient Algorithm for Bounded Clipped Covariance

In this section, we show that the filtering algorithm for robust mean estimation under moment constraints (cf. for example [Li19, DK22]) can be adapted to also yield an efficient algorithm for robustly estimating the location parameter of symmetric distributions. For now, we focus on the case that  $k = 2$ , we discuss the extension to higher moments in Section 6. Again, we will focus on the abstract setting and show that we can apply these results to semi-product and elliptical distributions. We will closely follow the exposition in [Li19, DK22].

Recall that we denote  $\mathcal{L}^w (\mu) = \sum_{i = 1}^n w_iF\big(\mu -y_i\big)$ .

Assumption 5.1 (Goodness Condition). Let  $F\colon \mathbb{R}^d\to \mathbb{R},f = \nabla F,\mu^*\in \mathbb{R}^d,0 <   \sigma ,0 <   \alpha <  1,0 <   L$  and  $y_{1},\ldots ,y_{n}\in \mathbb{R}^{d}$  . Let  $D$  be a distribution over  $\mathbb{R}^d$  and  $\eta_1^*,\dots ,\eta_n^*$  be  $n$  i.i.d. samples from  $D$  . We say that the goodness condition holds, if

1.  $f$  is  $L$ -Lipschitz,  
2.  $\sqrt{\varepsilon} \leqslant \frac{\alpha}{1000L}$ ,  
3.  $\alpha \| \hat{\mu}^w -\mu^*\| ^2\leqslant 10\bigl \langle \nabla \mathcal{L}^w (\mu^*),\mu^* -\hat{\mu}^w\bigr\rangle ,$  
4.  $\left\| \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i = 1}^{n}f(\pmb{\eta}_i^*)\right\| \leqslant \frac{\alpha}{100L}\sigma ,$  
5.  $\bar{\mathbf{m}}_2 = \max_{v\in \mathbf{S}^{d - 1}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i = 1}^n\left\langle f(\pmb{\eta}_i^*),v\right\rangle^2}\leqslant \sigma .$

Under this condition we can show the following theorem.

Theorem 5.2. Let  $\mu^{*} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}, 0 < \sigma, 0 < L, 0 < \alpha < 1, \varepsilon > 0$ , and  $\eta_{1}^{*}, \ldots, \eta_{1}^{*}$  be  $n$  i.i.d. samples from some distribution  $D$  over  $\mathbb{R}^{d}$ . Let  $y_{1}, \ldots, y_{n}$  be an  $\varepsilon$ -corruption of  $y_{1}^{*} = \mu^{*} + \eta_{1}^{*}, \ldots, y_{n}^{*} = \mu^{*} + \eta_{n}^{*}$ . Assume Assumption 5.1 (the goodness condition) holds, then Algorithm 5.3, given  $y_{1}, \ldots, y_{n}$  and  $\sigma$ , terminates after at most  $2\varepsilon n + 1$  iterations and computes  $\hat{\mu}$  such that

$$
\left\| \hat {\mu} ^ {w} - \mu^ {*} \right\| \leqslant O \left(\frac {\left\| \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} f (\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {*}) \right\|}{\alpha} + \frac {\sqrt {\varepsilon}}{\alpha} \cdot \sigma\right).
$$

Moreover each iteration can be implemented in time  $n^{O(1)}d^{O(1)}$ .

To describe the algorithm, let  $C > 0$  be some universal constant and for  $\mu \in \mathbb{R}^d$ ,  $w \in \mathcal{W}_{\varepsilon}$  let

$$
\hat {\mu} (w) = \min  _ {\mu \in \mathbb {R} ^ {d}} \mathcal {L} ^ {w} (\mu),
$$

$$
\Sigma_ {f} (w) = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} f (\hat {\mu} ^ {w} - y _ {i}) f (\hat {\mu} ^ {w} - y _ {i}) ^ {\top}.
$$

We will use the following Filtering Algorithm:

Algorithm 5.3 (Filtering Algorithm).

Input:  $\varepsilon$ -corrupted sample  $y_{1},\ldots ,y_{n}$  and  $\sigma >0$

Output: Location estimate  $\hat{\mu}$

Let  $w^{(0)} = \frac{1}{n}\mathbb{1}_n$  
- Compute  $\hat{\mu}^{(0)} = \min_{\mu \in \mathbb{R}^d}\mathcal{L}^{w^{(0)}}(\mu)$  and  $\Sigma_f^{(0)} = \Sigma_f(w^{(0)})$  
Let  $t = 0$  
while  $\left\| \Sigma_f^{(t)}\right\| >100\cdot \sigma$  do

- Compute  $v^{(t)}$  the top eigenvector of  $\Sigma_f^{(t)}$ .  
- For  $i \in [n]$ , compute  $\tau_i^{(t)} = \left\langle v^{(t)}, f(\hat{\mu}^{(t)} - y_i) \right\rangle^2$ .  
- For  $i \in [n]$ , set  $w_i^{(t+1)} = \left( 1 - \frac{\tau_i}{\tau_{\max}} \right) w_i^{(t)}$ , where  $\tau_{\max} = \max_{i \in [n]} \tau_i$ .  
- Compute  $\hat{\mu}^{(t + 1)} = \min_{\mu \in \mathbb{R}^d}f^{w^{(t + 1)}}(\mu)$  and  $\Sigma_f^{(t + 1)} = \Sigma_f(w^{(t + 1)})$  
-  $t\gets t + 1$

- Output  $\hat{\mu}^{(t)}$ .

The proof of Theorem 5.2 is very similar to the one in [Li19]. We will use the following lemma whose proof we will give at the end of this section

Lemma 5.4. Assume that Theorem 5.2  $\| \Sigma_f^{(t)}\| >100\cdot \sigma$  and

$$
\sum_ {i \in S _ {g}} \left(\frac {1}{n} - w _ {i} ^ {(t)}\right) <   \sum_ {i \in S _ {b}} \left(\frac {1}{n} - w _ {i} ^ {(t)}\right).
$$

Then

$$
\sum_ {i \in S _ {g}} \left(\frac {1}{n} - w _ {i} ^ {(t + 1)}\right) <   \sum_ {i \in S _ {b}} \left(\frac {1}{n} - w _ {i} ^ {(t + 1)}\right).
$$

With this in hand, we will prove Theorem 5.2.

Proof of Theorem 5.2. First note, that every iteration can clearly be implanted to run in time  $n^{O(1)}d^{O(1)}$ . First, we will show that the algorithm terminates after at most  $\lceil 2\varepsilon n \rceil$  iterations. We will prove it by contradiction. Suppose that the algorithm does not terminate after  $T = \lceil 2\varepsilon n \rceil$  iterations. Note that the number of zero entries of  $w^{(t)}$  increases by at least 1 in every iteration. Hence, after  $T$  iterations we have set at least  $\varepsilon n$  entries of  $w$  to zero whose index lies in  $S_g$ . By assumption that the algorithm didn't terminate and Lemma 5.4 it holds that

$$
\varepsilon \leqslant \sum_ {i \in S _ {g}} \left(\frac {1}{n} - w _ {i} ^ {(T)}\right) <   \sum_ {i \in S _ {b}} \left(\frac {1}{n} - w _ {i} ^ {(T)}\right) \leqslant \frac {| S _ {b} |}{n} \leqslant \varepsilon .
$$

which is a contradiction.

Next, we prove the correctness of the algorithm. Let  $T$  be the index of the last iteration of the algorithm before termination. Note that by our invariant

$$
\left\| \frac {1}{n} - w ^ {(T)} \right\| _ {1} = \sum_ {i \in S _ {g}} \frac {1}{n} - w _ {i} ^ {(T)} + \sum_ {i \in S _ {b}} \frac {1}{n} - w _ {i} ^ {(T)} <   2 \sum_ {i \in S _ {b}} \frac {1}{n} - w _ {i} ^ {(T)} \leqslant 2 \varepsilon .
$$

Since also  $0 \leqslant w^{(T)} \leqslant \frac{1}{n}$ , it follows that  $w^{(T)} \in \mathcal{W}_{2\varepsilon}$ . By Theorem 4.1 and since  $\left\| \Sigma_f^{(T)} \right\| \leqslant 100 \cdot \sigma$  it follows that

$$
\left\| \hat {\mu} ^ {w} - \mu^ {*} \right\| \leqslant O \left(\frac {\left\| \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} f (\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {*}) \right\|}{\alpha} + \frac {\sqrt {\varepsilon}}{\alpha} \cdot \sigma\right).
$$

Lastly, we will prove Lemma 5.4.

Proof of Lemma 5.4. For simplicity, let  $w = w^{(t)}$  and  $w' = w^{(t+1)}$ . Note, that it is enough to show that

$$
\sum_ {i \in S _ {g}} w _ {i} - w _ {i} ^ {\prime} <   \sum_ {i \in S _ {b}} w _ {i} - w _ {i} ^ {\prime}.
$$

Further, recall that  $w_{i}^{\prime} = \left(1 - \frac{\tau_{i}}{\tau_{\max}}\right)w_{i}$ , so for all  $i \in [n]$ ,  $w_{i} - w_{i}^{\prime} = \frac{1}{\tau_{\max}}\tau_{i}w_{i}$ . Hence is enough to show that

$$
\sum_ {i \in S _ {g}} \tau_ {i} w _ {i} <   \sum_ {i \in S _ {b}} \tau_ {i} w _ {i}.
$$

Since  $S_g$  and  $S_b$  partition  $[n]$  and

$$
\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} \tau_ {i} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} \Big \langle v ^ {(t)}, f \Big (\hat {\mu} ^ {(t)} - y _ {i} \Big) \Big \rangle^ {2} = \Big (v ^ {(t)} \Big) ^ {\top} \Sigma_ {f} ^ {(t)} \Big (v ^ {(t)} \Big) = \Big \| \Sigma_ {f} ^ {(t)} \Big \|,
$$

we can prove  $\sum_{i\in S_g}\tau_iw_i < \sum_{i\in S_b}\tau_iw_i$  by showing that

$$
\sum_ {i \in S _ {g}} \tau_ {i} w _ {i} <   \frac {| | \Sigma_ {f} ^ {(t)} | |}{2}.
$$

To this end, note that

$$
\begin{array}{l} \sum_ {i \in S _ {g}} \tau_ {i} w _ {i} = \sum_ {i \in S _ {g}} w _ {i} \left\langle v ^ {(t)}, f (\hat {\mu} ^ {(t)} - y _ {i}) \right\rangle^ {2} \\ \leqslant \frac {2}{n} \sum_ {i \in [ n ]} \left\langle v ^ {(t)}, f (\mu^ {*} - y _ {i}) \right\rangle^ {2} + \frac {2}{| S _ {g} |} \sum_ {i \in S _ {g}} \left\langle v ^ {(t)}, f (\hat {\mu} ^ {(t)} - y _ {i}) - f (\mu^ {*} - y _ {i}) \right\rangle^ {2}. \\ \end{array}
$$

The first term is at most  $2\sigma^2$ . For the second term we observe that since  $f$  is  $L$ -Lipschitz, for all  $i \in [n]$  it holds that

$$
\left\langle v ^ {(t)}, f \left(\hat {\mu} ^ {(t)} - y _ {i}\right) - f \left(\mu^ {*} - y _ {i}\right) \right\rangle^ {2} \leqslant \left\| f \left(\hat {\mu} ^ {(t)} - y _ {i}\right) - f \left(\mu^ {*} - y _ {i}\right) \right\| ^ {2} \leqslant L ^ {2} \cdot \left\| \hat {\mu} ^ {(t)} - \mu^ {*} \right\| ^ {2}.
$$

Hence, by Theorem 4.1,

$$
\sum_ {i \in S _ {g}} \tau_ {i} w _ {i} \leqslant 2 \sigma^ {2} + L ^ {2} \big \| \hat {\mu} ^ {(t)} - \beta^ {*} \big \| ^ {2} \leqslant 2 \sigma^ {2} + \frac {1 0 0 ^ {2} L ^ {2}}{\alpha^ {2}} \bigg (\bigg \| \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} f (\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {*}) \bigg \| + \sqrt {\varepsilon} \cdot \Sigma_ {f} ^ {(t)} \bigg) ^ {2} <   \frac {\Sigma_ {f} ^ {(t)}}{2},
$$

where we used  $\varepsilon \lesssim \frac{\alpha^2}{L^2}$  and  $\left\| \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(\boldsymbol{\eta}_i^*) \right\|\lesssim \frac{\alpha}{L} \sigma$

# 6 Efficient Algorithm for Bounded Higher-Order Clipped Moments

In this section, we show how to adapt the filtering algorithm to incorporate higher-order moments of the transformed noise. We follow closely the exposition in [DK22, Chapter 6.5]. In particular, consider the following definition similar to the by now standard notion of cerifiably bounded moments or certifiable subgaussianity [KSS18, HL18]. Let  $f\colon \mathbb{R}^d\to \mathbb{R}^d$  be some function.

Definition 6.1. Let  $k, \ell \in \mathbb{N}$  and  $\sigma > 0$ . We say a distribution  $D$  over  $\mathbb{R}^d$  has  $(2k, \ell)$ -certifiably  $\sigma$ -bounded  $f$ -moments if there is a degree- $\ell$  SoS proof (cf. Section 3), in formal variables  $v$ , that

$$
\underset {\eta \sim D} {\mathbb {E}} \langle f (\boldsymbol {\eta}), v \rangle^ {2 k} \leqslant (\sigma \cdot \| v \|) ^ {2 k}.
$$

We again define a goodness condition analogous to Assumption 5.1. Specifically, we assume

Assumption 6.2 (Higher-Order Goodness Condition). Let  $F\colon \mathbb{R}^d\to \mathbb{R},f = \nabla F,\mu^*\in \mathbb{R}^d,0 <   \sigma ,0 <   \alpha <  1,0 <   L,k\in \mathbb{N}$  and  $y_{1},\ldots ,y_{n}\in \mathbb{R}^{d}$  . Let  $D$  be a distribution over  $\mathbb{R}^d$  and  $\eta_1^*,\dots,\eta_n^*$  be  $n$  i.i.d. samples from  $D$  . We say that the higher-order goodness condition holds, if

1.  $f$  is  $L$ -Lipschitz,  
2.  $\varepsilon^{1 - \frac{1}{2k}}\leqslant \frac{\alpha}{1000L}$  
3.  $\alpha \| \hat{\mu}^w -\mu^*\| ^2\leqslant 10\bigl {\langle}\nabla \mathcal{L}^w (\mu^*),\mu^* -\hat{\mu}^w\bigr\rangle ,$  
4.  $\left\| \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i = 1}^{n}f(\pmb{\eta}_i^*)\right\| \leqslant \frac{\alpha}{100L}\sigma ,$

$$
\left| \frac {v}{\ell} \right. \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \langle f (\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {*}), v \rangle^ {2 k} \leqslant (\sigma \cdot \| v \|) ^ {2 k}.

Specifically, we will show

Theorem 6.3. Let  $\mu^{*} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}, 0 < \sigma, 0 < L, 0 < \alpha < 1, k \in \mathbb{N}$ , and  $\eta_{1}^{*}, \ldots, \eta_{1}^{*}$  be  $n$  i.i.d. samples from some distribution  $D$  over  $\mathbb{R}^{d}$ . Assume Assumption 6.2 (the higher-order goodness condition) holds, then Algorithm 6.4, given  $y_{1}, \ldots, y_{n}$  and  $\sigma$ , terminates after at most  $2\varepsilon n + 1$  iterations and computes  $\hat{\mu}$  such that

$$
\left\| \hat {\mu} - \mu^ {*} \right\| \leqslant O \left(\frac {\left\| \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} f (\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {*}) \right\|}{\alpha} + \frac {\varepsilon^ {1 - \frac {1}{2 k}}}{\alpha} \cdot \sigma\right).
$$

Moreover each iteration can be implemented in time  $n^{O(1)} \cdot d^{O(\ell)}$ .

Additionally to the notation introduced in Section 5, consider the following polynomial in formal variables  $v$

$$
p ^ {w} (v) = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} \langle v, f (y _ {i} - \hat {\mu}) \rangle^ {2 k}.
$$

In what follows we will write  $p^{w(t)}$  as just  $p^{(t)}$ .

Algorithm 6.4 (Filtering Algorithm).

Input:  $\varepsilon$ -corrupted sample  $y_{1},\ldots ,y_{n}$  and  $\sigma >0$

Output: Location estimate  $\hat{\mu}$

Let  $w^{(0)} = \frac{1}{n}$  
- Compute  $\hat{\mu}^{(0)} = \min_{\mu \in \mathbb{R}^d}\mathcal{L}^{w^{(0)}}(\mu)$ .  
Let  $t = 0$  
- while there is no degree- $\ell$  SoS proof (in variables  $v$ ) that  $p^{(t)}(v) \leqslant (100 \cdot \sigma \cdot \|v\|)^{2k}$  do

- Find a degree- $\ell$  pseudo-expectation that satisfies the constraint  $\|v\|^2 = 1$  such that  $\tilde{\mathbb{E}} p^t(v) > (99 \cdot \sigma)^{2k}$ .  
- For  $i \in [n]$ , compute  $\tau_i^{(t)} = \tilde{\mathbb{E}}\left\langle v, f(\hat{\mu}^{(t)} - y_i)\right\rangle^{2k}$ .  
- For  $i \in [n]$ , set  $w_i^{(t+1)} = \left( 1 - \frac{\tau_i}{\tau_{\max}} \right) w_i^{(t)}$ , where  $\tau_{\max} = \max_{i \in [n]} \tau_i$ .  
- Compute  $\hat{\mu}^{(t + 1)} = \min_{\mu \in \mathbb{R}^d}f^{w^{(t + 1)}}(\mu)$ .  
-  $t\gets t + 1$

Output  $\hat{\mu}^{(t)}$

# 7 Product Distributions

In this section we prove that  $(\alpha, \rho)$ -semi-product distributions satisfy Assumption 5.1 with  $L = 1$ ,  $\sigma = O(\rho)$  and with entrywise Huber loss function with parameter  $2\rho$

$$
F (x) = \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {d} \Phi_ {2 \rho} \left(x _ {j}\right)
$$

with probability  $1 - \delta$  as long as  $n \gtrsim \frac{d + \log(1 / \delta)}{\alpha^2}$ . Moreover, if in addition  $n \gtrsim d^k \cdot \log(d / \delta)$ , then Assumption 6.2 are also satisfied. Combining the above with Theorem 6.3 we get Theorem 1.3.

Note that  $f = \nabla F$  is 1-Lipschitz.  $\varepsilon \lesssim \alpha^2$  holds by our assumption on  $\varepsilon$ . In the next to lemmas we prove the third assumption from Assumption 5.1.

Lemma 7.1. Let  $w \in \mathcal{W}_{2\varepsilon}$  for  $\varepsilon \lesssim \alpha$ . Suppose that  $n \gtrsim \log(d)/\alpha$ . Then with probability  $1 - \exp(-\Omega(\alpha n))$  for all  $u \in \mathbb{R}^d$  such that  $\|u\|_{\max} \leqslant \rho$ ,

$$
\mathcal {L} ^ {w} (\boldsymbol {\mu} ^ {*} + \boldsymbol {u}) - \mathcal {L} ^ {w} (\boldsymbol {\mu} ^ {*}) - \left\langle \nabla \mathcal {L} ^ {w} (\boldsymbol {\mu} ^ {*}), \boldsymbol {u} \right\rangle \geqslant \frac {\alpha}{4} \cdot \| \boldsymbol {u} \| ^ {2}.
$$

Proof. Let  $j \in [d]$  and  $\zeta_i = \mu - y_i$ .

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {j} ^ {w} \left(\mu_ {j} ^ {*} + u _ {j}\right) - \mathcal {L} _ {j} ^ {w} \left(\mu_ {j} ^ {*}\right) - \left(\mathcal {L} _ {j} ^ {w}\right) ^ {\prime} \left(\mu_ {j} ^ {*}\right) \cdot u _ {j} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} \left[ \Phi \left(\zeta_ {i j} + u _ {j}\right) - \Phi \left(\zeta_ {i j}\right) - u _ {j} \cdot \phi \left(\zeta_ {i j}\right) \right].
$$

Let  $M$  be the set of uncorrupted samples such that  $|\zeta_{ij}| = |\pmb{\eta}_{ij}| \leqslant h / 2$ . Note that for  $i \in M$ ,  $\Phi (\zeta_{ij} + u_j) = \frac{1}{2} |\zeta_{ij} + u_j|^2$  and  $\Phi (\zeta_{ij} + u_j) = \frac{1}{2} |\zeta_{ij}|^2$ . Hence

$$
\sum_ {i \in M} w _ {i} \left[ \Phi \left(\zeta_ {i j} + u _ {j}\right) - \Phi \left(\zeta_ {i j}\right) - u _ {j} \cdot \phi \left(\zeta_ {i j}\right) \right] = \frac {1}{2} \sum_ {i \in M} w _ {i} u _ {j} ^ {2}.
$$

And by convexity,

$$
\sum_ {i \not \in M} w _ {i} \left[ \Phi (\| \zeta_ {i} + u \|) - \Phi (\| \zeta_ {i} \|) - \left\langle f (\zeta_ {i}), u \right\rangle \right] \geqslant 0.
$$

Since  $w\in \mathcal{W}_{2\varepsilon}$

$$
\sum_ {i \in M} \left| w _ {i} - \frac {1}{n} \right| \leqslant 2 \varepsilon .
$$

Hence

$$
\sum_ {i \in M} w _ {i} \geqslant \frac {| M |}{n} - 2 \varepsilon .
$$

By Chernoff bound,

$$
| M | \geqslant 0. 9 \alpha - \varepsilon \geqslant \alpha / 2
$$

with probability  $1 - \exp(-\alpha n)$ . The result follows from union bound over all  $j \in [d]$ .

Lemma 7.2. Let  $w \in \mathcal{W}_{2\varepsilon}$  for  $\varepsilon \lesssim \alpha$ , and suppose that  $n \gtrsim \log(d)/\alpha^2$ . Then with probability  $1 - \exp(-\Omega(\alpha^2 n))$ ,

$$
\left\langle \nabla \mathcal {L} ^ {w} \left(\mu^ {*}\right), \mu^ {*} - \hat {\mu} ^ {w} \right\rangle \geqslant \frac {\alpha}{4} \| \hat {\mu} ^ {w} - \mu^ {*} \| ^ {2}.
$$

Proof. Fix  $j \in d$  and let  $u_{j} = \mu_{j}^{*} - \hat{\mu}_{j}(w)$ . Let  $u_{j}'$  and  $t$  be such that

$$
| u _ {j} ^ {\prime} | \leqslant \rho ,
$$

$$
\hat {\mu} _ {j} (w) = \mu_ {j} ^ {*} + t \cdot u _ {j} ^ {\prime}.
$$

It follows that  $t = \max \left\{1, |u_j|\right\}$ . Clearly,  $\hat{\mu}_j(w)$  is the (unique) minimizer of  $\mathcal{L}_j^w$ . Let  $\hat{\mu}_j' = \mu_j^* + u_j'$ , by convexity of  $\mathcal{L}_j^w$  it holds that  $\mathcal{L}_j^w(\hat{\mu}_j') \leqslant \mathcal{L}_j^w(\mu_j^*)$ . Since  $|u_j'| \leqslant \rho$  it follows by Lemma 7.1 that

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {j} ^ {w} \left(\mu_ {j} ^ {*}\right) \geqslant \mathcal {L} _ {j} ^ {w} \left(\hat {\mu} _ {j} ^ {\prime}\right) \geqslant \mathcal {L} _ {j} ^ {w} \left(\mu_ {j} ^ {*}\right) + \left(\mathcal {L} _ {j} ^ {w}\right) ^ {\prime} \left(\mu_ {j} ^ {*}\right) \cdot (- u _ {j} ^ {\prime}) + \frac {\alpha}{4} \left(u _ {j} ^ {\prime}\right) ^ {2}
$$

with probability at least  $1 - \exp (-\Omega (\alpha n))$ . Rearranging yields

$$
\left| u _ {j} ^ {\prime} \right| \leqslant \frac {4}{\alpha} \cdot \left(\mathcal {L} _ {j} ^ {w}\right) ^ {\prime} \left(\mu_ {j} ^ {*}\right).
$$

We next examine

$$
\begin{array}{l} \left(\mathcal {L} _ {j} ^ {w}\right) ^ {\prime} \left(\mu_ {j} ^ {*}\right) = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} \phi \left(\left(y _ {i}\right) _ {j} - \mu_ {j} ^ {*}\right) \\ = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} \phi (\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i j}) + \sum_ {i \in S _ {b}} ^ {n} w _ {i} \left[ \phi \left(\left(y _ {i}\right) _ {j} - \mu_ {j} ^ {*}\right) - \phi \left(\left(\boldsymbol {y} _ {i} ^ {*}\right) _ {j} - \mu_ {j} ^ {*}\right) \right]. \\ \end{array}
$$

By Hoeffding's inequality,

$$
\left| \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} \phi (\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i j}) \right| \leqslant \left| \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \phi (\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i j}) \right| + 2 \rho \varepsilon \leqslant 2 \rho \tau / \sqrt {n} + 4 \rho \varepsilon
$$

with probability at least  $1 - \exp (-\tau^2 /2)$

The second term can be bounded as follows:

$$
\sum_ {i \in S _ {b}} w _ {i} \cdot \left[ \phi \left(\boldsymbol {\mu} _ {j} ^ {*} - (\boldsymbol {y} _ {i}) _ {j}\right) - \phi \left(\boldsymbol {\mu} _ {j} ^ {*} - (\boldsymbol {y} _ {i} ^ {*}) _ {j}\right) \right] \leqslant 4 \rho \varepsilon ,
$$

where we used that for all  $x, y \in \mathbb{R}$  it holds that  $|\phi(x) - \phi(y)| \leqslant 2\rho$  and  $\sum_{i \in S_b} w_i \leqslant 2\varepsilon$ . Putting everything together we obtain

$$
| u _ {j} ^ {\prime} | \leqslant \rho \frac {2 \tau}{\alpha \sqrt {n}} + \frac {4 \rho \varepsilon}{\alpha}
$$

with probability  $1 - \exp(-\tau^2 / 2)$ . For  $\tau = \alpha \sqrt{n} / 10, |u_j'| < \rho$ . Hence,  $t = 1$  since otherwise

$$
\left| u _ {j} \right| = \left| \hat {\mu} _ {j} (w) - \mu_ {j} ^ {*} \right| = t \cdot \left| u _ {j} ^ {\prime} \right| <   \left| u _ {j} \right|.
$$

It follows that  $u_{j}^{\prime} = u_{j}$  and thus  $|\hat{\mu}_j(w) - \mu_j^*| < \rho$ .

By Lemma 7.1

$$
\mathcal {L} ^ {w} (\boldsymbol {\mu} ^ {*}) \geqslant \mathcal {L} ^ {w} (\hat {\boldsymbol {\mu}}) \geqslant \mathcal {L} ^ {w} (\boldsymbol {\mu} ^ {*}) + \left\langle \nabla \mathcal {L} ^ {w} (\boldsymbol {\mu} ^ {*}), - u \right\rangle + \frac {\alpha}{4} \| u \| ^ {2},
$$

Hence

$$
\left\langle \nabla \mathcal {L} ^ {w} \big (\mu^ {*} \big), u \right\rangle \geqslant \frac {\alpha}{4} \| u \| ^ {2}.
$$

In the following lemma we show that assumption 4 of Assumption 5.1 is satisfied for  $\sigma = O(\rho)$  with probability  $1 - \delta$  as long as  $n \gtrsim \frac{d + \log(1 / \delta)}{\alpha^2}$ .

Lemma 7.3. With probability at least  $1 - \delta$

$$
\left\| \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} f (\pmb {\eta} _ {i}) \right\| \leqslant 1 0 \rho \sqrt {\frac {d + \log (1 / \delta)}{n}}.

Proof. Let  $u \in \mathbb{R}^d$  be some fixed vector. Since  $\mathrm{sign}(\eta)$  is independent of  $\mathrm{abs}(\eta)$ , random variables  $\tilde{\pmb{u}}_{ij} = u_j \cdot |\pmb{\eta}_{ij}|$  are independent of  $\mathrm{sign}(\eta)$ . By Hoeffding's inequality

\left| \left\langle \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} f (\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i}), u \right\rangle \right| = \left| \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {d} \operatorname {s i g n} (\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i j}) \tilde {\boldsymbol {u}} _ {i j} \right| \leqslant 2 \rho \cdot \| u \| \cdot \tau / \sqrt {n}

$$

Hence by union bound,

$$
\left\| \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} f (\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i}) \right\| = \sup _ {\| u \| \leqslant 1} \left| \left\langle \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} f (\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i}), u \right\rangle \right| \leqslant 2 \sup _ {u \in \mathcal {N} _ {1 / 2}} \left| \left\langle \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} f (\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i}), u \right\rangle \right| \leqslant 4 \rho \sqrt {\frac {2 \ln (6) \cdot d + \log (1 / \delta)}{n}}.

In the following lemma we show that assumption 5 of Assumption 5.1 is satisfied for  $\sigma = O(\rho)$  with probability  $1 - \delta$  as long as  $n \gtrsim d + \log(1 / \delta)$ .

Lemma 7.4. Let  $\delta \in (0,1)$  and suppose that  $n\geqslant d + \log (1 / \delta)$ . Then

$$
\left\| \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} f (\pmb {\eta} _ {i}) (f (\pmb {\eta} _ {i})) ^ {\top} \right\| \leqslant O (\rho^ {2})
$$

with probability at least  $1 - \delta$ .

Proof. Let  $u \in \mathbb{R}^d$  be a fixed vector. Since the random vector  $\mathrm{sign}(\eta)$  is independent of  $\mathrm{abs}(\eta)$ , the random vector  $\mathrm{abs}(f(\eta)) \circ u$  with entries  $u_j \cdot |\eta(j)|$  is independent of  $\mathrm{sign}(\eta)$ . Since the entries of  $\mathrm{sign}(\eta)$  are iid Rademacher, for every positive integer  $k$ ,

$$
\begin{array}{l} \sup  _ {\| u \| \leqslant 1} \mathbb {E} \left\langle f (\boldsymbol {\eta}), u \right\rangle^ {2 k} \leqslant \sup  _ {\| u \| \leqslant 1} \mathbb {E} \left\langle \operatorname {s i g n} (\boldsymbol {\eta}), \operatorname {a b s} (f (\boldsymbol {\eta})) \circ u \right\rangle^ {2 k} \\ \leqslant \sup  _ {\| u \| \leqslant 2 \rho} \mathbb {E} \left\langle \operatorname {s i g n} (\boldsymbol {\eta}), u \right\rangle^ {2 k} \\ \leqslant \left(2 \rho\right) ^ {2 k} \sup  _ {\| u \| \leqslant 1} \mathbb {E} \left\langle \operatorname {s i g n} (\eta), u \right\rangle^ {2 k} \\ \leqslant \left(2 \rho\right) ^ {2 k}. \\ \end{array}
$$

Hence  $f(\eta)$  is  $2\rho$ -subgaussian and the result follows from concentration of empirical covariance for subgaussian distributions (see Theorem 6.5 in [Wai19]).

In the following lemma we show that assumption 5 of Assumption 6.2 is satisfied for  $\sigma = O(\rho)$  with probability  $1 - \delta$  as long as  $n \gtrsim d^k \cdot \log(d / \delta)$ .

Lemma 7.5. Let  $k$  be a positive integer and suppose that  $n \geqslant 10 \cdot d^k \cdot \log (d / \delta)$ . Then with probability at least  $1 - \delta$ , uniform distribution over the set  $\{\pmb{\eta}_1, \dots, \pmb{\eta}_n\}$  has  $(2k, 2k)$ -certifiable  $4\rho$ -bounded  $f$ -moments.

Proof. First we show that the random vector  $\pmb{\eta}$  has  $(2k, 2k)$ -certifiable  $2\rho$ -bounded  $f$ -moments. Let's call a multi-index  $\beta$  even if every element in  $\beta$  appears even number of times. Note that  $\beta$  is even iff  $\beta = 2\beta'$  for a multi-index  $\beta'$  of size  $k$ . Denote by  $\mathcal{E}$  the set of all even multi-indices of size  $2k$ . Let  $v_1, \ldots, v_d$  be variables, and consider the polynomial  $\mathbb{E}\bigl(f(\pmb{\eta}), v\bigr)^{2k}$ . It follows that

$$
\begin{array}{l} \left| \frac {v}{2 k} \mathbb {E} \left\langle f (\boldsymbol {\eta}), v \right\rangle^ {2 k} = \mathbb {E} \mathbb {E} \left[ \left\langle \operatorname {s i g n} (\boldsymbol {\eta}), \operatorname {a b s} (f (\boldsymbol {\eta})) \circ v \right\rangle^ {2 k} \mid \operatorname {a b s} (f (\boldsymbol {\eta})) \right] \right. \\ = \mathbb {E} \sum_ {\beta \in \mathcal {E}} \mathbb {E} \left[ \operatorname {s i g n} (\boldsymbol {\eta}) ^ {\beta} \left(\operatorname {a b s} (f (\boldsymbol {\eta})) \circ v\right) ^ {\beta} \mid \operatorname {a b s} (f (\boldsymbol {\eta})) \right] \\ = \mathbb {E} \sum_ {\beta \in \mathcal {E}} \left(\operatorname {a b s} \left(f (\boldsymbol {\eta})\right) \circ v\right) ^ {\beta} \\ \leqslant \left(2 \rho\right) ^ {2 k} \sum_ {\beta \in \mathcal {E}} v ^ {\beta} \\ = \left(2 \rho\right) ^ {2 k} \sum_ {| \beta^ {\prime} | = k} v ^ {2 \beta^ {\prime}} \\ = \left(2 \rho\right) ^ {2 k} \| v \| ^ {2 k}. \\ \end{array}
$$

Hence  $\pmb{\eta}$  has  $(2k, 2k)$ -certifiable  $2\rho$ -bounded  $f$ -moments. Since  $\forall x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ ,  $\left\| f(x) \right\| \leqslant 2\rho \sqrt{d}$ , the result follows from Lemma A.4.

# 8 Elliptical Distributions

In this section we use a different normalization from the one described in the Introduction. Concretely, we assume that  $\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma) = d$  and that for some  $\rho > 0$  and  $\alpha \in (0,1)$ ,  $\mathbb{P}\left[R \leqslant \rho \sqrt{d}\right] \geqslant \alpha$ . For

$\alpha = 1/2$ , this is the same model as in the Introduction, since we can multiply  $\Sigma^{1/2}$  by  $s = \sqrt{d / \mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma)}$  and divide  $R$  by  $s$ . Hence the new parameter  $\rho$  written in terms of the parameters defined in the Introduction is equal to  $\sqrt{2}/s = \sqrt{2 \operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma)/d}$ . Note that  $\rho \| \Sigma \|$  in new notation is equal to  $\sqrt{2} \| \Sigma \|$  in the notation used in the Introduction.

We assume that  $\rho$  is known. In the case of unknown  $\rho$  and  $\alpha \geqslant \Omega(1)$ , we can use some estimation for  $\rho$  and obtain the same guarantees as for known  $\rho$  (up to a constant factor). We describe the estimation of  $\rho$  in Section 8.1.

We prove that elliptical distributions satisfy Assumption 5.1 and with  $L = 1$ ,  $\sigma = O(\rho \| \Sigma \|$  ) and a function  $F:\mathbb{R}^d\to \mathbb{R}$  defined as follows

$$
F (x) = \Phi_ {2 0 \rho \sqrt {d}} (\| x \|),
$$

with probability  $1 - \delta$  as long as  $n \gtrsim \frac{d\log(d / \delta)}{\alpha^2}$ . Moreover, if in addition  $n \gtrsim d^k \cdot \log (d / \delta)$ , then Assumption 6.2 are also satisfied. Combining the above with Theorem 6.3 we get Theorem 1.4.

Note that  $f = \nabla F$  is 1-Lipschitz.  $\varepsilon \lesssim \alpha^2$  holds by our assumption on  $\varepsilon$ . In the next three lemmas we prove the third assumption from Assumption 5.1.

Lemma 8.1. Suppose that  $d \geqslant 100$ . Then

$$
\mathbb {P} \left[ \left\| R \Sigma^ {1 / 2} u \right\| \leqslant h / 2 \right] \geqslant 0. 8 \alpha ,
$$

where  $h = 20\rho \sqrt{d}$ .

Proof. Let  $g \sim N(0, 4\rho^2\Sigma)$  be a  $d$ -dimensional Gaussian vector such that  $g = R'\Sigma^{1/2}u$ , where  $R'$  is independent of  $R$  and has distribution of the norm of Gaussian vector  $N(0, 2\rho^2\mathrm{Id})$ . Note that  $\operatorname{Tr}\big(\mathbb{E}gg^\top\big) = 4\rho^2d$ . Applying Fact A.6 with  $t = 2$ , we get

$$
\mathbb {P} \left[ \left\| g \right\| \geqslant 1 0 \rho \sqrt {d} \right] \leqslant \exp (- 2) \leqslant 0. 1 5.
$$

Since

$$
\mathbb {P} \left[ R ^ {\prime} \geqslant \rho \sqrt {d} \right] \geqslant 0. 9 9,
$$

we get

$$
\mathbb {P} \left[ \left\| g \right\| \leqslant 1 0 \rho \sqrt {d}, R ^ {\prime} \geqslant \rho \sqrt {d} \right] \geqslant 0. 8.
$$

Since  $\pmb{R}$  is independent of  $\pmb{g}$  and  $R'$ , we get

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathbb {P} \left[ \left\| R \Sigma^ {1 / 2} u \right\| \leqslant 1 0 \sqrt {d} \right] \geqslant \mathbb {P} \left[ \left\| R \Sigma^ {1 / 2} u \right\| \leqslant \left\| g \right\|, \left\| g \right\| \leqslant 1 0 \rho \sqrt {d} \right] \\ = \mathbb {P} \left[ R \leqslant R ^ {\prime}, \| g \| \leqslant 1 0 \rho \sqrt {d} \right] \\ \geqslant \mathbb {P} \left[ R \leqslant \rho \sqrt {d}, R ^ {\prime} \geqslant \rho \sqrt {d}, \| g \| \leqslant 1 0 \rho \sqrt {d} \right] \\ \geqslant 0. 8 \alpha . \\ \end{array}
$$

Lemma 8.2. Let  $w \in \mathcal{W}_{2\varepsilon}$  for  $\varepsilon \lesssim \alpha$ . Then with probability  $1 - \exp(-\Omega(\alpha n))$  for all  $u \in \mathbb{R}^d$  such that  $\| u \| \leqslant \rho \sqrt{d}$ ,

$$
\mathcal {L} ^ {w} \left(\mu^ {*} + u\right) - \mathcal {L} ^ {w} \left(\mu^ {*}\right) - \left\langle \nabla \mathcal {L} ^ {w} \left(\mu^ {*}\right), u \right\rangle \geqslant \frac {\alpha}{4} \cdot \| u \| ^ {2}.
$$

Proof. Let  $\zeta_{i} = \mu -y_{i}$

$$
\mathcal {L} ^ {w} (\boldsymbol {\mu} ^ {*} + \boldsymbol {u}) - \mathcal {L} ^ {w} (\boldsymbol {\mu} ^ {*}) - \left\langle \mathcal {L} ^ {w} (\boldsymbol {\mu} ^ {*}), \boldsymbol {u} \right\rangle = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} \left[ f (\| \zeta_ {i} + \boldsymbol {u} \|) - f (\| \zeta_ {i} \|) - \left\langle f (\zeta_ {i}), \boldsymbol {u} \right\rangle \right]
$$

Let  $M$  be the set of uncorrupted samples such that  $\| \zeta_i\| = \| \pmb {\eta}_i\| \leqslant h / 2$ . Note that for  $i\in M$ ,  $f(\| \zeta_i + u\|) = \frac{1}{2}\| \zeta_i + u\|^2$  and  $f(\| \zeta_i\|) = \frac{1}{2}\| \zeta_i\|^2$ . Hence

$$
\sum_ {i \in M} w _ {i} \left[ f (\| \zeta_ {i} + u \|) - f (\| \zeta_ {i} \|) - \left\langle f (\zeta_ {i}), u \right\rangle \right] = \frac {1}{2} \sum_ {i \in M} w _ {i} \| u \| ^ {2}.
$$

And by convexity,

$$
\sum_ {i \not \in M} w _ {i} \left[ f (\| \zeta_ {i} + u \|) - f (\| \zeta_ {i} \|) - \left\langle f (\zeta_ {i}), u \right\rangle \right] \geqslant 0.
$$

Since  $w\in \mathcal{W}_{2\varepsilon}$

$$
\sum_ {i \in M} \left| w _ {i} - \frac {1}{n} \right| \leqslant 2 \varepsilon .
$$

Hence

$$
\sum_ {i \in M} w _ {i} \geqslant \frac {| M |}{n} - 2 \varepsilon .
$$

By Lemma 8.1 and Chernoff bound,

$$
| M | \geqslant 0. 6 \alpha - \varepsilon \geqslant \alpha / 2
$$

with probability  $1 - \exp (-\alpha n)$

Lemma 8.3. Let  $w \in \mathcal{W}_{2\varepsilon}$  for  $\varepsilon \lesssim \alpha$ , and suppose that  $n \gtrsim \log(d)/\alpha^2$ . Then with probability  $1 - \exp(-\Omega(\alpha^2 n))$ ,

$$
\left\langle \nabla \mathcal {L} ^ {w} \left(\mu^ {*}\right), \mu^ {*} - \hat {\mu} ^ {w} \right\rangle \geqslant \frac {\alpha}{4} \| \hat {\mu} ^ {w} - \mu^ {*} \| ^ {2}.
$$

Proof. Let  $u = \mu^{*} - \hat{\mu}(w)$ . Let  $u'$  and  $t$  be such that

$$
\left\| u ^ {\prime} \right\| \leqslant \rho \sqrt {d},
$$

$$
\hat {\mu} (w) = \mu^ {*} + t \cdot u ^ {\prime}.
$$

It follows that  $t = \max \{1, \| u\| \}$ . Clearly,  $\hat{\mu} (w)$  is the (unique) minimizer of  $\mathcal{L}^w$ . Let  $\hat{\mu}' = \mu^{*} + u'$ , by convexity of  $\mathcal{L}^w$  it holds that  $\mathcal{L}^w (\hat{\mu}')\leqslant \mathcal{L}^w (\mu^*)$ . Since  $\| u^{\prime}\| \leqslant \rho \sqrt{d}$  it follows by Lemma 8.2 that

$$
\mathcal {L} ^ {w} (\boldsymbol {\mu} ^ {*}) \geqslant \mathcal {L} ^ {w} (\hat {\boldsymbol {\mu}} ^ {\prime}) \geqslant \mathcal {L} ^ {w} (\boldsymbol {\mu} ^ {*}) - \left\langle \nabla \mathcal {L} ^ {w} (\boldsymbol {\mu} ^ {*}), u \right\rangle + \frac {\alpha}{4} \| u ^ {\prime} \| ^ {2}
$$

with probability at least  $1 - \exp (-\Omega (\alpha n))$ . Rearranging yields

$$
\| u ^ {\prime} \| \leqslant \frac {4}{\alpha} \cdot \left\| \nabla \mathcal {L} ^ {w} \left(\mu^ {*}\right) \right\|.
$$

We next examine

$$
\nabla \mathcal {L} ^ {w} (\mu^ {*}) = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} f ((y _ {i}) - \mu^ {*})
$$

$$
= \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} f (\pmb {\eta} _ {i}) + \sum_ {i \in S _ {b}} ^ {n} w _ {i} \left[ f ((y _ {i}) - \mu^ {*}) - f ((\pmb {y} _ {i} ^ {*}) - \mu^ {*}) \right].
$$

By vector Bernstein inequality Fact A.2,

$$
\left\| \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} f (\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i}) \right\| \leqslant \left\| \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} f (\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i}) \right\| + 4 0 \varepsilon \rho \sqrt {d} \leqslant 2 0 0 \rho \sqrt {d} \cdot \left(\tau / \sqrt {n} + \tau^ {2} / n\right) + 4 0 \varepsilon \rho \sqrt {d}
$$

with probability at least  $1 - \exp (-\tau^2 /2)$

The second term can be bounded as follows:

$$
\sum_ {i \in S _ {b}} w _ {i} \cdot \left[ f \left(\mu^ {*} - \left(y _ {i}\right)\right) - f \left(\mu^ {*} - \left(\boldsymbol {y} _ {i} ^ {*}\right)\right) \right] \leqslant 4 0 \varepsilon \rho \sqrt {d},
$$

where we used that for all  $x, y \in \mathbb{R}$  it holds that  $|f(x) - f(y)| \leqslant 20\rho \sqrt{d}$  and  $\sum_{i \in S_b} w_i \leqslant 2\varepsilon$ . Putting everything together we obtain

$$
\| u ^ {\prime} \| \leqslant 2 0 0 \frac {\rho \sqrt {d}}{\alpha} \cdot \left(\tau / \sqrt {n} + \tau^ {2} / n\right) + \frac {4 0 \varepsilon \rho \sqrt {d}}{\alpha}
$$

with probability  $1 - \exp(-\tau^2 / 2)$ . For  $\tau = \alpha \sqrt{n} / 1000$ ,  $|u'| < \rho$ . Hence,  $t = 1$  since otherwise

$$
\left\| u \right\| = \left\| \hat {\mu} (w) - \mu^ {*} \right\| = t \cdot \left\| u ^ {\prime} \right\| <   \left\| u \right\|.
$$

It follows that  $u' = u$  and thus  $\| \hat{\mu}(w) - \mu^{*} \| < \rho \sqrt{d}$ .

By Lemma 8.2

$$
\mathcal {L} ^ {w} \left(\mu^ {*}\right) \geqslant \mathcal {L} ^ {w} \left(\hat {\mu}\right) \geqslant \mathcal {L} ^ {w} \left(\mu^ {*}\right) + \left\langle \nabla \mathcal {L} ^ {w} \left(\mu^ {*}\right), - u \right\rangle + \frac {\alpha}{4} \| u \| ^ {2},
$$

Hence

$$
\left\langle \nabla \mathcal {L} ^ {w} (\boldsymbol {\mu} ^ {*}), u \right\rangle \geqslant \frac {\alpha}{4} \| u \| ^ {2}.
$$

In the following lemma we show that assumption 4 of Assumption 5.1 is satisfied for  $\sigma = O(\rho \| \Sigma \|)$  with probability  $1 - \delta$  as long as  $n \gtrsim \frac{d\log(d / \delta)}{\alpha^2}$ .

Lemma 8.4. Let  $\delta \in (0,1)$  and suppose that  $n\geqslant \log (1 / \delta)$ . Then with probability at least  $1 - \delta$

\left\| \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} f (\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i}) \right\| \leqslant O \left(\rho \sqrt {\frac {d \log (1 / \delta)}{n}}\right).
$$

Proof. The lemma is a direct consequence of vector Bernstein inequality Fact A.2.  $\square$

In the following lemma we show that assumption 5 of Assumption 5.1 is satisfied for  $\sigma = O(\rho)$  with probability  $1 - \delta$  as long as  $n \gtrsim d\log (d / \delta)$ .

Lemma 8.5. Let  $\delta \in (0,1)$  and suppose that  $n\geqslant d\log (d / \delta)$ . Then

$$
\left\| \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} f (\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i}) (f (\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i})) ^ {\top} \right\| \leqslant O (\rho^ {2} \| \Sigma \|)
$$

with probability at least  $1 - \delta$ .

Proof. Let  $s(\boldsymbol{\eta}) = \frac{h}{\|\boldsymbol{\eta}\|} \boldsymbol{\eta} = \frac{h}{\|f(\boldsymbol{\eta})\|} f(\boldsymbol{\eta})$ , where  $h = 20\rho \sqrt{d}$ . Note that  $\|s(\boldsymbol{\eta})\| \geqslant \|f(\boldsymbol{\eta})\|$ . By Fact A.5,

$$
\sup  _ {\| u \| \leqslant 1} \mathbb {E} \left\langle f (\boldsymbol {\eta}), u \right\rangle^ {2} \leqslant \sup  _ {\| u \| \leqslant 1} \mathbb {E} \left\langle s (\boldsymbol {\eta}), u \right\rangle^ {2} \leqslant (2 0 \rho) ^ {2} \| \Sigma \|.
$$

By Fact A.3,

$$
\left\| \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} f (\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i}) (f (\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i})) ^ {\top} \right\| \leqslant O \left(\rho^ {2} \| \Sigma \| + \frac {\rho^ {2} d \cdot \log (d / \delta)}{n}\right) \leqslant O (\rho^ {2} \| \Sigma \|),
$$

where we used  $\| \Sigma \| \geqslant 1$

In the following lemma we show that assumption 5 of Assumption 6.2 is satisfied for  $\sigma = O(\rho \| \Sigma \|)$  with probability  $1 - \delta$  as long as  $n \gtrsim d^k \log (d / \delta)$ .

Lemma 8.6. Let  $k$  be a positive integer and suppose that  $n \geqslant 10 \cdot d^{k} \cdot \log (d / \delta)$  and

$$
\left\| \Sigma \right\| _ {F} \leqslant \frac {\operatorname {T r} (\Sigma)}{1 0 \sqrt {k \log d}}.
$$

Then with probability at least  $1 - \delta$ , uniform distribution over the set  $\{\pmb{\eta}_1, \dots, \pmb{\eta}_n\}$  has  $(2k, 2k)$ -certifiable  $O\left(\rho \sqrt{k\|\Sigma\|}\right)$ -bounded  $f$ -moments.

Proof. By Lemma B.4,  $\pmb{\eta}$  has  $(2k, 2k)$ -certifiable  $O\left(\rho \sqrt{k\|\Sigma\|}\right)$ -bounded  $f$ -moments. Since  $\forall x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ ,  $\left\| f(x) \right\| \leqslant 20\rho \sqrt{d}$ , and since  $\|\Sigma\| \geqslant 1$  the result follows from Lemma A.4.

# 8.1 Unknown  $\rho$

If  $\rho$  is not known and if  $\alpha = 1/2$  (or arbitrary constant), we can first divide the  $n$  samples into 2 subsamples of size at least  $n' = \lfloor n/2 \rfloor$ , and use the first half to learn  $\rho$ , and the second half for mean estimation.

To do this, let us take the first  $n'$  samples and again divide them into two subsamples of size at least  $n'' = \lfloor n'/2 \rfloor$ , and subtract the second half from the first half (if  $n'$  is odd, let us ignore the last sample). Then we get  $n''$  ( $\varepsilon$ -corrupted) iid copies  $\xi_1, \ldots, \xi_{n''}$  of an elliptically distributed vector with location 0 and the scatter matrix  $\Sigma$  as the original distribution. Moreover,  $R$  for this distribution satisfies  $\mathbb{P}\left[R \leqslant 2\rho\sqrt{d}\right] \geqslant \alpha^2 = 1/4$ . Let us compute and sort the norms of the samples, and let us take the maximal value  $b$  among the smallest 0.2 norms. If  $\varepsilon \leqslant 0.01$ , then by Chernoff bound with probability at least  $1 - \exp(-\Omega(\alpha n))$ ,  $b \leqslant 2\rho\sqrt{d}$  and  $\mathbb{P}[R \leqslant b \mid b] \geqslant 0.1$ .

Now, we take the second  $n'$  samples, divide them into two subsamples of size at least  $n'' = \lfloor n'/2 \rfloor$ , and add the second half to the first half (if  $n'$  is odd, let us ignore the last sample).

Since the initial noise distribution is symmetric about zero, we get (ε-corrupted) iid samples  $2\mu + \zeta_1, \ldots, 2\mu + \zeta_{n''}$ , where  $\zeta_i$  have the same distribution as  $\xi_i$  described above. Hence we can use  $h = 20b$  in our proofs and get the same guarantees as if we used  $h = 20\rho \sqrt{d}$  (up to a constant factor).
