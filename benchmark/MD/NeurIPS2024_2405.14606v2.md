# Abstract

In pioneering work from 2019, Barceló and coauthors identified logics that precisely match the expressive power of constant iteration-depth graph neural networks (GNNs) relative to properties definable in first-order logic. In this article, we give exact logical characterizations of recurrent GNNs in two scenarios: (1) in the setting with floating-point numbers and (2) with reals. For floats, the formalism matching recurrent GNNs is a rule-based modal logic with counting, while for reals we use a suitable infinitary modal logic, also with counting. These results give exact matches between logics and GNNs in the recurrent setting without relativising to a background logic in either case, but using some natural assumptions about floating-point arithmetic. Applying our characterizations, we also prove that, relative to graph properties definable in monadic second-order logic (MSO), our infinitary and rule-based logics are equally expressive. This implies that recurrent GNNs with reals and floats have the same expressive power over MSO-definable properties and shows that, for such properties, also recurrent GNNs with reals are characterized by a (finitary!) rule-based modal logic. In the general case, in contrast, the expressive power with floats is weaker than with reals. In addition to logic-oriented results, we also characterize recurrent GNNs, with both reals and floats, via distributed automata, drawing links to distributed computing models.

# 1 Introduction

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) [10, 26, 32] have proven to be highly useful for processing graph data in numerous applications that span a remarkable range of disciplines including bioinformatics [33], recommender systems [31], traffic forecasting [19], and a multitude of others. The success of GNNs in applications has stimulated lively research into their theoretical properties such as expressive power. A landmark result is due to Barceló et al. [5] which was among the first to characterize the expressive power of GNNs in terms of logic, see [11, 8, 6, 23] and references therein for related results. More precisely, Barceló et al. show that a basic GNN model with a constant number of iterations has exactly the same expressive power as graded modal logic GML in restriction to properties definable in first-order logic FO.

In this article, we advance the analysis of the expressive power of GNNs in two directions. First, we study the relation between GNN models based on real numbers, as mostly studied in theory, and GNN models based on floating point numbers, as mostly used in practice.

And second, we focus on a family of basic recurrent GNNs while previous research has mainly considered GNNs with a constant number of iterations and, in one case, recurrent GNNs with two termination conditions [23]. The GNNs studied in the current paper have a simple and natural termination condition: termination is signaled via designated feature vectors and thus the GNN "decides" by itself about when to terminate; this also means that termination can be learned in the training phase. This termination condition is enough to capture, e.g., termination after a pre-determined number of rounds as in [28]. We remark that some of our results also apply to constant iteration GNNs and to recurrent GNNs with a termination condition based on fixed points, as used in the inaugural work on GNNs [10]; see the conclusion for further details on the latter.

We provide three main results. The first one is that recurrent GNNs with floats, or  $\mathrm{GNN[F]s}$ , have the same expressive power as the graded modal substitution calculus GMSC [21, 1, 3]. This is a rule-based modal logic that extends the modal substitution calculus MSC [21] with counting modalities. MSC has been shown to precisely correspond to distributed computation models based on automata [21] and Boolean circuits [1]. It is related to the graded modal  $\mu$ -calculus, but orthogonal in expressive power. The correspondence between  $\mathrm{GNN[F]s}$  and GMSC is as follows.

Theorem 3.2. The following have the same expressive power: GNN[F]s, GMSC, and  $R$ -simple aggregate-combine GNN[F]s.

Here  $R$ -simple aggregate-combine GNN[F]s mean GNN[F]s that use basic aggregate-combine functions as specified by Barceló et al. [5] and the truncated ReLU as the non-linearity function, see Section 2.1 for the formalities. The theorem shows that an R-simple model of GNN[F]s suffices, and in fact GNN[F]s with a more complex architecture can be turned into equivalent R-simple ones. We emphasize that the characterization provided by Theorem 3.2 is absolute, that is, not relative to a background logic. In contrast, the characterization by Barceló et al. [5] is relative to first-order logic. Our characterization does rely, however, on an assumption about floating point arithmetics. We believe that this assumption is entirely natural as it reflects practical implementations of floats.

Our second result shows that recurrent GNNs with reals, or  $\mathrm{GNN}[\mathbb{R}]\mathrm{s}$ , have the same expressive power as the infinitary modal logic  $\omega$ -GML that consists of infinite disjunctions of GML-formulas.

Theorem 3.4. GNN  $[\mathbb{R}]$ s have the same expressive power as  $\omega$ -GML.

Again, this result is absolute. As we assume no restrictions on the arithmetics used in GNN  $[\mathbb{R}]\mathrm{s}$ , they are very powerful: with the infinitary logic  $\omega$ -GML it is easy to define even undecidable graph properties. We regard the theorem as an interesting theoretical upper bound on the expressivity of GNNs operating in an unrestricted, recurrent message passing scenario with messages flowing to neighbouring nodes. We note that GNN  $[\mathbb{R}]\mathrm{s}$  can easily be shown more expressive than GNN[F]s.

Our third result considers GNN[R]s and GNN[F]s relative to a very expressive background logic, probably the most natural choice in the recurrent GNN context: monadic second-order logic MSO.

Theorem 4.3. Let  $P$  be a property definable in MSO. Then  $P$  is expressible as a GNN[R] if and only if it is expressible as a GNN[F].

This result says that, remarkably, for the very significant and large class of MSO-definable properties, using actual reals with unrestricted arithmetic gives no more expressive power than using floats, by Theorem 3.2 even in the R-simple aggregate-combine setting. Thus, for this class of properties, the theoretical analyses from the literature do not diverge from the practical implementation! Taken together, the above results also imply that in restriction to MSO-definable properties, GNN[R]s are equivalent to the (finitary!) graded modal substitution calculus GMSC. In fact, all of the following have the same expressive power relative to MSO: GNN[R]s, GNN[F]s, R-simple aggregate-combine GNN[F]s, graded modal substitution calculus GMSC, and  $\omega$ -GML.

In passing, we also develop characterizations of GNNs in terms of distributed automata, which are also crucial tools in our proofs, building links between GNNs and distributed computing.

Potential future research as well as the limitations of the results presented in this article are discussed in the conclusion section.

Related Work. Barceló et al. [5] study aggregate-combine GNNs with a constant number of iterations. They characterize these—in restriction to properties definable in first-order logic—in terms of graded modal logic GML and show that a global readout mechanism leads to a model equivalent to the two-variable fragment of first-order logic with counting quantifiers  $\mathrm{FOC}^2$ . Our work extends the former result to include recurrence in a natural way while we leave studying global readouts as future work. Grohe [12] connects the guarded fragment of first-order logic with counting  $\mathrm{GFO + C}$  and polynomial-size bounded-depth circuits, linking (non-recurrent) GNNs to the circuit complexity class  $\mathrm{TC}^0$ . Benedikt et al. [6] use logics with a generalized form of counting via Presburger quantifiers to obtain characterizations for (non-recurrent) GNNs. It is worth mentioning that the expressive power of (basic) recurrent graph neural network models is invariant under the Weifeiler-Leman test, which has recently been studied in numerous works also in relation to GNNs [22, 34, 11, 5].

Pfluger et al. [23] investigate recurrent GNNs with two kinds of termination conditions: one based on reaching a fixed point when iteratively generating feature vectors, and one by terminating after a number of rounds determined by the size of the input graph. Their result is relative to a certain logic LocMMFP, introduced specifically for this purpose. LocMMFP extends first-order logic with a least fixed-point operator over unary monotone formulas, and their characterization is in terms of the two-way graded  $\mu$ -calculus. We remark that MSO significantly generalizes LocMMFP and that the graded two-way  $\mu$ -calculus is incomparable in expressive power to our GMSC. Pfluger et al. do not consider R-simple aggregate-combine GNNs. We view our work as complementing and being in the spirit of both Barceló et al. [5] and [23].

GNNs are essentially distributed systems, and logical characterizations for distributed systems have been studied widely. A related research direction begins with Hella et al. [14], Kuusisto [21] and Hella et al. [15] by results linking distributed computation models to modal logics. The articles [14] and [15] give characterizations of constant-iteration scenarios with modal logics, and [21] lifts the approach to recurrent message-passing algorithms via showing that the modal substitution calculus MSC captures the expressivity of finite distributed message passing automata. Building on the work on MSC, Reiter showed in [24] that the  $\mu$ -fragment of the modal  $\mu$ -calculus captures the expressivity of finite message passing automata in the asynchronous scenario. Ahvonen et al. [1] recently established a match between MSC and distributed systems based on circuits.

# 2 Preliminaries

We let  $\mathbb{Z}$ ,  $\mathbb{N}$ ,  $\mathbb{Z}_{+}$  and  $\mathbb{R}$  denote the sets of integers, non-negative integers, positive integers, and real numbers respectively. For all  $n \in \mathbb{N}$ , we let  $[n] := \{1, \ldots, n\}$  and  $[0; n] := \{0, \ldots, n\}$ . For any function  $f: X \to Y$ , we let  $\operatorname{dom}(f)$  denote the domain  $X$  of  $f$ . With  $|X|$  we denote the cardinality of the set  $X$ , with  $\mathcal{P}(X)$  the power set of  $X$  and with  $\mathcal{M}(X)$  the set of multisets over  $X$ , i.e., the set of functions  $X \to \mathbb{N}$ . With  $\mathcal{M}_k(X)$  we denote the set of  $k$ -multisets over  $X$ , i.e., the set of functions  $X \to [0; k]$ . Given a  $k$ -multiset  $M$  over  $X$  and  $x \in X$ , intuitively  $M(x) = n < k$  means that there are  $n$  copies of  $x$  and  $M(x) = k$  means that there are  $k$  or more copies of  $x$ . A set  $S$  of multisets is bounded if each  $M \in S$  is a  $k$ -multiset for the same  $k$ .

We let  $\mathrm{PROP} = \{p_i \mid i \in \mathbb{N}\}$  and  $\mathrm{VAR} = \{V_i \mid i \in \mathbb{N}\}$  denote countably infinite sets of proposition symbols and (Boolean) schema variables. We mostly use metasymbols  $p, q, \ldots$  and  $X, Y, \ldots$  to denote elements in PROP and VAR respectively. We work with (node-labeled) directed graphs (with self-loops allowed) that are represented as Kripke models. A Kripke model over a set  $\Pi \subseteq \mathrm{PROP}$  (or  $\Pi$ -model) is a tuple  $M = (W, R, V)$ , where  $W$  is a set of nodes,  $R \subseteq W \times W$  is an accessibility relation and  $V: W \to \mathcal{P}(\Pi)$  is a valuation function. Note that the proposition symbols serve as node labels, representing features. This is in line with previous investigations of the expressive power of GNNs. All our results easily extend to graphs that admit labels on both nodes and edges. A pointed Kripke model is a pair  $(M, w)$  where  $w \in W$ . Given a pointed model  $((W, R, V), w)$ , the set of in-neighbours of  $w$  is  $\{v \mid (v, w) \in R\}$  and the set of out-neighbours of  $w$  is  $\{v \mid (w, v) \in R\}$ . If clear from the context, we may say neighbors in place of out-neighbours. A node property is a class of pointed Kripke models and a node property over  $\Pi$  is a class of pointed  $\Pi$ -models. Graph properties (i.e., classes of graphs) can be considered a special case of node properties; a graph property  $\mathcal{G}$  corresponds to a node property  $\mathcal{N}$  if the following holds:  $M \in \mathcal{G}$  iff  $(M, w) \in \mathcal{N}$  for every node  $w$  of  $M$ . Henceforth a property means a node property.

# 2.1 Graph neural networks

A graph neural network (GNN) is a neural network architecture for graph-structured data. It may be viewed as a distributed system where the nodes of the (directed, node-labeled) input graph calculate with real numbers and communicate synchronously in discrete rounds. More formally, given  $d \in \mathbb{Z}_+$  and  $\Pi \subseteq \mathrm{PROP}$ , a recurrent graph neural network GNN[  $\mathbb{R}$ ] for  $(\Pi, d)$  is a tuple  $\mathcal{G} = (\mathbb{R}^d, \pi, \delta, F)$ . A recurrent graph neural network computes in a (node-labeled) directed graph (represented as a Kripke model) as follows. In any  $\Pi$ -model  $(W, R, V)$ , the initialization function  $\pi: \mathcal{P}(\Pi) \to \mathbb{R}^d$  assigns to each node  $v$  an initial feature vector or state  $x_v^0 = \pi(V(v))$ . In each round  $t = 1, 2, \ldots$ , every node computes a new feature vector  $x_v^t$  using a transition function  $\delta: \mathbb{R}^d \times \mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^d) \to \mathbb{R}^d, \delta(x, y) = \mathrm{COM}(x, \mathrm{AGG}(y))$ , which is a composition of an aggregation function AGG:  $\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^d) \to \mathbb{R}^d$  (typically sum, min, max or average) and a combination function COM:  $\mathbb{R}^d \times \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^d$  such that

$$
x _ {v} ^ {t} = \operatorname {C O M} \left(x _ {v} ^ {t - 1}, \operatorname {A G G} \left(\left\{\left\{x _ {u} ^ {t - 1} \mid (v, u) \in R \right\} \right\}\right)\right)
$$

where double curly brackets  $\{\{\dots\}\}$  denote multisets. The recurrent GNN  $\mathcal{G}$  accepts a pointed  $\Pi$ -model  $(M, w)$  if  $w$  visits (at least once) a state in the set of accepting feature vectors  $F \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ , i.e.,  $x_w^t \in F$  for some  $t \in \mathbb{N}$ . A constant-iteration GNN[  $\mathbb{R}$ ] is a pair

$(\mathcal{G}, L)$  where  $\mathcal{G}$  is a GNN $[\mathbb{R}]$  and  $L \in \mathbb{N}$ . It accepts a pointed model  $(M, w)$  if  $x_w^L \in F$ . Informally, we simply run a GNN $[\mathbb{R}]$  for  $L$  iterations and accept/reject based on the last iteration. We say that  $\mathcal{G}$  (resp.,  $(\mathcal{G}, L)$ ) defines (or expresses) a node property  $\mathcal{P}$  over  $\Pi$  if  $\mathcal{G}$  (resp.,  $(\mathcal{G}, L)$ ) accepts exactly the pointed  $\Pi$ -models in  $\mathcal{P}$ . A node property  $\mathcal{P}$  is definable by (or expressible as) a GNN $[\mathbb{R}]$  (resp. a constant-iteration GNN $[\mathbb{R}]$ ) if there exists a GNN $[\mathbb{R}]$  (resp. a constant-iteration GNN $[\mathbb{R}]$ ) that defines  $\mathcal{P}$ . Note that in GNNs, messages flow in the direction opposite to the edges of Kripke models, i.e., an edge  $(v, u) \in R$  means that  $v$  receives messages sent by  $u$ . This is only a convention that could be reversed via using a modal logic that scans the inverse relation of  $R$  instead of  $R$ ; we could also admit both directions, as in [23].

One common, useful and simple possibility for the aggregation and combination functions, also used by Barceló et al. (see [5], and also the papers [22, 13]) is defined by

$$
\operatorname {C O M} \left(x _ {v} ^ {t - 1}, \operatorname {A G G} \left(\{\{x _ {u} ^ {t - 1} \mid (v, u) \in R \} \}\right)\right) = f \left(x _ {v} ^ {t - 1} \cdot C + \sum_ {(v, u) \in R} x _ {u} ^ {t - 1} \cdot A + \mathbf {b}\right),
$$

where  $f\colon \mathbb{R}^d\to \mathbb{R}^d$  is a non-linearity function (such as the truncated ReLU also used by Barceló et al. in [5], defined by  $\mathrm{ReLU}^{*}(x) = \min (\max (0,x),1)$ ), and  $C,A\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$  are matrices and  $\mathbf{b}\in \mathbb{R}^d$ . We refer to GNNs that use aggregation and combination functions of this form and  $\mathrm{ReLU}^{*}$  as the non-linearity function as  $\mathbf{R}$ -simple aggregate-combine GNNs (here 'R' stands for  $\mathrm{ReLU}^{*}$ ).

Example 2.1. Reachability of a proposition symbol  $p$  is a node property  $\mathcal{P}$  containing pointed models  $(M, w)$  s.t. there is a path from  $w$  to some  $v$  with  $p \in V(v)$ . An R-simple aggregate-combine GNN can define  $\mathcal{P}$ : In round 0, the feature of  $v$  is set to 1 if  $p \in V(v)$  and else to 0. In later rounds, the feature of  $v$  is 1 if  $v$ 's feature was 1 or it gets 1 from its neighbours; else the feature is set to 0.

Remark 2.2. Notice that unrestricted  $\mathrm{GNN}[\mathbb{R}]\mathrm{s}$  can define, even in a single iteration, node properties such as that the number of immediate out-neighbours of a node is a prime number. In fact, for any  $U\subseteq \mathbb{N}$ , including any undecidable set  $U$ , a  $\mathrm{GNN}[\mathbb{R}]$  can define the property that the number  $l$  of immediate out-neighbours is in the set  $U$ . See [6] for related undecidability results.

Informally, a floating-point system contains a finite set of rational numbers and arithmetic operations  $+$  and  $\cdot$ . Formally, if  $p \in \mathbb{Z}_+$ ,  $n \in \mathbb{N}$  and  $\beta \in \mathbb{Z}_+ \setminus \{1\}$ , then a floating-point system is a tuple  $S = ((p, n, \beta), +, \cdot)$  that consists of the set  $D_S$  of all rationals accurately representable in the form  $0.d_1 \cdots d_p \times \beta^e$  or  $-0.d_1 \cdots d_p \times \beta^e$  where  $d_i \in [0; \beta - 1]$  and  $e \in \{-n, \dots, n\}$ . It also consists of arithmetic operations  $+$  and  $\cdot$  of type  $D_S \times D_S \to D_S$ . We adopt the common convention where  $+$  and  $\cdot$  are defined by first taking a precise sum/product w.r.t. reals and then rounding to the closest float in  $D_S$ , with ties rounding to the float with an even rightmost non-zero digit, e.g.,  $0.312 + 0.743$  evaluates to 1.06 if the real sum 1.055 is not in the float system. We typically just write  $S$  for  $D_S$ .

In GNNs, sum is a common aggregation function (also used in R-simple GNNs). As the sum of floats is not associative, counting the sum of a multiset of floats in different orders can give different results. In real-life implementations, the set of nodes  $W$  of the graph studied can typically be easily associated with an implicit linear order relation  $<^W$  (which is not part of the actual graph). It is then natural to count features of out-neighbours in the order  $<^W$ . However, this allows GNNs to distinguish between isomorphic nodes, which violates the desire that GNNs should be invariant under isomorphism. For example,

summing 1, -1 and 0.01 in two orders in a system where the numbers must be representable in the form  $0.d_{1}d_{2} \times 10^{e}$  or  $-0.d_{1}d_{2} \times 10^{e}$ : first  $(1 + (-1)) + 0.01 = 0 + 0.01 = 0.01$  while  $(1 + 0.01) + (-1) = 1 + (-1) = 0$ , since 1.01 is not representable in the system. A float GNN could distinguish two isomorphic nodes with such ordering of neighbours.

To ensure isomorphism invariance for GNNs with floats that use sum, it is natural to order the floats instead of the nodes. For example, adding floats in increasing order (of the floats) is a natural and simple choice. Summing in this increasing order is also used widely in applications, being a reasonable choice w.r.t. accuracy (see, e.g., [30],[25], [16]). Generally, floating point sums in applications have been studied widely, see for example [17]. Summing multisets of floats in increasing order leads to a bound in the multiplicities of the elements of the sum; see Proposition 2.3 for the formal statement. Before discussing its proof, we define the  $k$ -projection of a multiset  $M$  as  $M_{|k}$  where  $M_{|k}(x) = \min \{M(x), k\}$ . Given a multiset  $N$  of floats in  $S$ , we let  $\mathrm{SUM}_{\mathrm{S}}(N)$  denote the output of the sum  $f_1 + \dots + f_\ell$  where (1)  $f_i$  appears  $N(f_i)$  times (i.e., its multiplicity) in the sum, (2) the floats appear and are summed in increasing order and (3)  $+$  is according to  $S$ .

Proposition 2.3. For all floating-point systems  $S$ , there exists a  $k \in \mathbb{N}$  such that for all multisets  $M$  over floats in  $S$ , we have  $\mathrm{SUM}_S(M) = \mathrm{SUM}_S(M_{|k})$ .

Proof. (Sketch) See also in Appendix A.1. Let  $a = 0.0 \cdots 01 \times \beta^e$  and  $b = 0.10 \cdots 0 \times \beta^{e+1}$ . Now, notice that given a large enough  $n > 2$ , summing  $a$  to itself  $m > n$  times will always give  $b$ .

Due to Proposition 2.3, GNNs using the sum in increasing order are bounded in their ability to fully count their out-neighbours. Thus, it is natural to assume that floating-point GNNs are bounded GNNs, i.e., the aggregation function can be written in the form  $\mathcal{M}_k(U^d) \to U^d$  for some bound  $k \in \mathbb{N}$ , i.e., for every multiset  $M \in \mathcal{M}(U^d)$ , we have  $\mathrm{AGG}(M) = \mathrm{AGG}(M_{|k})$ , where  $M_{|k}$  is the  $k$ -projection of  $M$  (and  $U^d$  is the set of states of the GNN). We finally give a formal definition for floating-point GNNs: a floating-point graph neural network (GNN[F]) is simply a bounded GNN where the set of states and the domains and co-domains of the functions are restricted to some floating-point system  $S$  (note that  $S$  can be any floating-point system). A GNN[F] obtained by removing the condition on boundedness is called an unrestricted GNN[F]. Notice that by default and unless otherwise stated, a GNN[ $\mathbb{R}$ ] is unbounded, whereas a GNN[F] is bounded. Now, it is immediately clear that unrestricted GNN[F] $s$  (with an unrestricted aggregation and combination function) are more expressive than GNN[F] $s$ : defining the property that a node has an even number of out-neighbours is easy with unrestricted GNN[F] $s$ , but the centers of two star graphs with  $k$  and  $k + 1$  neighbours cannot be distinguished by any bounded GNN[F] with bound  $k$ .

# 2.2 Logics

We then define the logics relevant for this paper. The set of  $\Pi$ -formulae of graded modal logic (GML) is given by the grammar

$$
\varphi := \top | p | \neg \varphi | \varphi \land \varphi | \diamond_ {\geq k} \varphi ,
$$

where  $p \in \Pi$  and  $k \in \mathbb{N}$ . The connectives  $\vee, \to, \leftrightarrow$  are considered abbreviations in the usual way. Let  $M = (W, R, V)$  be a  $\Pi$ -model and  $w \in W$ . The truth of a formula  $\varphi$  in a

pointed model  $(M, w)$  (denoted  $M, w \models \varphi$ ) is defined as usual for the Boolean operators and  $\top$ , while for  $p \in \Pi$  we define that  $M, w \models p$  iff  $p \in V(w)$ . For  $\diamond_{\geq k}\varphi$  we define

$$
M, w \models \diamond_ {\geq k} \varphi \text {i f f} | \{v \mid M, v \models \varphi , (w, v) \in R \} | \geq k.
$$

That is,  $\diamond_{\geq k}\varphi$  is true in  $(M,w)$  iff  $\varphi$  is true in at least  $k$  out-neighbours of  $w$ . We use the abbreviations  $\diamond \varphi \coloneqq \diamond_{\geq 1}\varphi$ ,  $\square \varphi \coloneqq \neg \diamond \neg \varphi$  and  $\diamond_{= n}\varphi \coloneqq \diamond_{\geq n}\varphi \land \neg \diamond_{\geq n + 1}\varphi$ .

The set of  $\Pi$ -formulae of  $\omega$ -GML is given by the grammar

$$
\varphi := \psi \mid \bigvee_ {\psi \in S} \psi ,
$$

where  $\psi$  is a  $\Pi$ -formula of GML and  $S$  is an at most countable set of  $\Pi$ -formulae of GML. The truth of infinite disjunctions is defined in the obvious way:

$$
M, w \models \bigvee_ {\psi \in S} \psi \iff M, w \models \psi \text {f o r s o m e} \psi \in S.
$$

We next introduce the graded modal substitution calculus (or GMSC), which is an extension of the modal substitution calculus [21, 1, 3] with counting capabilities. Let  $\mathcal{T} = \{X_1,\ldots ,X_n\} \subseteq \mathrm{VAR}$ . The set of  $(\Pi ,\mathcal{T})$ -schemata is defined by the grammar

$$
\varphi := \top \mid p \mid X \mid \neg \varphi \mid \varphi \land \varphi \mid \diamond_ {\geq k} \varphi
$$

where  $p \in \Pi$ ,  $X \in \mathcal{T}$  and  $k \in \mathbb{N}$ . A  $(\Pi, \mathcal{T})$ -program  $\Lambda$  of GMSC consists of two lists of expressions

$$
X _ {1} (0) \vdots - \varphi_ {1} \qquad X _ {1} \vdots - \psi_ {1}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{c c c} \vdots & & \\ & \vdots & \\ & & \end{array}
$$

$$
X _ {n} (0): - \varphi_ {n} \qquad X _ {n}: - \psi_ {n}
$$

where  $\varphi_1, \ldots, \varphi_n$  are  $\Pi$ -formulae of GML and  $\psi_1, \ldots, \psi_n$  are  $(\Pi, \mathcal{T})$ -schemata of GMSC. A program is also associated with a set of appointed predicates  $\mathcal{A} \subseteq \mathcal{T}$ . The expressions  $X_i(0) \vdash \varphi_i$  are called terminal clauses and the expressions  $X_i \vdash \psi_i$  are called iteration clauses. The schema variable  $X_i$  in front of the clause is called the head predicate and the formula  $\varphi_i$  (or schema  $\psi_i$ ) is called the body of the clause. The terminal and iteration clauses are the rules of the program. When we do not need to specify  $\mathcal{T}$ , we may refer to a  $(\Pi, \mathcal{T})$ -program as a  $\Pi$ -program of GMSC. Now, the  $n$ th iteration formula  $X_i^n$  of a head predicate  $X_i$  in round  $n \in \mathbb{N}$  is defined as follows. The zeroth iteration formula  $X_i^0$  is  $\varphi_i$  and the  $(n + 1)$ st iteration formula  $X_i^{n + 1}$  is obtained by replacing each head predicate  $Y$  appearing in schema  $\psi_i$  by the formula  $Y^n$ . We write  $M, w \models \Lambda$  and say that  $\Lambda$  accepts  $(M, w)$  iff  $M, w \models X^n$  for some appointed predicate  $X \in \mathcal{A}$  and some  $n \in \mathbb{N}$ .

We recall that monadic second-order logic MSO is obtained as an extension of FO by quantification of unary relation variables  $X$ , i.e., if  $\varphi$  is an MSO-formula, then so are  $\forall X\varphi$  and  $\exists X\varphi$ . Given a  $\Pi \subseteq \mathrm{PROP}$ , an FO or an MSO formula  $\varphi$  over  $\Pi$  is a formula over the vocabulary which contains a unary predicate for each  $p \in \Pi$  and the accessibility relation symbol  $R$ . Equality is admitted.

Given an  $\omega$ -GML-formula, GMSC-schema, GMSC-program, or a rule of a program  $\varphi$ , the modal depth (resp. width) of  $\varphi$  is the maximum number of nested diamonds in  $\varphi$  (resp. the maximum number  $k \in \mathbb{N}$  that appears in a diamond  $\diamond_{\geq k}$  in  $\varphi$ ). If an  $\omega$ -GML-formula has no maximum depth (resp., width), its depth (resp., width) is  $\infty$ . If an  $\omega$ -GML-formula

has finite modal depth (resp., width), it is depth-bounded (resp., width-bounded). The formula depth of a GML-formula or GMSC-schema is the maximum number of nested operators  $\neg$ ,  $\land$  and  $\diamondsuit_{\geq k}$ .

Given a  $\Pi$ -program of GMSC or a  $\Pi$ -formula of  $\omega$ -GML  $\varphi$  (respectively, a formula  $\psi(x)$  of MSO or FO over  $\Pi$ , where the only free variable is the first-order variable  $x$ ), we say that  $\varphi$  (resp.,  $\psi(x)$ ) defines (or expresses) a node property  $\mathcal{P}$  over  $\Pi$  if for each pointed  $\Pi$ -model  $(M, w): (M, w) \in \mathcal{P}$  iff  $M, w \models \varphi$  (or resp.  $M \models \psi(w)$ ). A node property  $\mathcal{P}$  is definable (or expressible) in GMSC (resp., in  $\omega$ -GML, FO or MSO) if there is a  $\Pi$ -program of GMSC (resp., a  $\Pi$ -formula of  $\omega$ -GML, FO or MSO) defining  $\mathcal{P}$ .

Proposition 2.4. Node properties definable in GMSC are definable in  $\omega$ -GML, but not vice versa.

Proof. A GMSC-definable property is  $\omega$ -GML-definable by  $\bigvee_{X_i \in \mathcal{A}, n \in \mathbb{N}} \psi_i^n$  where  $\psi_i^n$  is the iteration formula of appointed predicate  $X_i$  in round  $n$ . Like GNN[ $\mathbb{R}$ ]s,  $\omega$ -GML can define undecidable properties (cf. Remark 2.2). Clearly GMSC-programs  $\Lambda$  cannot, as configurations defined by  $\Lambda$  in a finite model eventually loop, i.e., the truth values of iteration formulae start repeating cyclically.

Example 2.5 ([21]). A pointed Kripke model  $(M, w)$  has the centre-point property if there exists an  $n \in \mathbb{N}$  such that every directed path starting from  $w$  leads to a node with no out-neighbours in exactly  $n$  steps. It is easy to see that the program  $X(0): -\square \bot$ ,  $X: -\diamond X \land \square X$ , where  $X$  is an appointed predicate, defines the centre-point property. In [21], it was established that the centre-point property is not definable in MSO and that there are properties definable in  $\mu$ -calculus and MSO that are not definable in MSC (e.g. non-reachability), with the same proofs applying to GMSC.

While GMSC is related to the graded modal  $\mu$ -calculus ( $\mu$ GML), which originates from [20] and is used in [23] to characterize a recurrent GNN model,  $\mu$ GML and GMSC are orthogonal in expressivity. Iteration in GMSC need not be over a monotone function and does not necessarily yield a fixed point, and there are no syntactic restrictions that would, e.g., force schema variables to be used only positively as in  $\mu$ GML. The centre-point property from Example 2.5 is a simple property not expressible in  $\mu$ GML (as it is not even expressible in MSO, into which  $\mu$ GML translates). Conversely, GMSC offers neither greatest fixed points nor fixed point alternation. In particular, natural properties expressible in the  $\nu$ -fragment of  $\mu$ GML such as non-reachability are not expressible in GMSC; this is proved in [21] for the non-graded version, and the same proof applies to GMSC.

# 3 Connecting GNNs and logics via automata

In this section we establish exact matches between classes of GNNs and our logics. The first main theorem is Theorem 3.2, showing that  $\mathrm{GNN[F]s}$ , R-simple aggregate-combine  $\mathrm{GNN[F]s}$  and GMSC are equally expressive. Theorem 3.4 is the second main result, showing that  $\mathrm{GNN}[\mathbb{R}]s$  and  $\omega$ -GML are equally expressive. We begin by defining the concept of distributed automata which we will mainly use as a tool for our arguments but also leading to nice additional characterizations.

Counting message passing automata are defined and operate similarly to GNNs. A counting message passing automaton (CMPA) over the set  $\Pi$  of proposition symbols is a

tuple  $(Q, \pi, \delta, F)$  where  $Q$  is an at most countable set and  $\pi, \delta$  and  $F \subseteq Q$  are defined in a similar way as for GNNs:  $\pi \colon \mathcal{P}(\Pi) \to Q$  is an initialization function,  $\delta \colon Q \times \mathcal{M}(Q) \to Q$  a transition function and  $F \subseteq Q$  a set of accepting states. Computation of a CMPA over  $\Pi$  is defined in a  $\Pi$ -model  $M$  analogously to GNNs: for each node  $w$  in  $M$ , the initialization function gives the initial state for  $w$  based on the propositions true in  $w$ , and the transition function is applied to the previous state of  $w$  and the multiset of states of immediate out-neighbours of  $w$ . Acceptance is similar to GNNs: the CMPA accepts  $(M, v)$  if the CMPA visits (at least once) an accepting state at  $v$  in  $M$ . A bounded CMPA is a CMPA whose transition function can be written as  $\delta \colon Q \times \mathcal{M}_k(Q) \to Q$ , (i.e.,  $\delta(q, M) = \delta(q, M_{|k})$  for each multiset  $M \in \mathcal{M}(Q)$  and state  $q \in Q$ ) for some  $k \in \mathbb{N}$ . A finite CMPA (or FCMPA) is a CMPA with finite  $Q$ . We define bounded FCMAs similarly to bounded CMPAs. A CMPA  $\mathcal{A}$  over  $\Pi$  defines a node property  $\mathcal{P}$  if  $\mathcal{P}$  is a node property over  $\Pi$  and  $\mathcal{A}$  accepts  $(M, w)$  iff  $(M, w) \in \mathcal{P}$ . We define whether a node property  $\mathcal{P}$  is definable by a CMPA in a way analogous to GNNs.

Let  $\mathcal{C}$  be the class of all GNNs, CMPAs,  $\omega$ -GML-formulae and GMSC-programs. Two objects in  $\mathcal{C}$  are equivalent if they define the same node property. Subsets  $A, B \subseteq \mathcal{C}$  have the same expressive power, if each  $x \in A$  has an equivalent  $y \in B$  and vice versa. It is easy to obtain the following.

Proposition 3.1. Bounded FCMPAs have the same expressive power as GMSC.

Proof. (Sketch) Details in Appendix B.2. To obtain a bounded FCMPA equivalent to a GMSC-program  $\Lambda$ , we first turn  $\Lambda$  into an equivalent program  $\Gamma$  where the modal depth of terminal clauses (resp., iteration clauses) is zero (resp., one). Then from  $\Gamma$  with head predicate set  $\mathcal{T}'$ , we construct an equivalent FCMPA  $\mathcal{A}$  as follows.  $\mathcal{A}$  uses  $\mathcal{P}(\Pi \cup \mathcal{T}')$  as the set of states and  $\mathcal{A}$  enters in round  $n \in \mathbb{N}$  in node  $w$  into a state that contains precisely the propositions true in  $w$  and the predicates  $X$  whose iteration formula  $X^n$  is true at  $w$ . For the converse, we create a head predicate for each state in  $\mathcal{A}$ , and let predicates for accepting states be appointed. The terminal clauses simulate  $\pi$  using disjunctions of conjunctions of non-negated and negated propositions. The iteration clauses simulate  $\delta$  using, for each pair  $(q, q')$ , a subschema specifying the multiset's that take  $q$  to  $q'$ .

We are ready to show equiexpressivity of GMSC and GNN[F]s. This applies without relativising to any background logic. The direction from GNN[F]s to GMSC is trivial, but the other direction is more challenging, in particular when going all the way to recurrent R-simple GNN[F]s. In Appendix D, we show that this theorem also applies for other accepting conditions.

Theorem 3.2. The following have the same expressive power: GNN[F]s, GMSC, and  $R$ -simple aggregate-combine GNN[F]s.

Proof. (Sketch) Details in Appendix B.3. By definition, a  $\mathrm{GNN}[\mathrm{F}]$  is just a bounded FCMPA and translates to a GMSC-program by Proposition 3.1. To construct an R-simple aggregate-combine  $\mathrm{GNN}[\mathrm{F}]\mathcal{G}_{\Lambda}$  for a GMSC-program  $\Lambda$ , we first turn  $\Lambda$  into an equivalent program  $\Gamma$ , where each terminal clause has the body  $\perp$  and the formula depth is  $L$  for each body of an iteration clause. We choose a floating-point system that can express all integers up to the largest number  $k$  appearing in a diamond  $\diamond_{\geq k}$  in  $\Gamma$ . We define binary feature vectors that are split into two halves: the 1st half intuitively calculates the truth values of the head predicates and subschemata of  $\Gamma$  one formula depth at a time in the style of Barceló et al. [5]. The 2nd half records the current formula depth under evaluation.  $\mathcal{G}_{\Lambda}$

simulates one round of  $\Lambda$  in  $L + 1$  rounds, using the 2nd half of the features to accept nodes only every  $L + 1$  rounds: the truth values of head predicates are correct in those rounds.

To show that  $\mathrm{GNN}[\mathbb{R}]\mathrm{s}$  and  $\omega$ -GML are equally expressive, we first prove a useful theorem.

# Theorem 3.3. CMPAs have the same expressive power as  $\omega$ -GML.

Proof. (Sketch) Details in Appendix B.4. To construct a CMPA for each  $\omega$ -GML-formula  $\chi$ , we define a GML-formula called the "graded type of modal depth  $n$  " for each pointed model  $(M, w)$ , which expresses all the local information of the neighbourhood of  $w$  up to depth  $n$  (with the maximum out-degree of  $M$  plus 1 sufficing for width). We show that each  $\omega$ -GML-formula  $\chi$  is equivalent to an infinite disjunction  $\psi_{\chi}$  of types. We then define a CMPA that computes the type of modal depth  $n$  of each node in round  $n$ . Its accepting states are the types in the type-disjunction  $\psi_{\chi}$ . For the converse, to show that each CMPA has an equivalent  $\omega$ -GML-formula, we first show that two pointed models satisfying the same type of modal depth  $n$  have identical states in each round  $\ell \leq n$  in each CMPA. The  $\omega$ -GML-formula is the disjunction containing every type  $T$  such that some  $(M, w)$  satisfying  $T$  is accepted by the automaton in round  $n$ , where  $n$  is the depth of  $T$ .

Next we characterize GNN[  $\mathbb{R}$  ]s with  $\omega$ -GML without relativising to a background logic. As the theorems above and below imply that GNN[  $\mathbb{R}$  ]s and CMPAs are equally expressive, it follows that GNN[  $\mathbb{R}$  ]s can be discretized to CMPAs having—by definition—an only countable set of states.

# Theorem 3.4. GNN  $[\mathbb{R}]s$  have the same expressive power as  $\omega$ -GML.

Proof. (Sketch) Details in Appendix B.5. For any  $\mathrm{GNN}[\mathbb{R}]$ , we build an equivalent  $\omega$ -GML-formula using the same method as in the proof of Theorem 3.3, where we show that for each CMPA, we can find an equivalent  $\omega$ -GML-formula. For the converse, we first translate an  $\omega$ -GML-formula to a CMPA  $\mathcal{A}$  by Theorem 3.3 and then translate  $\mathcal{A}$  to a CMPA  $\mathcal{A}'$  with maximal ability to distinguish nodes. Then we build an equivalent  $\mathrm{GNN}[\mathbb{R}]$  for  $\mathcal{A}'$  by encoding states into integers.

Remark 3.5. It is easy to show that unrestricted GNN[F]s have the same expressive power as FCMPAs. Moreover, the proof of Theorem 3.4 is easily modified to show that bounded GNN[R]s have the same expressive power as width-bounded  $\omega$ -GML. See Appendix B.6 for the proofs.

In Appendix B.7 we show that our model of constant-iteration GNN  $[\mathbb{R}]$ s is equivalent to the one in Barceló et al. [5]. Thus the below generalizes the result in [5] for constant-iteration GNN  $[\mathbb{R}]$ s.

Theorem 3.6. Constant-iteration GNN  $[\mathbb{R}]s$  have the same expressivity as depth-bounded  $\omega$ -GML.

# 4 Characterizing GNNs over MSO-definable properties

In this section we consider (node) properties that are definable in MSO. The first main result is Theorem 4.1, where we show that for properties definable in MSO, the expressive

power of  $\mathrm{GNN}[\mathbb{R}]\mathrm{s}$  is captured by a finitary logic. In fact, this logic is GMSC and by Theorem 3.2, it follows that relative to MSO,  $\mathrm{GNN}[\mathbb{R}]\mathrm{s}$  have the same expressive power as  $\mathrm{GNN}[\mathrm{F}]\mathrm{s}$  (Theorem 4.3 below).

Theorem 4.1. Let  $P$  be a property definable in MSO. Then  $P$  is expressible as a GNN[  $\mathbb{R}$ ] if and only if it is definable in GMSC.

Theorem 4.1 is proved later in this section. The recent work [23] relates to Theorem 4.1; see the introduction for a discussion. The proof of Theorem 4.1 can easily be adapted to show that a property  $P$  definable in MSO is expressible as a constant-iteration GNN[  $\mathbb{R}$ ] iff it is definable in GML. This relates to [5] which shows the same for FO in place of MSO. The MSO-version in the this particular case is not an actual generalization, as based on [9], we show the following; see Appendix C.1.

Lemma 4.2. Any property definable by MSO and constant-iteration GNN[  $\mathbb{R}$ ] is also FO-definable.

Uniting Theorems 4.1 and 3.2, we see that (recurrent) GNN  $[\mathbb{R}]\mathrm{s}$  and GNN[F]s coincide relative to MSO. It is easy to get a similar result for constant-iteration GNNs (the details are in Appendix C.2).

Theorem 4.3. Let  $P$  be a property definable in MSO. Then  $P$  is expressible as a GNN[  $\mathbb{R}$ ] if and only if it is expressible as a GNN[F]. The same is true for constant-iteration GNNs.

To put Theorem 4.3 into perspective, we note that by Example 2.5, the centre-point property is expressible in GMSC, but not in MSO. From Theorem 3.2, we thus obtain the following.

Proposition 4.4. There is a property  $P$  that is expressible as a GNN[F] but not in MSO.

Theorem 3.6 shows that constant-iteration GNN[  $\mathbb{R}$  ]s and depth-bounded  $\omega$ -GML-formulae are equally expressive. The proof of Proposition 2.4 shows that already depth-bounded  $\omega$ -GML can define properties that are not definable in GMSC. Thus, by Theorem 3.2 we obtain the following.

Proposition 4.5. There is a property definable in constant-iteration GNN  $[\mathbb{R}]$  but not as a GNN[F].

We next discuss the proof of Theorem 4.1. We build upon results due to Janin and Walukiewicz [29, 18], reusing an automaton model from [29] that captures the expressivity of MSO on tree-shaped Kripke models. With a tree-shaped Kripke model we mean a potentially infinite model that, when viewed as a directed graph, is a directed tree. The out-degree of nodes is unrestricted, different nodes may have different degree, and both leaves and infinite paths are admitted in the same tree.

We next introduce the mentioned automaton model. Although we are only going to use it on tree-shaped Kripke models, in its full generality it is actually defined on unrestricted such models. We nevertheless call them tree automata as they belong to the tradition of more classical forms of such automata. In particular, a run of an automaton is tree-shaped, even if the input model is not. Formally, a parity tree automaton (PTA) is a tuple  $\mathcal{A} = (Q,\Sigma_N,q_0,\Delta ,\Omega)$ , where  $Q$  is a finite set of states,  $\Sigma_N\subseteq \mathrm{PROP}$  is a finite node

alphabet,  $q_0 \in Q$  is an initial state,  $\Delta \subseteq Q \times \Sigma_N \times \mathcal{F}$  is a transition relation with  $\mathcal{F}$  being the set of all transition formulas for  $\mathcal{A}$  defined below, and  $\Omega : Q \to \mathbb{N}$  is a priority function. A transition formula for  $\mathcal{A}$  is a disjunction of FO-formulas of the form

$$
\exists x _ {1} \dots \exists x _ {k} (\operatorname {d i f f} (x _ {1}, \dots , x _ {k}) \wedge q _ {1} (x _ {1}) \wedge \dots \wedge q _ {k} (x _ {k}) \wedge \forall z (\operatorname {d i f f} (z, x _ {1}, \dots , x _ {k}) \rightarrow \psi))
$$

where  $k \geq 0$ ,  $\text{diff}(y_1, \ldots, y_n)$  shortens an FO-formula declaring  $y_1, \ldots, y_n$  pairwise distinct,  $q_i \in Q$  are states used as unary predicates and  $\psi$  is a disjunction of conjunctions of atoms  $q(z)$ , with  $q \in Q$ .

A PTA  $\mathcal{A}$  accepts a language  $L(\mathcal{A})$  consisting of (possibly infinite) Kripke models  $M$ . We have  $M \in L(\mathcal{A})$  if there is an accepting run of  $\mathcal{A}$  on  $M$ , and runs are defined in the spirit of alternating automata. While details are in Appendix C.3, we mention that transition formulas govern transitions in the run: a transition of a PTA currently visiting node  $v$  in state  $q$  consists of sending copies of itself to successors of  $v$ , potentially in states other than  $q$ . It is not required that a copy is sent to every successor, and multiple copies (in different states) can be sent to the same successor. However, we must find some  $(q, V(v), \vartheta) \in \Delta$  such that the transition satisfies  $\vartheta$  in the sense that  $\vartheta$  is satisfied in the model with one element for each successor of  $v$  and unary predicates (states of  $\mathcal{A}$ ) are interpreted according to the transition. This specific form of PTAs is interesting to us due to the following.

Theorem 4.6. Let  $\varphi(x)$  be an MSO-formula equivalent to an  $\omega$ -GML formula. Then there is a PTA  $\mathcal{A}$  such that for any Kripke model  $M$ :  $M \models \varphi(w)$  iff the unraveling of  $M$  at  $w$  is in  $L(\mathcal{A})$ .

Proof. Theorem 9 in [18] states that for every MSO sentence  $\psi$ , there is a PTA  $\mathcal{A}$  such that for every tree-shaped Kripke model  $M$ :  $M \in L(\mathcal{A})$  iff  $M \models \psi$ . To obtain a PTA for  $\varphi(x)$ , we start from the MSO sentence  $\psi \coloneqq \exists x (\varphi(x) \land \neg \exists y R(y, x))$  and build the corresponding PTA  $\mathcal{A}$ . Then  $\mathcal{A}$  is as desired. In fact,  $M \models \varphi(w)$  iff  $U \models \varphi(w)$  with  $U$  the unraveling of  $M$  at  $w$  since  $\varphi(x)$  is equivalent to an  $\omega$ -GML-formula which is invariant under unraveling (defined in Appendix C.1). Moreover,  $U \models \varphi(w)$  iff  $U \models \psi$  iff  $U \in L(\mathcal{A})$ .

In view of Theorem 4.6 and since GMSC-programs are invariant under unraveling, we may prove Theorem 4.1 by showing that for every PTA  $\mathcal{A} = (Q,\Sigma_N,q_0,\Delta ,\Omega)$  that is equivalent to an  $\omega$  -GML-formula, we can find a GMSC-program  $\Lambda$  such that for every tree-shaped Kripke model  $M$  with root  $w$ , we have  $M\in L(\mathcal{A})$  iff  $M,w\models \Lambda$ . We then say that  $\mathcal{A}$  and  $\Lambda$  are tree equivalent.

For a state  $q \in Q$ , let  $\mathcal{A}_q$  be the PTA defined like  $\mathcal{A}$  but with  $q$  as its starting state. For a tree-shaped Kripke model  $T$ , set  $Q_T = \{ q \in Q \mid T \in L(\mathcal{A}_q) \}$ . We say  $\mathcal{Q} \subseteq \mathcal{P}(Q)$  is the universal set for  $P \subseteq \Sigma_N$  if  $\mathcal{Q}$  consists precisely of the sets  $Q_T$ , for all tree-shaped Kripke models  $T$ , whose root is labeled exactly with the proposition letters in  $P$ . Let  $T = (W, R, V)$  be a tree-shaped Kripke model with root  $w$  and  $k \in \mathbb{N}$ , and let  $W_k$  be the restriction of  $W$  to the nodes on level at most  $k$  (the root being on level 0). A  $k$ -prefix decoration of  $T$  is a mapping  $\mu : W_k \to \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(Q))$  such that the following conditions hold:

1. for each  $S \in \mu(w)$ :  $q_0 \in S$ ;  
2. for all  $v \in W$  on the level  $k$ ,  $\mu(v)$  is the universal set for  $V(v)$ ;  
3. for each  $v \in W$  on some level smaller than  $k$  that has successors  $u_1, \ldots, u_n$  and all  $S_1 \in \mu(u_1), \ldots, S_n \in \mu(u_n)$ ,  $\mu(v)$  contains the set  $S$  that contains a state  $q \in Q$  iff there is a triple  $(q, V(v), \vartheta) \in \Delta$  with  $\vartheta$  satisfied in the following model:

- the universe is  $\{u_1, \ldots, u_n\}$  and  
- each unary predicate  $q' \in Q$  is interpreted as  $\{u_i \mid q' \in S_i\}$ .

A  $k$ -prefix decoration of  $T$  represents a set of accepting runs of  $\mathcal{A}$  on the prefix  $T_{k}$  of  $T$ . As these runs start in universal sets, for each extension of  $T_{k}$  obtained by attaching trees to nodes on level  $k$ , we can find a run among the represented ones that can be extended to an accepting run of  $\mathcal{A}$  on that extension. In fact  $T$  is such an extension. The following crucial lemma is proved in Appendix C.3.

Lemma 4.7. For every tree-shaped Kripke model  $T$ :  $T \in L(\mathcal{A})$  if and only if there is a  $k$ -prefix decoration of  $T$ , for some  $k \in \mathbb{N}$ .

Using the above, we sketch the proof of Theorem 4.1; the full proof is in Appendix C.3. It follows from Lemma 4.7 that given a PTA  $\mathcal{A}$ , we obtain a tree equivalent GMSC-program  $\Lambda$  by constructing  $\Lambda$  so that it is satisfied at the root of a tree-shaped Kripke model  $M$  iff  $M$  has a  $k$ -prefix decoration for some  $k$ . This requires care, but is indeed possible; the details are in Appendix C.3. A crucial part of the construction is expressing transition formulas of PTAs as GML-formulas.

# 5 Conclusion

We have characterized the expressivity of recurrent GNNs with floats and reals in terms of modal logic, both in general and relative to MSO. This also yields the result that, within MSO, GNNs with floats and with reals have the same expressiveness. Interesting future directions include extending our GNN model, e.g., with global readouts. This should be easy and in fact global readouts should correspond to the (counting) global modality, see e.g. [7]. It is also interesting to consider other termination conditions. In fact, a variant of our proofs gives a counterpart of Theorem 3.2 for recurrent GNNs where termination is based on fixed points, studied under the name "RecGNN" in [23] and closely related to the termination condition in [26, 10]. This requires also modifying the acceptance condition of GMSC to be based on fixed points. The details are in Appendix D. The lack of global readouts, as seen above, is a central limitation of our work. Another limitation is that further acceptance conditions should be investigated in more depth. An alternative way to add fixed points without changing the acceptance conditions would be via global readouts in both GMSC and GNNs. These should be examined in more detail; we leave this for future work.
