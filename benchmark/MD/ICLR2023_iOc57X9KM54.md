# ABSTRACT

Procedural planning aims to implement complex high-level goals by decomposition into sequential simpler low-level steps. Although procedural planning is a basic skill set for humans in daily life, it remains a challenge for large language models (LLMs) that lack a deep understanding of the cause-effect relations in procedures. Previous methods require manual exemplars to acquire procedural planning knowledge from LLMs in the zero-shot setting. However, such elicited pre-trained knowledge in LLMs induces spurious correlations between goals and steps, which impair the model generalization to unseen tasks. In contrast, this paper proposes a neuro-symbolic procedural PLANner (PLAN) that elicits procedural planning knowledge from the LLMs with commonsense-infused prompting. To mitigate spurious goal-step correlations, we use symbolic program executors on the latent procedural representations to formalize prompts from commonsense knowledge bases as a causal intervention toward the Structural Causal Model. Both automatic and human evaluations on WikiHow and RobotHow show the superiority of PLAN on procedural planning without further training or manual exemplars.

# 1 INTRODUCTION

How to make a cup of coffee? As humans, we can easily identify the procedure with the commonsense reasoning ability we acquire. However, can we endow machines with the same ability to sketch out a sequential plan list? Procedural planning (Pearson, 1996; Huang et al.; Lyu et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2020a,b) is a task that aims to decompose a specific task into a sequence of temporally extended steps (Figure 1). Such ability is a necessity for a wide range of downstream applications, such as household robots (Ahn et al., 2022; Blukis et al., 2021; Min et al., 2022; Padmakumar et al., 2022; Shridhar et al., 2020) and conversational assistants (Budzianowski et al., 2018; Ding et al., 2022; Eric et al., 2017; Ilievski et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2022).

Existing formalization of the procedural planning task can mainly be categorized as 1) sequential choice making (Lyu et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2020a;b), which reasons about the next step from the options given, the task, and previous steps; 2) conditioned generation (Huang et al.; Ahn et al., 2022), which generates the plans composing of temporal-extended steps to implement the task. We study the procedural planning task as the conditioned generation problem since it do not require a skillfully annotated dataset and is close to real-world scenarios. Previous approaches require manual exemplars (Wei et al., 2022; Huang et al.) to acquire such procedural knowledge in the zero-shot setting. However, it is impossible to cover the ever-changing task setups and the flexible dependency relations between internal steps. In fact, the biased data may cause the model to learn spurious correlations and hinder the model from generalizing to counterfactual tasks. Thus we take a step back and rethink how humans conduct procedural planning. Studies in cognitive science state that humans rely on past experience (Duncker & Lees, 1945; Knoblich et al., 1999; Smith, 1995; Zhang et al., 2019) and chunking mechanisms (Gobet et al., 2001; Miller, 1956) to solve novel and complex problems.

Such generalizable planning ability in complex tasks, if not using exemplars, can be achieved by learning the causal-effect relations among the goals and steps in the procedure. Thus we first devise a Structural Causal Model (SCM) (Peters et al., 2017) (Figure 2) for procedural planning and show that pre-trained knowledge (D) in LLMs confounds language models to make biased decisions toward an unreasonable action. Since the confounder is unobservable, intervention techniques such as backdoor

Through the mediator, we can identify causal effects among goals and steps by investigating the indirect effect from the goals, which is essentially the frontdoor adjustment in causality (Pearl, 2009).

In addition, reasoning the logical order of the plan is a critical ability in procedural planning. For example, switching on TV happens before sitting on the sofa and watching TV, but switching on TV is interchangeable with grabbing the remote control. However, directly incorporating the graph structure external commonsense knowledge into language models cannot maximally elicit commonsense reasoning ability for order. Thus we apply a symbolic program (Mao et al., 2019; Yi et al., 2018) to construct such commonsense-infused prompt from external resource that attends to the high-level goals and maintains the procedural structure. More specifically, we parse the goal into an entity to retrieve the subgraph that represents the common knowledge related to the entity, including the prerequisite event, subsequent event, location, usage, etc. Finally, we semantically translate the knowledge to the admissible knowledge and aggregate with the task as commonsense-infused prompts to elicit procedural planning ability from LLMs.

We conduct experiments on two datasets that involve completing daily household goals, RobotHow (Puig et al., 2018) and WikiHow (Koupae & Wang, 2018). Furthermore, we devise 3 families of automatic intervention methods to construct a counterfactual dataset based on the original dataset. PLAN surpasses powerful baselines (Chain of Thoughts (Wei et al., 2022) and Zero-shot Planner (Huang et al.)) by large margins on both the original and the counterfactual samples. Our major contributions in this work can be summarized as:

- We propose a neuro-symbolic model using commonsense-infused prompting to tackle the procedural planning task without annotated exemplars or further training.  
- We develop the first causal framework for procedural planning by 1) defining a temporally extended Structural Causal Model and 2) resolving spurious correlation between high-level goals and low-level steps via frontdoor adjustment with a prompt-based mediator.  
- Extensive evaluation results show the superiority of PLAN in reasoning the cause-effect relations among goals and steps and achieving promising procedural planning ability.

# 2.2 A CAUSAL LOOK AT PROCEDURE PLANNING WITH LLMS

We seek to empower the LLMs with the ability to reason cause-effect relations in procedural planning. Thus, we devise a causal framework by first defining a Structural Causal Model (SCM) of procedural planning in Figure 2. The SCM describes the temporal dynamics and procedural cause-effect relationship. Our causal assumption in SCM indicates that there is a backdoor path from task to step, which must be blocked with frontdoor adjustment. Therefore, we model the input prompt as a mediator which is created from external knowledge. More specifically, we define our Full Temporal Causal Graph as in Figure 2a, which is an unrolled Structural Causal Model (SCM) for sequential decision making. Our goal is to identify the causal effect between attended task  $T$  and plan procedures  $\mathbf{S} = \{S_1, S_2, \ldots\}$  from LLMs. Initially, there are direct paths  $T \rightarrow S_i$  and  $S_k \rightarrow S_i$ ,  $k < i$  because  $S_i$  relies on the LLM attended task entities and previous accomplished steps.  $D$  is an unobserved confounder from learned knowledge during pre-training.  $D$  builds a backdoor path between  $T$  and  $S_i$  and misguides the LLM to attend on false entities to generate the next step (see Fig. 2b). Note that  $D$  is unobservable as we directly adopt the LLM without knowing the pre-training data. To mitigate the spurious correlation, we then introduce a mediator  $P_i$  for each  $S_i$  as shown in Figure 2a to achieve our frontdoor adjustment by injecting external knowledge into LLMs in a neuro-symbolic approach. We describe the implementation of such frontdoor adjustment in Section 3.1.

# 3 OUR APPROACH

Though LLMs have strong general language intelligence, they still perform poorly in reasoning the cause-effect relations in procedural plans due to a lack of daily life experience. We propose to elicit the unbiased procedural planning knowledge from the LLMs using the created commonsense-infused Prompt  $P$  as  $\mathbb{P}(S_t|T,S_{0:t - 1},P)$ . Figure 3 and Algorithm 1 depict how PLAN tackles the procedural planning in a five-stage manner. We illustrate the commonsense-infused prompt construction (the first three stages) in Section 3.1 and planning with LLMs (the last stage) in Section 3.2.

Stage1: Task-Relevant Knowledge Subgraph Sampling First, we investigate the causal effect  $T \rightarrow P_i$  and  $S_{i-1} \rightarrow P_i$  (Figure 2). Note that in the path  $T \rightarrow D \rightarrow S_i \leftarrow P_i$ ,  $S_i$  is a collider that blocks the association between  $D$  and  $P_i$ . Since there is no backdoor path for  $T \rightarrow P_i$  and similarly for  $S_{i-1} \rightarrow P_i$ , we simply have the conditional probability after applying do-operators:

$$
\mathbb {P} \left(P _ {i} = p \mid d o (T)\right) = \mathbb {P} \left(P _ {i} = p \mid T\right), \quad \mathbb {P} \left(P _ {i} = p \mid d o \left(S _ {i - 1}\right)\right) = \mathbb {P} \left(P _ {i} = p \mid S _ {i - 1}\right) \tag {1}
$$

We achieve the do-operation in a prompting way by modifying the input so that the model can attend on the correct entities. We extract the entities from the task name, and retrieve a three-hop graph from an external knowledge base. The graph can be verbalized and applied as a prompt so that the LLM attends on the correct entities to plan  $S_{1}$ . As for the implementation, the high-level task name  $T$  is Semantically Parsing into the Entity Set  $T_{E}$  with the named entity information to extract the representative nodes from the graph. We use NLTK tockenize and pos_tag the task text. Then we use the noun (e.g. television), noun phrases (e.g. remote control), and verb phrases (e.g. watch television) as the entity node. Each entity  $e\in T_E$  is used as a query for sampling the local subgraph  $G_{e}\subseteq \mathcal{N}_{e}\times \mathcal{R}_{s}\times \mathcal{N}_{e}$  from the external knowledge base  $G\subseteq \mathcal{N}\times \mathcal{R}\times \mathcal{N}$  (e.g., ConceptNet (Speer et al., 2017)), where  $\mathcal{N}$  and  $\mathcal{R}$  represent the number of concept nodes and commonsense relations respectively. When extracting the Task-relevant Subgraph  $G_{s}$  from the External Knowledge Base  $G$  we keep the triplets with relation type in household domain (e.g., AtLocation, UsedFor) and filter out ones in the linguistic domain (e.g., DistinctFrom, DerivedFrom) for the procedural planning task.  $\mathcal{N}_e$  is maintained in a set of top-  $k$  task-relevant nodes using the weight of each  $R_{e}$  which is adapted using the edge-wise invariance module illustrated in the next paragraph.  $G_{s}$  is a composite of the  $\{G_e,e\in E\}$ , representing the task-relevant external knowledge.

Stage2: Edge-Wise Adaption and Symbolic Structuring Second, we need to find the causal effect for  $P_{i} \rightarrow S_{i}$ . Since the path  $P_{i} \gets T \gets D \rightarrow S_{i}$  contains a backdoor from  $P_{i}$  to  $S_{i}$ , we cannot rely on the conditional probability. Instead, we add intervention on  $P_{i}$  by applying the do-operator to cut off  $D \rightarrow T$ :

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathbb {P} (S _ {i} | d o (P _ {i} = p)) = \sum_ {t, s} \mathbb {P} (S _ {i} | p, T = t, S _ {i - 1} = s) \mathbb {P} (T = t, S _ {i - 1} = s) \\ = \sum_ {t, s} ^ {t, s} \mathbb {P} \left(S _ {i} | p, T = t, S _ {i - 1} = s\right) \mathbb {P} \left(S _ {i - 1} = s \mid T = t\right) \mathbb {P} (T = t) \tag {2} \\ \end{array}
$$

The retrieved entity-centered graph has multiple edges representing various relationships with other actions/entities. Therefore, the summation over intervened  $T$  can be achieved by incorporating these edges into the prompt. For instance, "living room" can be "walked to" and "used for reading" while "book" can locate in "living room" and "bedroom". Similarly, we extrapolate over the edges for  $i - 1$  hops to aggregate the intervened  $S_{i}$ , i.e.  $P(S_{i - 1} = s|T = t)$ . To mitigate this, we propose to adapt the weight of each triplet (Edge-wise Adaption). The adaptive weight is the addition of the original edge weight and the cosine similarity between the tail node embedding  $\pmb{n}_{tail}$  and the task embedding  $\pmb{v}_{task}$ . The embeddings are projected from the node text and task name using the sentence-transformer (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019). To better track the utilized external knowledge during inference, we construct the task-dependent commonsense prompt with a symbolic program executor (Symbolic Structuring) guided by the relation type of each triplet in  $G_{s}$  with the adapted edge weight beyond threshold  $\theta_{e}$ . Specifically, the executor acquires the neural information of each natural language node and executes the sequential mapping program by sampling the operation  $Op$  from the Symbolic Rule Set  $R$  according to the edge relation type. For instance, the AtLocation edge samples the operation Op_AtLocation from  $R$ , which takes the commonsense relation of the triplet from  $G_{s}$  as the parameters to query the procedural concept output given the natural language meaning of the linked nodes (e.g., go to the location of Start_Node_Of  $(r_e)$  in this case). Similarly, Op_UsedFor may refer to "go to find End_Node_Of  $(r_e)$  and use it for Start_Node_Of  $(r_e)$ ". And operators Op_HasSubevent and Op_HasPrerequisite will recursively navigate the subgraph  $G_{e}$ . After navigating the subgraph, we linearize the transformed triplets as the Procedural Prompt  $P_G$ , which is then translated to Admissible Knowledge Prompt  $\hat{P}_G$  by the Translation Language Model  $LM_T$ .

Stage3: Temporally-Extended Aggregation To acquire temporal order in procedure, we obtain the Prompt  $P$  at timestep  $t$  with the aggregation of task  $T$ , history steps  $S_{0:t-1}$  and current external knowledge  $\hat{P}_G$ . The underlying causal mechanism is a combination of Eq. 1 and Eq. 2:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathbb {P} (S _ {i} | d o (T), d o (S _ {i - 1})) = \sum_ {p} \mathbb {P} (S _ {i} | d o (P _ {i} = p)) \mathbb {P} (p | d o (T), d o (S _ {i})) \\ = \sum_ {p} ^ {p} \mathbb {P} (p | T) \sum_ {t, s} \mathbb {P} \left(S _ {i} | p, T = t, S _ {i - 1} = s\right) \mathbb {P} \left(T = t, S _ {i - 1} = s\right) \tag {3} \\ \end{array}
$$

The adjustment and marginalization in Eq. 3 is achieved in the input space by forming the Procedural Prompt  $P_{G}$  that allows the LLM to attend on the causal entities instead of the highly correlated ones for the next step generation. The LLM can reason over the most relevant edges to link the concepts with the task entities as a context. The prompts from knowledge bases are independent of the pre-training data distribution so that  $P_{i}$  is independent of  $D$  and satisfies the frontdoor criterion. # 3.2 PROCEDURAL PLANNING WITH LLMS

Stage4: Semantic Generation The external knowledge is further concatenated with the high-level goal (task name) as the initial prompt. The initial prompt contains limited semantics and executability, but still contains external knowledge and structure that help leverage the reasoning ability of the large language models. Given the prompt, the language model Generation  $LM_{G} \in \{P_{AR}, P_{AE}\}$  (e.g.,

GPT3, BART) generates the next sentence, and the most confident prediction is then appended to previous prompts. The Termination Condition is either reaching the max step  $t$  or the matching score is below threshold  $\theta$ . The joint probabilities of auto-regressive  $(P_{AR})$  and auto-encoder  $(P_{AE})$  model is factorized as:

$$
\mathbb {P} _ {A R} (x) = \prod_ {i = 1} ^ {n} p \left(s _ {n} \mid \hat {P} _ {G}, s _ {1: n - 1}, T\right), \quad \mathbb {P} _ {A E} (x) = \prod_ {i = 1} ^ {n} p \left(s _ {n} \mid \hat {P} _ {G}, \left\{s _ {1: n - 1}, [ M A S K ] \right\}, T\right) \tag {4}
$$

where  $\hat{P_G}$  represent the commonsense knowledge and  $T$  represent the task name.

Stage5: Admissible Step Translation To ensure that the generated procedural plans could ground to the specific task domain (e.g. RobotHow, WikiHow), we should avoid producing the steps that are inadmissible (e.g. Toast the table). In other words, the generated steps should be fully constrained to the admissible composite of action and object in a certain task domain. Thus previous works ((Huang et al.; Ahn et al., 2022)) have explored using the model (which is  $LM_{T}$  in our case) to score a step selected from a fixed set of available options, instead of directly sampling from the output distributions of the language model (which is  $LM_{G}$  in our case). Specifically, we match the generated step by  $LM_{G}$  to the most similar admissible step in the embedding space encoded by the Translation Language Model  $LM_{T}$ . Following (Huang et al.), we utilize a Sentence-Transformer (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019) to calculate the cosine similarity:

$$
\mathbb {P} \left(s _ {i} | x\right) = L M _ {T} \left(L M _ {G} (x)\right), \text {w h e r e} s _ {i} = \{k \mid k \in [ a, o ], a \in A, o \in O \} \tag {5}
$$

which translates  $LM_{G}(x)$  into the admissible step  $s_i \in S$  that is the closest in the embedding space measured by the cosine similarity.  $A$  and  $O$  represent the action set and object set respectively.

# 4.1 PROCEDURAL PLANNING SETUP

Datasets We conduct zero-shot experiments on two datasets with procedural information, WikiHow<sup>1</sup> (collected following (Koupae & Wang, 2018)) and RobotHow (Puig et al., 2018) without training. WikiHow is a large-scale text summarization dataset that is constructed from a human-written knowledge base, involving procedural tasks that spans various topics. We utilize "how to" title as the task names and the summarized headlines as the steps. RobotHow is a large knowledge base of common household tasks collected in the VirtualHome (Puig et al., 2018) simulator. The dataset contains the programs with high-level task names and low-level steps.  $D_{T}$  is composed of 292 and 2000 distinct tasks from RobotHow and WikiHow respectively. In RobotHow,  $A$  is of size 42 and  $O$  is of size 195. In WikiHow,  $A$  is of size 1161 and  $O$  is of size 13873. Human evaluations use randomly sampled 50 task examples for each dataset. We assume the performance gain of GPT is due to a smaller gap between the objective of their pre-training and the procedural planning task.

# 4.5 CORRELATION BETWEEN AUTOMATIC AND HUMAN EVALUATION

We evaluate segment-level Pearson Correlation between human and automatic metrics. We observe that BERTScore has a moderate correlation to the human coverage score and WMD has a moderate correlation to the human order score, with  $23.3\%$  and  $32.3\%$  respectively. Similar to the prior findings (Xu et al., 2021), n-gram-based metrics (Sentence-BLEU and ROUGE) have a relatively weaker correlation to the human coverage score, with a Pearson correlation of  $16.4\%$  and  $21.1\%$ . Overall, our automatic and human evaluation scores are consistent with the main claim of this paper. However, human evaluation is still irreplaceable for procedural planning at the current stage.

# 5 RELATED WORK

Procedural Planning Learning to generate procedural plan (Zhang et al., 2020b; Chang et al., 2020; Huang et al.) is important for embodied agentTellex et al. (2011); Jansen (2020); Ahn et al. (2022) and conversational assistants (Ilievski et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2022). Previous work views procedural script learning as a structured form of commonsense knowledge Gupta et al. (2004); Regneri et al. (2010); Wanzare et al. (2016), while more recent work strengthens its association with the changing environments for executable action planning Puig et al. (2018); Shridhar et al. (2020). Our method tackles the problem by utilizing commonsense-infused prompts via a neuro-symbolic approach (Mao et al., 2019; Nye et al., 2021; Yi et al., 2018) for zero-shot procedural planning.

Causality for Language Generation The integration of causality and machine learning has been an intriguing topic for many problems Pearl (2009); Schölkopf (2022). Previous studies focusing on causal inference for natural language understanding Chen et al. (2020); Keith et al. (2020); Wood-Doughty et al. (2018) and generating counterfactual text representations Feder et al. (2021). Weber et al. (2020) proposes an intervention method for script learning. However, their method cannot be directly applied to procedural generation. The generated plan in our case has a formal structure and our method is based on mediation analysis VanderWeele (2015) and causal intervention Pearl (2009); Peters et al. (2017).

Prompt for Large Language Model There is an emerging interest in using prompts to extract knowledge from large language models Chen et al. (2022); Le Scao & Rush (2021); Su et al. (2022); Ye et al. (2022); Zhou et al. (2022). Cao et al. (2022) treats the prompt as a cause of the task-specific predictor and investigates biases in prompt-based probing evaluations. Chain of thought Wei et al. (2022) discovers that LLM can perform better on reasoning tasks when the prompt is designed as a series of short sentences that mimic the reasoning process of humans.

# 6 CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK

Procedural planning is a newly emerged research area of great importance to various applications, such as household robots and virtual assistants. We propose a neuro-symbolic procedural PLANner (PLAN) with commonsense-infused prompts elicited from the external knowledge base to solve the procedural planning problem in a zero-shot manner. Experiments show the effectiveness of our proposed PLAN on both automatic and human evaluation results. Extending neuro-symbolic procedural planning to handle the long-horizon composite tasks and provide effective automatic evaluation metrics are important directions for future work.

# 7 ETHICAL STATEMENT

Given the limited diversified cultural background of the dataset we are using from RobotHow and WikiHow, we assume our results may be biased toward a single cultural background. For instance, given the task "make breakfast", it should take multi-culture into consideration to generate the procedural plans.
