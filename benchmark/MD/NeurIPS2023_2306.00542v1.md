# Abstract

We study causal representation learning, the task of inferring latent causal variables and their causal relations from high-dimensional functions ("mixtures") of the variables. Prior work relies on weak supervision, in the form of counterfactual pre- and post-intervention views or temporal structure; places restrictive assumptions, such as linearity, on the mixing function or latent causal model; or requires partial knowledge of the generative process, such as the causal graph or the intervention targets. We instead consider the general setting in which both the causal model and the mixing function are nonparametric. The learning signal takes the form of multiple datasets, or environments, arising from unknown interventions in the underlying causal model. Our goal is to identify both the ground truth latents and their causal graph up to a set of ambiguities which we show to be irresolvable from interventional data. We study the fundamental setting of two causal variables and prove that the observational distribution and one perfect intervention per node suffice for identifiability, subject to a genericity condition. This condition rules out spurious solutions that involve fine-tuning of the intervened and observational distributions, mirroring similar conditions for nonlinear cause-effect inference. For an arbitrary number of variables, we show that two distinct paired perfect interventions per node guarantee identifiability. Further, we demonstrate that the strengths of causal influences among the latent variables are preserved by all equivalent solutions, rendering the inferred representation appropriate for drawing causal conclusions from new data. Our study provides the first identifiability results for the general nonparametric setting with unknown interventions, and elucidates what is possible and impossible for causal representation learning without more direct supervision.

# 1 Introduction

Causal representation learning seeks to describe high-dimensional, low-level observations through a small number of interpretable, causally-related latent variables [102, 104]. In doing so, its goal is to combine the strengths of classical causal inference with those of modern machine learning.

A causal model represents an entire family of distributions arising from interventions on a system of variables [10, 89, 94]. This provides a principled way for reasoning about distribution shifts, which facilitates out-of-distribution generalization and planning [9, 57, 90, 96]. However, causal models require that most (or at least some) relevant causal variables are directly observed. While reasonable in domains such as economics [7], social [85] or biomedical science [43, 52], this assumption has challenged the application of causal methodology to complex and high-dimensional data [79].

Machine learning, on the other hand, has proven successful at learning useful "representations"—latent vectors generating the observables via some nonlinear map—of high-dimensional data such as images, video, or text [16, 20, 70, 100]. However, most methods rely on independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) data and only extract associational information. As a consequence, they often fail under distribution shifts and do not generalize beyond the training distribution [101], as exhibited by their reliance on spurious correlations [13, 81] or their vulnerability to adversarial examples [37, 113].

It has been argued that to address these shortcomings, we ought to learn representations endowed with causal model semantics. However, a major challenge to this goal is that different representations can explain the same observations equally well: strictly simpler representation learning tasks, such as disentanglement [14] or independent component analysis (ICA) [48], are already non-identifiable in general [49, 77]. Identifiability studies are thus required to characterize additional assumptions under which the desired latent variables can be provably recovered [4, 17, 21, 39, 40, 46, 47, 50, 51, 59, 62, 63, 68, 78, 84, 95, 106, 108, 120, 130]. In causal representation learning, we need not only identify the latents, but also the causal graph encoding their relations. Even in the fully observed case, this task of causal discovery, or structure learning, is already challenging, since the graph can only be recovered up to Markov equivalence [109] based only on (observational) i.i.d. data [110], meaning that the direction of some edges cannot be determined. For representation learning settings, the task gets strictly harder. For instance, if the causal latents  $V_{i}$  in Fig. 1 (Left) form a valid representation, then replacing them by the independent exogenous  $U_{i}$  might be considered an equally valid alternative.

What sets causal representations apart? A crucial feature of causal variables is that they are the ones on which interventions are defined and whose relations we are interested in [89]. Causal discovery and causal representation learning thus often rely on non-i.i.d. data linked to interventions on the underlying causal variables [54, 65]. Unless all variables are subject to intervention, however, some fundamental differences between the fully observed and representation learning settings in the level of ambiguity in the graph remain [111], as illustrated in Fig. 1 (Right). In a sense, the non-identifiabilities of representation and structure learning combine, and both need to be addressed in conjunction.

Overview and Main Contributions. In the present work, we study the general nonparametric causal representation learning problem in which both the causal mechanisms and the mixing function are completely unconstrained (§ 3.1). Our goal (§ 3.2) is to identify the latent causal variables up to element-wise nonlinear rescaling and their graph up to isomorphism (Defn. 3.6), which we show to be irresolvable ambiguities in this case (Prop. 3.8). As motivated above, doing so (without further supervision) requires access to interventional data. To this end, we consider learning from heterogeneous data from multiple related distributions, or environments, that arise from interventions in a shared underlying causal model (§ 3.3). Crucially, we do not assume that the targets of interventions are known.

Our main efforts are spent on proving identifiability for this setting (§ 4). Specifically, we show that for the case of two latent causal variables, an observational environment and one for each perfect intervention on either variable suffice (Thm. 4.1)—provided that the intervened and unintervened mechanisms are not "fine-tuned" to each other, which we formalize in the form of a genericity condition (4.2). For any number of latent causal variables, we prove that access to pairs of environments corresponding to two distinct perfect interventions on each node guarantees identifiability (Thm. 4.3).

In § 5, we then deliberate on the broader implications of our results for learning causal representations from multiple interventional datasets. First, we sketch two possible learning approaches: one based on generative modelling and the other on an auto-encoding framework. We then investigate the question of how to use or interpret causal representations, and show that representations learnt from sufficiently diverse environments should indeed be causally interpretable, in the sense that certain quantities, such as the strengths of causal influences among variables, are preserved by all equivalent solutions (Thm. 5.2). We conclude by discussing limitations and extensions of our work in § 6.

# 2 Related Work

Prior work on causal representation learning with general nonlinear relationships (both among latents and between latents and observations) and without an explicit task or label typically relies on some form of weak supervision. One example of weak supervision is "multi-view" data consisting of tuples of related observations. von Kugelgen et al. [117] consider counterfactual pairs of observations arising from imperfect interventions through data augmentation, and prove identifiability for the invariant non-descendants of intervened-upon variables. Brehmer et al. [18] also use counterfactual pre- and post-intervention views and show that the latent SCM can be identified given all single-node perfect stochastic interventions. Another type of weak-supervision is temporal structure [2], possibly combined with nonstationarity [124, 125], interventions on known targets [74, 75], or observed actions inducing sparse mechanism shifts [67, 69, 104]. Other works use more explicit supervision in the form of annotations of the ground truth causal variables or a known causal graph [73, 105, 123].

A different line of work instead approaches causal representation learning from the perspective of causal discovery in the presence of latent variables [109]. Doing so from purely observational i.i.d. data requires additional constraints on the generative process, such as restrictions on the graph structure or particular parametric and distributional assumptions, and typically leverages the identifiability of linear ICA [26, 35, 72]. For linear, non-Gaussian models, Silva et al. [107] show that the causal DAG can be recovered up to Markov equivalence if all observed variables are "pure" in that they have a unique latent causal parent. Cai et al. [22] and Xie et al. [121, 122] extend this result to identify the full graph given two pure observed children per latent, and Adams et al. [1] provide sufficient and necessary graphical conditions for full identification. For discrete causal variables, Kivva et al. [61] introduce a similar "no twins" condition to reduce the task of learning the number and cardinality of latents and the Markov-equivalence class of the graph to mixture identifiability.

Recent work has relaxed such graphical constraints by relying on interventional data in the form of multiple datasets, or environments. Similar to [124, 125], Liu et al. [76] leverage recent advances in nonlinear ICA [50, 58] to identify a linear causal model with context-dependent weights and nonlinear mixing from sufficiently diverse environments. Most closely related to the present work are recent works which also study identifiability based on all single node interventions [5, 111, 116], thus mirroring the result of [18] in different interventional setups. Squires et al. [111] provide results for linear causal models and linear mixing; Ahuja et al. [5] consider nonlinear causal models and polynomial mixings, subject to additional constraints on the latent support [119]; and Varici et al. [116] employ a score-based approach for nonlinear causal models and linear mixing. A more detailed comparison of existing results with the present study is provided in Tab. 1 in Appx. A.

Other lines of work have investigated the relationship between causal models at different levels of coarse-graining or abstraction [6, 11, 12, 23-25, 32, 99, 126, 127], or learning causal, or invariant, representations in a supervised setting [3, 8, 30, 66, 80, 86, 118, 119], often for domain generalization.

# 3 Problem Setting: Nonparametric Causal Representation Learning

In this section, we describe the considered problem setting and state our main assumptions. First, we specify the assumed data generating process (§ 3.1) and learning task in the form of a target

identifiability class (§ 3.2). We then demonstrate the hardness of our task from i.i.d. data or imperfect interventions and use this to motivate a multi-environment approach with perfect interventions (§ 3.3).

Notation. We use upper-case  $X$  for random variables and lower-case  $x$  for their realizations. Bold uppercase  $X$  denotes random vectors and lowercase  $x$  their realizations. We assume throughout that all distributions  $P_{X}$  possess densities  $p(x)$  with respect to (w.r.t.) the Lebesgue measure. We denote the pushforward of  $P_{X}$  through a measurable function  $f$  by  $f_{*}(P_{X})$ , and write  $[n] = \{1, \dots, n\}$ .

# 3.1 Data Generating Process

The assumed data generating process consists of a latent causal model and a mixing function, see Fig. 1 (left). For the former, we build on the structural causal model (SCM) framework [89, 94].

Definition 3.1 (Latent SCM). Let  $V = \{V_{1},\dots,V_{n}\}$  denote a set of causal "endogenous" variables, with each  $V_{i}$  taking values in  $\mathbb{R}$ , and let  $U = \{U_{1},\dots,U_{n}\}$  denote a set of mutually independent "exogenous" random variables. The latent SCM consists of a set of structural equations

$$
\left\{V _ {i} := f _ {i} \left(\mathbf {V} _ {\mathrm {p a} (i)}, U _ {i}\right) \right\} _ {i = 1} ^ {n}. \tag {3.1}
$$

where  $V_{\mathrm{pa}(i)} \subseteq V \setminus \{V_i\}$  are the direct causes, or causal parents, of  $V_i$ , and  $f_i$  are deterministic functions; and a fully factorized joint distribution  $P_U$  over the exogenous variables. The associated causal diagram  $G$ , a directed graph with vertices  $V$  and edges  $V_j \to V_i$  iff.  $V_j \in V_{\mathrm{pa}(i)}$ , is assumed acyclic.

By acyclicity, recursive substitution of the assignments in (3.1) yields the reduced form  $V = f_{\mathrm{RF}}(U)$ . The SCM thus induces a unique distribution  $P_V$  over the endogenous variables, given by the pushforward of  $P_U$  via (3.1), that is,  $P_V = f_{\mathrm{RF}^*}(P_U)$ . By construction,  $P_V$  is Markovian w.r.t. the causal graph  $G$  [89, 94], meaning that its density obeys the causal Markov factorization:

$$
p \left(v _ {1}, \dots , v _ {n}\right) = \prod_ {i = 1} ^ {n} p _ {i} \left(v _ {i} \mid v _ {\mathrm {p a} (i)}\right). \tag {3.2}
$$

We place the following additional assumption on the distribution  $P_V$  induced by the SCM.

Assumption 3.2 (Faithfulness). The only (conditional) independence relations satisfied by  $P_{\mathbf{V}}$  are of the form  $V_{i} \perp V_{\mathrm{nd}(i)} \mid V_{\mathrm{pa}(i)}$ , where  $V_{\mathrm{nd}(i)} \subseteq V \setminus \{V_{i}\}$  denotes the non-descendants of  $V_{i}$  in  $G$ .

Asm. 3.2 ensures a one-to-one correspondence between (conditional) independence in  $P_V$  and graphical separation in  $G$  and is a standard assumption in causal discovery [109]. Faithfulness rules out cancellations of influences along different paths, which occurs with probability zero for random path-coefficients [115]. It can thus also be viewed as a minimality or genericity assumption.

In contrast to classical causal inference, we assume that both the exogenous variables  $\mathbf{U}$  and the endogenous causal variables  $\mathbf{V}$  are unobserved. Instead, we will only have access to  $d$ -dimensional nonlinear mixtures  $\mathbf{X}$  of  $\mathbf{V}$ . We therefore make the following additional assumption.

Assumption 3.3 (Known  $n$ ). The number  $n$  of latent causal variables is known.

Next, we specify the relationship between the unobserved causal variables  $V$  and the observed  $X$ .

Definition 3.4 (Mixing function). The observations  $X$  are deterministically generated from  $V$  by applying a mixing function  $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^d$  to  $V$ , that is  $X := f(V)$ .

The terminology and setting of a deterministic mixing is rooted in the nonlinear ICA literature [51]. In deep generative models, it is commonly relaxed by considering additive noise in Defn. 3.4 [58, 84].

For representation learning scenarios, we are particularly interested in the case  $n \ll d$ . To allow for recovery of  $V$  from  $X$ , we assume that  $f$  is invertible, which is a standard assumption for identifiability.

Assumption 3.5 (Diffeomorphic mixing).  $f$  is a diffeomorphism onto its image  $\operatorname{Im}(f) = \mathcal{X} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ .

# 3.2 Learning Target and Identifiability Class

Our goal is to infer the underlying latent causal variables  $\mathbf{V} = f^{-1}(\mathbf{X})$  and their causal relations. We therefore consider the true unmixing function  $f^{-1}$  and the causal graph  $G$  our joint learning target:

$f^{-1}$  informs us how to map observations  $\mathbf{X}$  to causal variables  $\mathbf{V}$ , and  $G$  tells us how to factorise the implied joint  $p(\mathbf{v})$  into the causal mechanisms  $p_i(v_i \mid v_{\mathrm{pa}(i)})$  from (3.2). Given only observations of  $\mathbf{X}$ , this is a challenging task since neither  $\mathbf{V}$  nor  $G$  are directly observed or known a priori.

When is a candidate solution  $(h, G')$  that satisfies a given learning objective (such as maximizing the likelihood, possibly subject to additional constraints) guaranteed to match the ground truth  $(f^{-1}, G)$ ? This is the subject of identifiability studies and the main focus of our work. A statistical model  $\mathcal{P} = \{p_{\theta} : \theta \in \Theta\}$  with parameter space  $\Theta$  is identifiable if the mapping  $\theta \mapsto p_{\theta}$  is injective [71]. For representation learning tasks, full identifiability is often not attainable, as there are some fundamental ambiguities that cannot be resolved. One therefore typically instead considers identifiability up to an appropriately chosen equivalence class in the model space [2, 58, 120].

For the assumed data generating process (§ 3.1), the order of the causal variables is arbitrary, since  $V$  is unobserved. We can therefore assume without loss of generality (w.l.o.g.) that the  $V_{i}$ 's are partially ordered w.r.t.  $G$ , that is,  $V_{i} \rightarrow V_{j} \Rightarrow i < j$ . Learning  $G$  thus reduces to inferring whether the edges  $\{V_{1}, \ldots, V_{i-1}\} \rightarrow V_{i}$  exist for  $i = 2, \ldots, n$ . The only remaining permutation ambiguity arises from permutations  $\pi$  that preserve the partial order: for example, if  $G$  is given by  $V_{1} \rightarrow V_{3} \leftarrow V_{2}$ , the order of  $V_{1}$  and  $V_{2}$  cannot be uniquely determined without further assumptions. Moreover, the scaling of the causal variables is also arbitrary: any invertible element-wise transformation can be undone as part of  $f$ . We therefore define the desired identifiability class through the following equivalence relation.

Definition 3.6 ( $\sim_{\mathrm{CRL}}$ -identifiability). Let  $\mathcal{H}$  be a space of unmixing functions  $h: \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}^n$  and let  $\mathcal{G}$  be the space of DAGs over  $n$  vertices. Let  $\sim_{\mathrm{CRL}}$  be the equivalence relation on  $\mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{G}$  defined as

$$
\left(h _ {1}, G _ {1}\right) \sim_ {\mathrm {C R L}} \left(h _ {2}, G _ {2}\right) \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \left(h _ {2}, G _ {2}\right) = \left(\boldsymbol {P} _ {\pi^ {- 1}} \circ \phi \circ h _ {1}, \pi (G _ {1})\right) \tag {3.3}
$$

for some element-wise diffeomorphism  $\phi(\pmb{v}) = (\phi_1(v_1), \dots, \phi_n(v_n))$  of  $\mathbb{R}^n$  and a permutation  $\pi$  of  $[n]$  such that  $\pi : G_1 \mapsto G_2$  is a graph isomorphism and  $\pmb{P}_{\pi}$  the corresponding permutation matrix.

Remark 3.7. When  $G$  has no edges, any permutation is admissible and  $\sim_{\mathrm{CRL}}$  reduces to the standard notion of identifiability up to permutation and element-wise reparametrisation of nonlinear ICA [51].

The ground truth  $(f^{-1}, G)$  is identified up to  $\sim_{\mathrm{CRL}}$  by a given learning objective if any candidate solution  $(h, G')$  satisfies  $(h, G') \sim_{\mathrm{CRL}} (f^{-1}, G)$ . We seek to discover suitable conditions that ensure this.

The following result, presented more formally in Appx. B, states that  $\sim_{\mathrm{CRL}}$  is, in fact, the best we can hope for when learning from interventional data, without any more direct forms of supervision.

Proposition 3.8 (Minimality of  $\sim_{\mathrm{CRL}}$ ; informal). Let  $Z$  be any representation that is  $\sim_{\mathrm{CRL}}$  equivalent to  $V$ , with  $G' = \pi(G)$  the associated DAG. Then for any intervention on  $V_{\mathcal{I}} \subseteq V$  in  $G$ , there exists an equally sparse intervention on  $Z_{\pi(\mathcal{I})} \subseteq Z$  in  $G'$  inducing the same observed distribution on  $X$ .

# 3.3 Multi-Environment Setup

Given only a single dataset of i.i.d. observations from  $P_{\mathbf{X}}$ , there is no hope for  $\sim_{\mathrm{CRL}}$ -identifiability. Even for observed  $\mathbf{V}$  (i.e., with  $n = d$  and known  $f = \mathrm{id}$ ),  $G$  can only be identified up to Markov equivalence [109]. With unknown mixing  $f$ , the degree of observational non-identifiability gets even worse: for example, by using the reduced form of the SCM (§ 3.1) we can express  $\mathbf{X}$  in terms of the latent exogenous variables  $\mathbf{U}$  via  $\mathbf{X} = f(\mathbf{V}) = f \circ f_{\mathrm{RF}}(\mathbf{U})$  [102]. This gives rise to a "spurious ICA solution"  $(f_{\mathrm{RF}}^{-1} \circ f^{-1}, G_{\mathrm{ICA}})$  where  $G_{\mathrm{ICA}}$  denotes the empty graph with independent components. Due to the non-identifiability of nonlinear ICA [49, 77], however, we cannot even learn the composition  $f \circ f_{\mathrm{RF}}$ , let alone separate it into its constituents  $f$  and  $f_{\mathrm{RF}}$  to isolate the intermediate causal variables  $\mathbf{V}$ .

Motivated by these challenges to identifiability from i.i.d. data, we instead consider learning from multiple environments  $e \in \mathcal{E}$ . That is, we assume access to heterogenous data from multiple distinct distributions  $P_{X}^{e}$ . Environments can arise, for example, from different experimental settings or correspond to broader contexts such as climate or time. Previous work has shown that this setting can, in principle, provide useful causal learning signals [5, 8, 19, 30, 31, 42, 45, 54, 64-66, 76, 83, 92, 93, 96, 98, 103, 111, 114, 116]. However, multi-environment data is not necessarily useful if the corresponding distributions  $P_{X}^{e}$  are allowed to differ in arbitrary ways. What makes this setting interesting is the assumption that certain parts of the causal generative process are shared across environments.

Figure 2: Multi-Environment Setup with Single-Node Perfect Interventions and Shared Mixing Function. Illustration of the considered multi-environment setup for  $n = 2$  causal variables  $V = \{V_{1}, V_{2}\}$  with graph  $G$  given by  $V_{1} \to V_{2}$ , shared mixing function  $f$ , and environments  $\mathcal{E} = \{e_{0}, e_{1}, e_{2}\}$ , corresponding to the observational setting  $(e_{0})$  and perfect stochastic interventions on  $V_{1}$  (in  $e_{1}$ ) and  $V_{2}$  (in  $e_{2}$ ). The learnt unmixing function, or encoder, is denoted by  $h$ , and the inferred latent representation by  $Z = h(X)$ . The corresponding inferred latent distributions  $Q_{Z}^{e_{i}} = h_{*}(P_{X}^{e_{i}})$  are Markovian w.r.t. the candidate graph  $G'$  (here, equal to  $G$ ). Since the intervention targets are not known, they may in principle differ in  $Q_{Z}^{e}$  as shown here. However, as we show in Thm. 4.1, such misalignment is only possible if a certain genericity condition (4.2) is violated.

Here, we assume that all environments share the same invariant mixing function and underlying SCM, and that any distribution shifts arise from interventions on some of the causal mechanisms. General interventions can be modelled in SCMs by replacing some of the equations in (3.1) by new assignments  $V_{i} \coloneqq \tilde{f}_{i}(V_{\mathrm{pa}(i)}, \tilde{U}_{i})$ , resulting in "intervened" mechanisms  $\tilde{p}_{i}(v_{i} \mid v_{\mathrm{pa}(i)})$  replacing the corresponding conditionals  $p_{i}(v_{i} \mid v_{\mathrm{pa}(i)})$  in (3.2) [28, 33, 89]. We summarise this as follows.

Assumption 3.9 (Shared mixing and mechanisms). Each environment  $e$  shares the same mixing  $f$ ,

$$
P _ {\boldsymbol {X}} ^ {e} = f _ {*} \left(P _ {\boldsymbol {V}} ^ {e}\right)
$$

and each  $P_V^e$  results from the same SCM through an intervention on a subset of mechanisms  $\mathcal{I}^e\subseteq [n]$

$$
p ^ {e} (\boldsymbol {v}) = p ^ {e} (v _ {1}, \dots , v _ {n}) = \prod_ {i \in \mathcal {I} ^ {e}} p _ {i} ^ {e} \left(v _ {i} \mid \boldsymbol {v} _ {\mathrm {p a} (i)}\right) \prod_ {j \in [ n ] \backslash \mathcal {I} ^ {e}} p _ {j} \left(v _ {j} \mid \boldsymbol {v} _ {\mathrm {p a} (j)}\right).
$$

Importantly, the intervention targets  $\mathcal{I}^e$  are not assumed to be known.

An intervention on some  $V_{i}$  that fully removes the influence from its parents  $V_{\mathrm{pa}(i)}$  is referred to as perfect, otherwise as imperfect. It has been shown that imperfect interventions are generally insufficient for full identifiability [18], even in the linear case [111]. This is intuitive: if arbitrary imperfect interventions were allowed, including ones which preserve  $f_{i}\big(V_{\mathrm{pa}(i)},\cdot \big)$  and only replace  $U_{i}$  with some new  $\tilde{U}_i$ , then the spurious ICA solution  $(f_{\mathrm{RF}}^{-1}\circ f^{-1},G_{\mathrm{ICA}})$  should be indistinguishable from the ground truth. In line with prior work [5, 18, 111, 116], we therefore assume perfect interventions.

Assumption 3.10 (Perfect interventions). For all  $e \in \mathcal{E}$  and  $i \in \mathcal{I}^e$ , we have  $p^e(v_i \mid v_{\mathrm{pa}(i)}) = p^e(v_i)$ .

Based on the principle of independent causal mechanisms [94, 103], the sparse mechanism shift hypothesis [92, 104] posits that distribution changes tend to manifest themselves in a sparse or local way in the causal factorization. In this spirit, we will assume single-node ("atomic") interventions for our main results, as also required for existing results [5, 18, 111, 116].

As motivated in § 3.2, given data from  $\{P_{\mathbf{X}}^{e}\}_{e \in \mathcal{E}}$  we consider candidate solutions of the form  $(h, G')$  where  $h$  is an unmixing function, or encoder, which maps observations  $\mathbf{X}$  to the inferred latents  $\mathbf{Z} = h(\mathbf{X})$ , and  $G'$  is causal graph capturing the relations among the  $Z_{i}$ . The corresponding distributions of the inferred latents are thus given by the push-forward

$$
Q _ {\boldsymbol {Z}} ^ {e} = h _ {*} \left(P _ {\boldsymbol {X}} ^ {e}\right) = (h \circ f) _ {*} P _ {\boldsymbol {V}} ^ {e}
$$

The multi-environment setup with unknown single-node perfect interventions is illustrated in Fig. 2.

# 4 Identifiability Theory

We now present our identifiability results. We first study the most fundamental bivariate case with two latent causal variables  $V_{1}$  and  $V_{2}$ . This can loosely be seen as the causal representation learning analogue of the widely studied cause-effect problem  $(X \to Y$  or  $Y \to X?$ ) in structure learning [82, 94].

Theorem 4.1 (Bivariate identifiability up to  $\sim_{\mathrm{CRL}}$  from one perfect stochastic intervention per node). Suppose that we have access to multiple environments  $\{P_X^e\}_{e\in \mathcal{E}}$  generated as described in § 3 under Asms. 3.2, 3.5, 3.9 and 3.10 with  $n = 2$ . Let  $(h,G')$  be any candidate solution such that the inferred latent distributions  $Q_Z^e = h_*(P_X^e)$  of  $Z = h(X)$  and the inferred mixing function  $h^{-1}$  satisfy the above assumptions w.r.t. the candidate causal graph  $G'$ . Assume additionally that

(A1) all densities  $p^e$  and  $q^e$  are continuously differentiable and fully supported on  $\mathbb{R}^n$ ;  
(A2) we have access to a known observational environment  $e_0$  and one single node perfect intervention for each node, with unknown targets: there exist  $n + 1$  environments  $\mathcal{E} = \{e_i\}_{i=0}^n$  such that  $\mathcal{I}^{e_0} = \emptyset$  and for each  $i \in [n]$  we have  $\mathcal{I}^{e_i} = \{\pi(i)\}$  for an unknown permutation  $\pi$  of  $[n]$ ;  
(A3) for all  $i \in [n]$ , the intervened mechanisms  $\tilde{p}_i(v_i)$  differ from the corresponding base mechanisms  $p_i(v_i \mid v_{\mathrm{pa}(i)})$  everywhere, in the sense that

$$
\forall \boldsymbol {v}: \quad \frac {\partial}{\partial v _ {i}} \frac {\tilde {p} _ {i} \left(v _ {i}\right)}{p _ {i} \left(v _ {i} \mid \boldsymbol {v} _ {\mathrm {p a} (i)}\right)} \neq 0; \tag {4.1}
$$

(A4) ("genericity") the base and intervened mechanisms are not fine-tuned to each other, in the sense that there exists a continuous function  $\varphi : \mathbb{R}^{+} \to \mathbb{R}$  for which

$$
\mathbb {E} _ {\boldsymbol {v} \sim P _ {V} ^ {e _ {0}}} \left[ \varphi \left(\frac {\tilde {p} _ {2} \left(v _ {2}\right)}{p _ {2} \left(v _ {2} \mid v _ {1}\right)}\right) \right] \neq \mathbb {E} _ {\boldsymbol {v} \sim P _ {V} ^ {e _ {1}}} \left[ \varphi \left(\frac {\tilde {p} _ {2} \left(v _ {2}\right)}{p _ {2} \left(v _ {2} \mid v _ {1}\right)}\right) \right] \tag {4.2}
$$

Then the ground truth is identified in the sense of Defn. 3.6, that is,  $(f^{-1}, G) \sim_{\mathrm{CRL}} (h, G')$ .

Proof sketch (full proof in Appx. C.2). Consider  $V_{1} \to V_{2}$  (the proof for  $V_{1} \nrightarrow V_{2}$  is similar). Let  $\psi = f^{-1} \circ h^{-1}: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$  such that  $\pmb{V} = \psi(\pmb{Z})$ . By the change of variable formula, for all  $z$

$$
q ^ {e} (\boldsymbol {z}) = p ^ {e} (\psi (\boldsymbol {z})) | \det  \boldsymbol {J} _ {\psi} (\boldsymbol {z}) | \tag {4.3}
$$

where  $(J_{\psi}(z))_{ij} = \frac{\partial\psi_i}{\partial z_j} (z)$  denotes the Jacobian of  $\psi$ . We consider two separate cases, depending on whether the intervention targets in  $q^{e_i}$  for  $e_i\in \{e_1,e_2\}$  match those in  $p^{e_i}$  (Case 1) or not (Case 2).

Case 1: Aligned Intervention Targets. According to Asm. 3.9 and (A2), (4.3) applied to the known observational environment  $e_0$  and the interventional environments  $e_1, e_2$  leads to the system of equations:

$$
q _ {1} \left(z _ {1}\right) q _ {2} \left(z _ {2} \mid z _ {\mathrm {p a} \left(2; G ^ {\prime}\right)}\right) = p _ {1} \left(\psi_ {1} (z)\right) p _ {2} \left(\psi_ {2} (z) \mid \psi_ {1} (z)\right) | \det  J _ {\psi} (z) | \tag {4.4}
$$

$$
\tilde {q} _ {1} \left(z _ {1}\right) q _ {2} \left(z _ {2} \mid z _ {\operatorname {p a} \left(2; G ^ {\prime}\right)}\right) = \tilde {p} _ {1} \left(\psi_ {1} (z)\right) p _ {2} \left(\psi_ {2} (z) \mid \psi_ {1} (z)\right) | \det  J _ {\psi} (z) | \tag {4.5}
$$

$$
q _ {1} \left(z _ {1}\right) \tilde {q} _ {2} \left(z _ {2}\right) = p _ {1} (\psi_ {1} (z)) \tilde {p} _ {2} (\psi_ {2} (z)) | \det  J _ {\psi} (z) | \tag {4.6}
$$

where  $z_{\mathrm{pa}(2;G')}$  denotes the parents of  $z_2$  in  $G'$ . Taking quotients of (4.5) and (4.4) yields

$$
\frac {\tilde {q} _ {1}}{q _ {1}} (z _ {1}) = \frac {\tilde {p} _ {1}}{p _ {1}} (\psi_ {1} (z)) \quad \stackrel {\frac {\partial}{\partial z _ {2}}} {\Longrightarrow} \quad 0 = \left(\frac {\tilde {p} _ {1}}{p _ {1}}\right) ^ {\prime} (\psi_ {1} (z)) \frac {\partial \psi_ {1}}{\partial z _ {2}} (z) \quad \stackrel {(A 3)} {\Longrightarrow} \quad \frac {\partial \psi_ {1}}{\partial z _ {2}} (z) = 0. \tag {4.7}
$$

Thus  $V_{1} = \psi_{1}(Z_{1})$  and  $q_{1}(z_{1}) = p_{1}(\psi_{1}(z_{1}))\left|\frac{\partial\psi_{1}}{\partial z_{1}}(z_{1})\right|$ . Substitution into (4.6) yields

$$
\tilde {q} _ {2} \left(z _ {2}\right) = \tilde {p} _ {2} \left(\psi_ {2} \left(z _ {1}, z _ {2}\right)\right) \left| \frac {\partial \psi_ {2}}{\partial z _ {2}} \left(z _ {1}, z _ {2}\right) \right| \tag {4.8}
$$

where we have used that, according to (4.7),  $J_{\psi}$  is triangular. According to (4.8), for all  $z_1$ , the mapping  $z_2 \mapsto \psi_2(z_1, z_2)$  is measure preserving for  $\tilde{q}_2$  and  $\tilde{p}_2$ . By Lemma C.1, it follows that

$\psi_{2}$  must be constant in  $z_{1}$ . Hence,  $\psi$  is an element-wise function. Finally,  $G = G^{\prime}$  follows from faithfulness (Asm. 3.2), for otherwise  $(V_{1}, V_{2}) = (\psi_{1}(Z_{1}), \psi_{2}(Z_{2}))$  would be independent.

Case 2: Misaligned Intervention Targets. If  $G' \neq G$ , a similar argument to Case 1 (with the roles of  $z_1$  and  $z_2$  interchanged) also yields a contradiction to faithfulness. This leaves  $G = G'$ . Writing down the system of equations similar to (4.4)-(4.6), and then taking ratios of  $e_1$  and  $e_2$  with  $e_0$  yields

$$
\frac {\tilde {q} _ {1}}{q _ {1}} (z _ {1}) = \frac {\tilde {p} _ {2} (\psi_ {2} (\boldsymbol {z}))}{p _ {2} (\psi_ {2} (\boldsymbol {z}) \mid \psi_ {1} (\boldsymbol {z}))} \quad \text {a n d} \quad \frac {\tilde {q} _ {2} (z _ {2})}{q _ {2} (z _ {2} \mid z _ {1})} = \frac {\tilde {p} _ {1}}{p _ {1}} (\psi_ {1} (\boldsymbol {z})). \tag {4.9}
$$

These conditions highlight the misalignment in intervention targets (see Fig. 2). Unlike in Case 1, they do not directly imply that some elements of  $J_{\psi}$  need to be zero, that is  $Z$  can be arbitrarily mixed w.r.t.  $V$ . However, (4.9) imposes constraints on the form of  $\psi$  that, by exploiting the invariance of  $q_{1}$  across  $e_{0}$  and  $e_{1}$  while  $p_{1}$  changes to  $\tilde{p}_{1}$ , can ultimately be shown to only be satisfied if the two expectations in (4.2) are equal for all continuous  $\varphi$ . However, such fine-tuning is ruled out by (A4).

Remark 4.2. The main difficulty of the proof is that (4.9) may, in principle, hold when  $(p,\tilde{p},q,\tilde{q})$  and  $\psi$  are completely unconstrained. This setting does not arise in prior work if the intervention targets are known [73-75] (Case 1), or the densities or mixing are parametrically constrained [3, 111, 116].

On the Genericity Condition (A4). The condition in (4.2) contrasts expectations of the same quantity w.r.t. the observational distribution  $P_V^{e_0}$  and the interventional distribution  $P_V^{e_1}$  corresponding to an intervention on  $V_{1}$  that turns  $p_1$  into  $\tilde{p}_1$ . The shared argument, on the other hand, is a function of the ratio between the intervened mechanism  $\tilde{p}_2$  and its base mechanism  $p_2$ . While the two expectations are always equal for linear  $\varphi$ , other choices imply non-trivial constraints. For instance,  $\varphi(y) = y^2$  yields

$$
\int \left(\tilde {p} _ {1} (v _ {1}) - p _ {1} (v _ {1})\right) \int \frac {\tilde {p} _ {2} (v _ {2}) ^ {2}}{p _ {2} (v _ {2} \mid v _ {1})} \mathrm {d} v _ {2} \mathrm {d} v _ {1} \neq 0.
$$

Since  $p_1 \neq \tilde{p}_1$  by assumption (A3), we argue that (A4) should generally hold for randomly chosen  $(p_1, p_2, \tilde{p}_1, \tilde{p}_2)$  and can only be violated if they are fine-tuned to each other. It can thus be viewed as encoding some notion of genericity—in line with the principle of independent causal mechanisms [15, 40, 55, 94, 103], but also involving the intervened mechanisms. Interestingly, related genericity conditions also arise in the study of nonlinear cause-effect inference from observational data, where identifiability is often obtained up to a set of pathological ("fine-tuned") spurious solutions satisfying a partial differential equation involving the original mechanisms [44, 53, 112, 128]. Further, we note that  $\varphi$  in (4.2) can also be thought of as a witness function of genericity, similar to witness functions in kernel-based two sample and independence testing [41].

Next, we provide our identifiability result for an arbitrary number of causal variables.

Theorem 4.3 (Identifiability up to  $\sim_{\mathrm{CRL}}$  from two paired perfect stochastic interventions per node). Suppose that we have access to multiple environments  $\{P_X^e\}_{e\in \varepsilon}$  generated as described in § 3 under Asms. 3.2, 3.3, 3.5, 3.9 and 3.10. Let  $(h,G')$  be any candidate solution such that the inferred latent distributions  $Q_Z^e = h_*(P_X^e)$  of  $Z = h(X)$  and the inferred mixing function  $h^{-1}$  satisfy the above assumptions w.r.t. the candidate causal graph  $G'$ . Assume additionally that

(A1) all densities  $p^e$  and  $q^e$  are continuously differentiable and fully supported on  $\mathbb{R}^n$ ;

(A2') we have access to two paired single-node perfect interventions for each node, with unknown targets: there exist  $n$  known pairs of environments  $\mathcal{E} = \{(e_i, e_i')\}_{i=1}^n$  such that for each  $i \in [n]$  we have  $\mathcal{I}^{e_i} = \mathcal{I}^{e_i'} = \{\pi(i)\}$  for an unknown permutation  $\pi$  of  $[n]$ ;

(A3') for all  $i \in [n]$ , the intervened mechanisms  $\tilde{p}_i(v_i)$  and  $\tilde{p}_i(v_i)$  differ everywhere, in the sense that

$$
\forall v _ {i}: \quad \left(\frac {\tilde {\tilde {p}} _ {i}}{\tilde {p} _ {i}}\right) ^ {\prime} (v _ {i}) \neq 0; \tag {4.10}
$$

Then the ground truth is identified in the sense of Defn. 3.6, that is,  $(f^{-1}, G) \sim_{\mathrm{CRL}} (h, G')$ .

Proof sketch (full proof in Appx. C.3). W.l.o.g., fix the intervention targets in  $p$  to  $\mathcal{I}^{e_i} = \mathcal{I}^{e_i'} = \{i\}$  and let  $\pi$  be a permutation of  $[n]$  such that  $\pi(i)$  denotes the inferred intervention target in  $q$  that by (A2') is shared across  $(e_i, e_i')$ . The first part of the proof is similar to Case 1 in the proof of Thm. 4.1: similar to (4.7), considering the ratios between  $e_i$  and  $e_i'$ , taking partial derivatives w.r.t.  $z_j$ , and using assumption (A3') yields  $\frac{\partial \psi_i}{\partial z_j}(z) = 0$  for any  $j \neq \pi(i)$ . This implies that  $V_i = \psi_i(Z_{\pi(i)})$ , that is,  $\psi$  is a permutation composed with an element-wise function. It remains to show that  $\pi$  is a graph isomorphism, that is,  $V_i \to V_j$  in  $G \iff Z_{\pi(i)} \to Z_{\pi(j)}$  in  $G'$ . We prove  $\Rightarrow$ ; the other direction is analogous. Suppose for a contradiction that there exist  $(i,j)$  such that  $V_i \to V_j$  in  $G$ , but  $Z_{\pi(i)} \not\to Z_{\pi(j)}$  in  $G'$ . Consider  $e_i$  in which there are perfect interventions on  $Z_{\pi(i)}$  and  $V_i$ . For  $Z_{\pi(k)} \in Z_{\mathrm{pa}(\pi(j);G')}$ , let  $\tilde{V}_k = \psi_k(Z_{\pi(k)})$  and denote  $\tilde{V} = \cup_k \tilde{V}_k \subset V \setminus \{V_i, V_j\}$ . Since  $Z_{\pi(i)}$  and  $Z_{\pi(j)}$  are d-separated by  $Z_{\mathrm{pa}(\pi(j);G')}$  in the post-intervention graph  $G'_{\overline{Z}_{\pi(i)}}$  with arrows pointing into  $Z_{\pi(i)}$  removed [89], it follows by Markovianity of  $q$  w.r.t.  $G'$  that  $Z_{\pi(i)} \perp Z_{\pi(j)} | Z_{\mathrm{pa}(\pi(j);G')}$  in  $Q_Z^{e_i}$ . By applying the corresponding diffeomorphic functions  $\psi_i$ , it follows from Lemma C.2 in Appx. C.1 that  $V_i \perp V_j | \tilde{V}$  in  $P_V^{e_i}$ . This violates faithfulness (Asm. 3.2) of  $P_V$  to  $G$  since  $V_i$  and  $V_j$  are d-connected in  $G_{\overline{V}_i}$ .

(A2') states that we know that a pair of datasets corresponds to two distinct interventions on the same underlying variable, even though we may not know the exact target of the intervention. This situation could arise, for example, if both datasets are collected under the same experimental setup but with varying experimental parameters. An observational environment is then not needed, cf. (A2) in Thm. 4.1.

(A3') states that the intervention mechanisms are distinct in that their ratio is strictly monotonic, similar to (4.1) in (A3). This is a slightly stronger version of the assumption of interventional discrepancy proposed by Liang et al. [73], which has been shown to be necessary for identifiability even if the graph  $G$  is known. For Gaussian  $\tilde{p}_i, \tilde{\tilde{p}}_i$ , (A3') is satisfied, for example, by a shift in mean. In the proofs, this is used to show that  $\psi$  must be an element-wise function, see (4.7). Intuitively, if  $\tilde{p}_i = \tilde{\tilde{p}}_i$  in some open set for more than one  $i$ , then the underlying variables can be nonlinearly mixed by a measure-preserving automorphism within this set without affecting the observed distributions [73].

# 5 Implications for Causal Representation Learning

Learning Objectives. While our main focus is on studying identifiability, our theoretical insights also suggest approaches to learning causal representations from finite interventional datasets sampled from  $\{P_{\mathbf{X}}^{e}\}_{e\in \varepsilon}$ . The main idea is to fit the data in a way that preserves the sparsity of interventions [92, 104] by employing the same (un)mixing function and sharing mechanisms across environments (Asm. 3.9). One approach would be an autoencoder framework in which we jointly learn an encoder  $h$ , a graph  $G^{\prime}$ , and intervention targets  $\mathcal{T}^e$  such that the encoded latents  $\mathbf{Z} = h(\mathbf{X})$  can be used to reconstruct the observed  $\mathbf{X}$  across all environments, while ensuring all but the intervened mechanisms are shared. Using  $E$  as an environment indicator, the latter corresponds to the constraint  $Z_{i}\perp E\mid Z_{\mathrm{pa}(i;G^{\prime})}$  for  $i\notin \mathcal{T}^e$ , implementable through a suitable conditional independence test [36, 88, 129]. Another approach would be to fit a generative model: given a candidate graph and candidate intervention targets, learn base and intervened mechanisms  $(p_i,\tilde{p}_i)$  and a mixing function  $f$  by maximum likelihood, then pick a graph and intervention targets that achieve the best fit. According to our theory, both approaches should asymptotically identify the ground truth up to  $\sim_{\mathrm{CRL}}$  if the set of environments  $\mathcal{E}$  is sufficiently diverse (and the other assumptions hold). We experimentally explore a generative approach based on normalizing flows in Appx. D.

Interpreting Causal Representations. Suppose that we succeed in identifying  $V$  and  $G$  up to  $\sim_{\mathrm{CRL}}$  (Defn. 3.6). How can we use or interpret such a causal representation? Since the scale of the variables is arbitrary (\$ 3.2), we clearly cannot predict the exact outcomes of interventions. We therefore seek causal quantities that are preserved by the irresolvable ambiguities of  $\sim_{\mathrm{CRL}}$ . A prime candidate for this are interventional causal notions defined in terms of information theoretic quantities [27].

Definition 5.1 (Causal influence; based on Defn. 2 of Janzing et al. [56]). Let  $P_V$  be Markovian w.r.t. a DAG  $G$  with vertices  $V$ . For any  $V_i \to V_j$  in  $G$ , the causal influence of  $V_i$  on  $V_j$  is given by

$$
\mathfrak {C} _ {i \rightarrow j} ^ {P _ {V}} := D _ {\mathrm {K L}} \big (P _ {V} \parallel P _ {V} ^ {i \rightarrow j} \big), \quad \text {w h e r e} \quad p _ {j} ^ {i \rightarrow j} \big (v _ {j} \mid \boldsymbol {v} _ {\mathrm {p a} (j) \backslash \{i \}} \big) = \int p _ {j} \big (v _ {j} \mid \boldsymbol {v} _ {\mathrm {p a} (j)} \big) p _ {i} (v _ {i})   \mathrm {d} v _ {i}
$$

and  $P_{\mathbf{V}}^{i \to j}$  is the interventional distribution arising from replacing the  $j^{\text{th}}$  mechanism by  $p_j^{i \to j}$ .<sup>9</sup>

The following result, proven in Appx. C.4, states that the causal influences are invariant to reparametrisation and equivariant to permutations, the two irresolvable ambiguities of the  $\sim_{\mathrm{CRL}}$  equivalence class.

Theorem 5.2 (Preservation of causal influences under  $\sim_{\mathrm{cRL}}$ ). Let  $P_V$  be Markovian w.r.t.  $G$ , let  $\pi$  be a graph isomorphism of  $G$ , and let  $\phi$  be an element-wise diffeomorphism. Let  $\mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{P}_{\pi^{-1}} \circ \phi(\mathbf{V})$  and denote its induced distribution by  $Q_{\mathbf{Z}}$ . Then for any  $V_i \to V_j$  in  $G$  we have  $\mathfrak{C}_{i \to j}^{P_V} = \mathfrak{C}_{\pi(i) \to \pi(j)}^{Q_Z}$ .

Thm. 5.2 implies that the strength of causal relations among variables in the inferred graph carry meaning. They can thus be used to uncover changes to the latent causal mechanisms underlying different experimental datasets, for example, to gain scientific insights when combined with domain knowledge.

# 6 Conclusions and Discussion

While previous work relied on counterfactual data or parametric constraints for identifiability, our study demonstrates that interventional data can be sufficient—even in the nonparametric case in which the spaces of mixing functions and mechanisms are infinite dimensional. Our results can be seen as a first step towards justifying the use of highly expressive machine learning methods for learning interpretable causal representations from high-dimensional experimental data in situations where parametric assumptions are undesirable, for example, for complex systems in physics, biology, or medicine.

Weaker Notions of Identifiability. In the present work, we have focused on the strongest notion of identifiability that is achievable in a nonparametric setting (Defn. 3.6). However, subject to the available data and the assumptions we are willing to make, identifying the variables and graph up to  $\sim_{\mathrm{CRL}}$  will not always be possible. In this case, weaker notions of identifiability may be of interest. For example, we may not be able to uniquely recover variables that are not targeted by interventions [5, 74, 117], or only recover groups of variables up to (non-)linear mixing [5, 116, 117] and the graph up to transitive closure [111] if interventions are imperfect, or soft. A precise characterization of weaker notions of nonparametric identifiability from different types of interventions (cf. [67] for a temporal, semi-parametric setting) is an interesting direction for future work.

Known vs. Unknown Intervention Targets. A key aspect of our study is that the intervention targets are not assumed to be known. While this substantially complicates the analysis (see Remark 4.2), unknown interventions are certainly more realistic. The world is full of domain shifts and different environments. In many cases, we cannot pin down what exactly differs between two domains, but we believe that it can reasonably be modelled as a change in some causal mechanisms.

Identifiability From One Intervention per Node for Any  $n$ . We conjecture that Thm. 4.1 can be generalized to  $n > 2$ , subject to a suitably adjusted set of genericity conditions involving several intervened and base mechanisms, akin to (4.2) for the bivariate case. The main challenge to such a generalization appears to be combinatorial, as there are  $n! - 1$  ways of misaligning intervention targets across  $p$  and  $q$ . In Thm. 4.3, we sidestep this issue by assuming pairs of environments. Thus, while two single-node perfect interventions are sufficient, we do not believe this to be necessary.

On the Assumption of Known  $n$  and Its Relation to Markovianity. Asm. 3.3 relates to the notions of causal sufficiency or Markovianity in classical causal inference, which correspond to the assumption of independent  $U_{i}$  in Defn. 3.1, implying the causal Markov factorization (3.2) [89, 94, 109]. With unobserved  $V$  and unknown  $n$ , the notion of "unobserved confounders" gets blurred, since one can, in principle, always construct a causally sufficient system by increasing  $n$  and adding any causes of two or more endogenous variables to  $V$ . Asm. 3.3 then states that the minimum number of variables required to do so is known. However, this can lead to very large systems, which may in turn challenge the assumption of an invertible mixing (Asm. 3.5). Extensions of our analysis to unknown  $n$ , non-Markovian, or non-invertible models constitute an interesting direction for future investigations.
