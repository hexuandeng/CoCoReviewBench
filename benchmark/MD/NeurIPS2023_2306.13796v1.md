# Abstract

We consider a weakly supervised learning scenario where the supervision signal is generated by a transition function  $\sigma$  of labels associated with multiple input instances. We formulate this problem as multi-instance Partial Label Learning (multi-instance PLL), which is an extension to the standard PLL problem. Our problem is met in different fields, including latent structural learning and neuro-symbolic integration. Despite the existence of many learning techniques, limited theoretical analysis has been dedicated to this problem. In this paper, we provide the first theoretical study of multi-instance PLL with possibly an unknown transition  $\sigma$ . Our main contributions are as follows. Firstly, we propose a necessary and sufficient condition for the learnability of the problem. This condition nontrivially generalizes and relaxes the existing small ambiguity degree in the PLL literature, since we allow the transition to be deterministic. Secondly, we derive Rademacher-style error bounds based on a top- $k$  surrogate loss that is widely used in the neuro-symbolic literature. Furthermore, we conclude with empirical experiments for learning under unknown transitions. The empirical results align with our theoretical findings; however, they also expose the issue of scalability in the weak supervision literature.

# 1 Introduction

Consider a scenario where a learner aims to learn one or more classifiers  $f_{1}, \ldots, f_{n}$ , each of which maps an instance  $x$  to its corresponding label  $y$ . The learner is given training examples that consist of a vector of  $M > 1$  instances  $\mathbf{x} = (x_{1}, \ldots, x_{M})$ , and each  $x_{i}$  is processed by one of the  $f_{j}$ 's. Differently from supervised learning, the gold labels  $\mathbf{y} = (y_{1}, \ldots, y_{M})$  of  $\mathbf{x}$  are hidden; instead, the learner is provided with a weak label  $s$  which is produced by applying a transition function  $\sigma$  to the gold labels  $\mathbf{y}$ . The transition  $\sigma$  itself may be unknown to the learner.

The above weak supervision setting has been a topic of active research in the NLP community [40, 34, 33, 31, 42, 47, 20]. Recently, it has received renewed attention as it is met in neuro-symbolic frameworks [12], i.e., frameworks that integrate inference and learning over neural models with inference and learning over symbolic models, such as logical theories [29, 48, 11, 53, 41, 30, 22, 25]. The unique benefit of this setting over architectures that approximate the  $f_{i}$ 's and  $\sigma$  via an end-to-end neural model [45] is that it offers the ability to reuse the latent models, something particularly useful in NLP [33, 31]. Below, we present an example of our learning setting in neuro-symbolic learning.

Example 1 (SUM2). We aim to learn an MNIST classifier  $f$ . Differently from supervised learning, we use training examples of the form  $(x_{1}, x_{2}, s)$ , where  $x_{1}$  and  $x_{2}$  are MNIST digits and  $s$  is

their sum. The above implies a transition function  $\sigma : \{0, \ldots, 9\} \times \{0, \ldots, 9\} \to \{0, \ldots, 18\}$ , s.t.  $\sigma(y, y') = y + y'$ . The target sum  $s$  restricts the space of labels that can be assigned to the input pair of digits  $(x_1, x_2)$ , e.g., if  $s = 2$ , then the only combinations of labels that abide by the constraint that the sum is 2 are  $(2, 0)$ ,  $(1, 1)$  and  $(0, 2)$ . This problem is referred to as SUM2 in literature [22, 29].

The transition function  $\sigma$  is not necessarily one-to-one. For instance, multiple combinations of digits can lead to the same target sum. The above leaves us with two questions to answer: can we provide any learning guarantees for the classifiers  $f_{1},\ldots ,f_{n}$ ? and what if  $\sigma$  is unknown? Despite that multiple techniques for neuro-symbolic and latent structural learning have been recently proposed, those theoretical questions remain open. Practically, for the single input case ( $M = 1$ ), our setting can reduce to that of partial label learning (PLL), where each input instance is accompanied by a set of labels, including the gold one [23, 9, 4, 28, 38, 49, 51, 54]. However, a key PLL learnability assumption – that the probability a wrong label co-occurs with the gold one is always less than one [26]– is violated in our setting. This is because  $\sigma$  is a deterministic but not necessarily bijective function, as opposed to  $\sigma$  in PLL which is typically stochastic.

Due to its relevance to PLL, we refer to our learning setting as multi-instance PLL. Differently from prior art, e.g., [40, 34], we do not restrict  $\sigma$  to specific types of functions, e.g., linear functions. Hence, we can express constraints in several formal languages, including systems of Boolean equations and Datalog [1], a language widely used in neuro-symbolic techniques [22]. Notice that many neuro-symbolic learning techniques are oblivious to the implementation of the symbolic component: abstracting the symbolic component as a transition  $\sigma$  has been actually proposed as the means to compositionally integrate neural with symbolic models [41].

We make minimal assumptions on the data distributions. In fact, we prove learnability even under the "toughest" distributions that concentrate their mass on a single instance. In addition, we provide learning guarantees under the semantic loss [50], a widely-used surrogate loss for training classifiers subject to logical theories [29, 41, 22]. To our knowledge, we are the first to provide this theoretical analysis, closing a gap in the neuro-symbolic and latent structural learning literature.

Contributions. Our contributions can be summarized into the following:

- We prove learnability of the multi-instance PLL problem assuming a single classifier  $f$  and a known transition and provide a Rademacher-style error bound using a top- $k$  approximation to the semantic loss [50], see Section 3. Our analysis confirms the intuition that a larger  $k$  decreases the empirical risk but increases the number of samples required for learning.  
- We extend the above results for the more general case where one learns a vector of classifiers  $\mathbf{f} = (f_{1},\dots ,f_{n})$  classifying instances from different domains, see Section 4.  
- We prove learnability with a single classifier and an unknown transition function, see Section 5, and assess the validity of our theoretical results using SOTA neuro-symbolic frameworks, see Section 6.

Proofs of all the formal statements and further details of our empirical analysis are in the appendix.

# 2 Preliminaries

We use  $\mathbb{P}(\cdot)$  to denote probability mass,  $\mathbb{E}[\cdot]$  to denote expectation and  $\mathbb{1}\{\cdot\}$  to denote an indicator function. For a positive integer  $M$ , we use  $[M]$  as a short for  $\{1,\dots,M\}$ . For a vector of  $M$  elements  $(x_{1},\ldots,x_{M})$ , a position  $j$  is a number in  $[M]$  used to identify the element  $x_{j}$ . A vector is said to be diagonal if the elements in all positions are equal. Suppose  $f$  is a function with domain  $\mathcal{X}$  and  $\pmb{x}$  is a vector of  $M$  elements in  $\mathcal{X}$ , we use the symbol  $f(\pmb{x})$  to denote the vector  $(f(x_{1}),\dots,f(x_{M}))$ .

Classifiers. Let  $\mathcal{X}$  denote an instance space and  $\mathcal{Y}$  denote an output space with  $|\mathcal{Y}| = c$ . Let also  $\mathcal{D}$  be the joint distribution of two random variables  $(X,Y)\in \mathcal{X}\times \mathcal{Y}$  and  $\mathcal{D}_X$  be the marginal of  $X$ . Throughout the text, we use the bold symbol  $\pmb{x}$  to denote  $(x_{1},\ldots ,x_{M})$  and  $\pmb{y}$  to denote the corresponding gold labels. We consider scoring functions of the form  $f:\mathcal{X}\to \Delta_c$ , where  $\Delta_c$  is the space of probability distributions on  $\mathcal{Y}$  (e.g.,  $f$  outputs the softmax probabilities of a neural network). We use  $f^{j}(x)$  to denote the value of the  $j$ -th output element of  $f(x)$ . A scoring function  $f$  induces

a classifier whose prediction  $[f](x)$  on  $x$  is defined by  $[f](x) := \operatorname{argmax}_{j \in [c]} f^j(x)$ . We use  $\mathcal{F}$  to denote the space of scoring functions and  $[\mathcal{F}]$  to denote the space of classifiers induced by  $\mathcal{F}$ . We will use VC-dimension, Natarajan dimension and Rademacher complexities to characterize the complexity of  $\mathcal{F}$ , which are defined in Appendix A.

Loss functions. We define the problem of learning a scoring function using a fixed set of training samples as the one of choosing the scoring function from  $\mathcal{F}$  that better fits the training samples. We give semantics to the term fits by using the risk  $\mathcal{R}(f;\ell)$  of  $f$  subject to a sampling distribution and a given loss function  $\ell$ : the lower  $\mathcal{R}(f;\ell)$ , the better  $f$  fits the data. When there exists an  $f^{*} \in \mathcal{F}$ , such that  $\mathcal{R}(f^{*};\ell) = 0$ , we say that the space  $\mathcal{F}$  is realizable under  $\ell$ . We use  $\widehat{\mathcal{R}}$  to denote the empirical risk, i.e., the risk computed over the training samples only. In supervised learning, we are provided with samples of  $(x,y)$  drawn from  $\mathcal{D}$ . The risk $^2$  of classifier  $f$  subject to a loss function  $\ell: \mathcal{Y} \times \mathcal{Y} \to \mathbb{R}^+$  is given by  $\mathcal{R}(f;\ell) := \mathbb{E}_{(X,Y) \sim \mathcal{D}}[\ell([f](X),Y)]$ .

A commonly used loss function is the zero-one loss defined by  $\ell^{01}(y, y') := \mathbb{1}\{y' \neq y\}$  for any given  $y, y' \in \mathcal{Y}$ . We use  $\mathcal{R}^{01}(f)$  as a short for  $\mathcal{R}(f; \ell^{01})$  and refer to it as the zero-one risk of  $f$ . We will also consider Semantic Loss (SL) [50], which has been adopted to train classifiers subject to Boolean formulas [29, 41, 22]. Let  $\varphi$  be a Boolean formula where each variable in  $\varphi$  is associated with a class from  $\mathcal{Y}$ . Then, the SL of  $\varphi$  subject to  $f(x)$  is the cross entropy of the weighted model counting (WMC) [5] of  $\varphi$  under  $f(x)$ , namely  $\mathrm{SL}(\varphi, f(x)) := -\log(\mathrm{WMC}(\varphi, f(x)))$ , where  $\mathrm{WMC}(\varphi, f(x))$  lies in  $(0, 1)$  and denotes the probability that  $\varphi$  is logically satisfied when its variables become true with probabilities specified by  $f(x)$ . We discuss WMC and the semantic loss in detail in Appendix A.

# 3 Learning A Single Classifier under a Known Transition

We begin with the setting where the goal is to learn a single classifier  $f \in \mathcal{F}$  with a known transition  $\sigma$ . Firstly, we discuss ERM-learnability with the partial zero-one loss (Theorem 1). Then, inspired by neuro-symbolic learning, we provide a Rademacher-style error bound with a top- $k$  surrogate loss based on weighted model counting (Theorem 2). Below, we formally introduce our learning setting.

Problem setting. Let  $\sigma : \mathcal{Y}^M \to S$  be a transition function, where  $S = \{1, \dots, d\}$  with  $|S| = d \geq 1$ . Let also  $\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{P}}$  be a set of  $m_{\mathbb{P}}$  partially labeled samples of the form  $(\boldsymbol{x}, s) = (x_1, \dots, x_M, s)$ . Each training sample is formed by, firstly drawing  $M$  i.i.d. samples  $(x_i, y_i)$  according to  $\mathcal{D}$  and then setting  $s = \sigma(y_1, \dots, y_M)$ . We use  $\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{P}}$  to denote the distribution followed by the training samples  $(\boldsymbol{x}, s)$ . In analogy to the zero-one classification loss  $\ell^{01}$ , we define the zero-one partial loss subject to  $\sigma$  as  $\ell_{\sigma}^{01}(\boldsymbol{y}, s) := \mathbb{1}\{\sigma(\boldsymbol{y}) \neq s\}$ , for any  $\boldsymbol{y} \in \mathcal{Y}^M$  and  $s \in S$ . Furthermore, we define the zero-one partial risk of  $f$  subject to  $\sigma$  as  $\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{P}}^{01}(f; \sigma) := \mathbb{E}_{(X_1, \dots, X_M, S) \sim \mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{P}}}[\ell_{\sigma}^{01}([f](X_1), \dots, [f](X_M)), S]$ .

We demonstrate these notions with Example 1. There,  $f$  is an MNIST classifier,  $\mathcal{X}$  is the space of MNIST digits and  $\mathcal{Y} = \{0,\dots ,9\}$ . The training samples are of the form  $(x_{1},x_{2},s)$  where  $s = \sigma (y_1,y_2) = y_1 + y_2$ . The partial risk of  $f$  is the probability of predicting the wrong sum.

Learnability. We say a PLL problem is learnable, if there exists a partial learning algorithm  $\mathcal{A}$  which takes a partially labeled dataset  $\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{P}}$  with  $|\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{P}}| = m_{\mathsf{P}}$  as input and outputs a function  $\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{P}})\in \mathcal{F}$  that satisfies: for any data distribution  $\mathcal{D}$  over  $\mathcal{X}\times \mathcal{Y}$  and any  $\delta ,\epsilon \in (0,1)$ , there is an integer  $m_{\epsilon ,\delta}$  such that  $m_{\mathsf{P}}\geq m_{\epsilon ,\delta}$  implies  $\mathcal{R}^{01}(\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{P}}))\leq \epsilon$  with probability at least  $1 - \delta$ . In particular, we consider the Empirical Risk Minimizer (ERM) algorithm, which finds the classifier  $f\in \mathcal{F}$  that minimizes the empirical estimates of the zero-one partial risk, denoted by  $\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathsf{P}}^{01}(f;\sigma ;\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{P}})$ .

# 3.1 ERM Learnability

To prove learnability under the partial zero-one loss, we need to bound  $\mathcal{R}^{01}(f)$  with the partial risk  $\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{P}}^{01}(f;\sigma)$ . Firstly, we provide a sufficient and necessary condition called  $M$ -unambiguity.

Recall that learnability requires handling all possible distributions of  $(\pmb{x},\pmb{y})$ . Therefore, to propose a necessary condition to guarantee learnability, we consider the most intriguing case: the one in

which  $\mathcal{D}$  concentrates its mass on a single item  $x_0$ , i.e.,  $\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}_X}(x_0) = 1$ . In that case, the only sample observed during learning includes only the instance  $x_0$ . Now, let  $l_0 \in \mathcal{V}$  be the gold label of  $x_0$ . If  $f$  mispredicts the class of  $x_0$ , i.e.,  $f(x_0) \neq l_0$ , and this misprediction leads to the same observed partial label, i.e.,  $\sigma(f(x_0), \ldots, f(x_0)) = \sigma(l_0, \ldots, l_0)$ , then the problem will be not learnable, as we will never be able to correct this error. So, we propose the following condition:

Definition 1 (M-unambiguity). Transition  $\sigma$  is  $M$ -unambiguous if for any two diagonal label vectors  $\mathbf{y}$  and  $\mathbf{y}' \in \mathcal{V}^M$  such that  $\mathbf{y} \neq \mathbf{y}'$ , we have that  $\sigma(\mathbf{y}') \neq \sigma(\mathbf{y})$ .

Below, we provide examples of transitions that satisfy (or not) the  $M$ -unambiguity condition.

Example 2. Consider the following learning problems.

- (SUM-M): Consider a variant of Example 1 where  $s$  is the sum of  $M$  digits, i.e.,  $\sigma(y_1, \ldots, y_M) = \sum_{i=1}^{M} y_i$ . The transition is  $M$ -unambiguous, since  $\sigma(y, \ldots, y) = \sigma(y', \ldots, y')$  holds for any  $y \neq y'$ .  
- (PRODUCT-M): Consider a variant of Example 1 where  $s$  is given by  $\sigma^{*}(y_{1},\ldots ,y_{M}) = \prod_{i = 1}^{M}y_{i}$ . Then,  $\sigma^{*}$  is  $M$ -unambiguous, since  $\sigma^{*}(y,\dots ,y) = y^{M}\neq (y^{\prime})^{M}$  holds for any  $y\neq y^{\prime}$ .  
-  $(XOR)$ : Consider Boolean labels. We take the  $XOR$  of the two digits as the partial label, i.e.,  $\sigma^{XOR}(y_1, y_2) = \mathbb{1}\{y_1 \neq y_2\}$ . Then  $\sigma^{XOR}$  is not  $M$ -unambiguous, since  $\sigma^{XOR}(1, 1) = \sigma^{XOR}(0, 0)$ .

$M$ -unambiguity is closely related to the small ambiguity degree condition in PLL literature. See Appendix E.3 for a detailed comparison. Although  $M$ -unambiguity is proposed as a necessary condition with special type of data distributions, we now show that it is also sufficient for proving ERM-learnability. Firstly, we prove the classification risk can be bounded by the partial risk.

Lemma 1. If  $\sigma$  is  $M$ -unambiguous, then we have:

$$
\mathcal {R} ^ {0 1} (f) \leq \mathcal {O} \left(\mathcal {R} _ {\mathrm {P}} ^ {0 1} (f; \sigma) ^ {1 / M}\right) \quad a s \quad \mathcal {R} _ {\mathrm {P}} ^ {0 1} (f; \sigma) \rightarrow 0 \tag {1}
$$

Moreover, if  $\sigma$  is not  $M$ -unambiguous, then learning from partial labels is arbitrarily difficult, in the sense that a classifier  $f$  with partial risk  $\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{P}}^{01}(f;\sigma) = 0$  can have a risk of  $\mathcal{R}^{01}(f) = 1$ .

To show learnability, we bound the partial risk with its empirical counterpart in the realizable case.

Theorem 1 (ERM learnability under  $M$ -unambiguity). Suppose  $\mathcal{F}$  is realizable under  $\ell_{\mathsf{P}}^{01}$  and  $[\mathcal{F}]$  has a finite Natarajan dimension  $d_{[\mathcal{F}]}$ . Then for any  $\epsilon, \delta \in (0,1)$ , there exists a universal constant  $C_0 > 0$ , such that with probability at least  $1 - \delta$ , the empirical partial risk minimizer with  $\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathsf{P}}^{01}(f; \sigma; \mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{P}}) = 0$  has a classification risk  $\mathcal{R}^{01}(f) < \epsilon$ , if

$$
m _ {\mathrm {P}} \geq C _ {0} \frac {c ^ {2 M - 2}}{\epsilon^ {M}} \left(d _ {[ \mathcal {F} ]} \log (6 c M d _ {[ \mathcal {F} ]}) \log \left(\frac {c ^ {2 M - 2}}{\epsilon^ {M}}\right) + \log \left(\frac {1}{\delta}\right)\right) \tag {2}
$$

Toward a faster convergence rate. Theorem 1 presents a rather slow convergence rate of  $(c / \epsilon)^M$ . In the following, we show that a better convergence rate is possible by introducing the concept of 1-unambiguity. 1-unambiguity requires the transition to be sensitive to 1-position perturbations:

Definition 2 (1-unambiguity). Transition  $\sigma$  is 1-unambiguous, if there exists an index  $1\leq i\leq M$  such that flipping the  $i$ -th label of any  $\mathbf{y}\in \mathcal{V}^{M}$ , results in a vector  $\pmb {y}'$  with  $\sigma (\pmb {y}')\neq \sigma (\pmb {y})$

In the following, we provide examples of transitions that satisfy (or not) the 1-unambiguity condition.

Example 3. Let us continue with Example 2. The transition  $\sigma$  from SUM-M is 1-unambiguous since  $\sigma(\pmb{y}) = \sum_{i=1}^{M} y_i$  always change when replacing any of the labels  $y_i$ . However, the transition  $\sigma^*$  from PRODUCT-M is not 1-unambiguous, since for the label vector  $\pmb{y} = (0,1,1)$  (with product zero), the flipped vector  $\pmb{y}' = (0,1,2)$  also leads to product zero.

We show that a better convergence rate can be achieved if  $\sigma$  is both 1- and  $M$ -unambiguous.

Proposition 1 (ERM learnability under 1- and  $M$ -unambiguity). If  $\sigma$  is both 1- and  $M$ -unambiguous, then we have:

$$
\mathcal {R} ^ {0 1} (f) \leq \mathcal {O} \left(\mathcal {R} _ {\mathrm {P}} ^ {0 1} (f; \sigma)\right) \quad a s \quad \mathcal {R} _ {\mathrm {P}} ^ {0 1} (f; \sigma) \rightarrow 0 \tag {3}
$$

Furthermore, if  $\mathcal{F}$  is realizable under  $\ell_{\mathsf{P}}^{01}$  and  $[\mathcal{F}]$  has a finite Natarajan dimension  $d_{[\mathcal{F}]}$ , then for any  $\delta \in (0,1)$  and  $\epsilon \in (0,1)$  that is sufficiently close to 0, there exists a universal constant  $C_1$ , such that with probability at least  $1 - \delta$ , the empirical partial risk minimizer with  $\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathsf{P}}^{01}(f;\sigma) = 0$  has a classification risk  $\mathcal{R}^{01}(f) < \epsilon$ , if

$$
m _ {\mathbb {P}} \geq C _ {1} \frac {1}{\epsilon} \left(d _ {[ \mathcal {F} ]} \log (6 c M d _ {[ \mathcal {F} ]}) \log \left(\frac {2}{\epsilon}\right) + \log \left(\frac {1}{\delta}\right)\right) \tag {4}
$$

Remark. The supervision power of a partial label  $s$  depends on the "instability" of  $\sigma$ : if  $\sigma$  returns different results under some perturbations in  $I$  positions for an  $I \in [M]$ , then the classification risk is bounded by  $\mathcal{R}^{01}(f) \leq \mathcal{O}(\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{P}}^{01}(f; \sigma)^{1 / I})$ . To formalize this intuition, in the appendix, we provide a generalized definition of both 1- and  $M$ -unambiguity, called  $I$ -unambiguity, and show learnability.

# 3.2 Error Bounds with the Top- $k$  Semantic Loss

We now study the error bounds with the semantic loss (see Section 2 and Appendix A) under top- $k$  approximations (Theorem 2). We are interested in this loss for two reasons. Firstly, risk minimization under the zero-one loss is intractable even for linear classifiers [15] and hence in practice, learning is performed by minimizing differentiable surrogate losses such as the semantic loss. Also, in our case, there is a second source of intractability: that of enumerating the entries of  $\sigma$  [22, 41, 30], e.g., in SUM- $M$ , we have to enumerate  $10^{M}$  digit combinations to populate  $\sigma$ , which is not scalable.

A popular way to reduce the second source of inefficiency is to consider only the  $k$  most probable label combinations during training, where the probability of a label vector  $\mathbf{y}$  assuming sample  $(\mathbf{x}, s)$  and classifier  $f$  is defined by  $P_{f(\mathbf{x})}(\mathbf{y}) \coloneqq \prod_{i=1}^{M} f^{y_i}(x_i)$ . Then, training can proceed by taking the semantic loss over the top- $k$  label vectors [22]. This is possible, as the top- $k$  label vectors can be equivalently viewed as a formula that is true iff one or more of the top- $k$  label vectors is the gold one.

Example 4. Let us return back to Example 1. Instead of considering all three combinations  $(2,0)$ ,  $(1,1)$  and  $(0,2)$  assuming a target  $s = 2$ , we can consider only the first and the last combination, if the probabilities of the first and the second image being 1 are way lower than those of being 0 or 2. Then, the top-2 label vectors  $(2,0)$ ,  $(0,2)$  can be seen as the formula  $(A_{1,2} \wedge A_{2,0}) \vee (A_{1,0} \wedge A_{2,2})$  where  $A_{i,j}$  is a Boolean variable that is true iff the  $i$ -th input digit has label  $j$ .

We use  $\bigvee_{i=1}^{k} \pmb{y}_{i}$  to denote the Boolean formula computed out of the  $k$  label vectors  $\pmb{y}_{1}, \ldots, \pmb{y}_{k}$ , where each  $\pmb{y}_{i}$  is the conjunction of Boolean variables of the form  $A_{i,y}$ , as in Example 4. Given a softmax prediction  $f(\pmb{x})$ , variable  $A_{i,y}$  is assigned probability  $f^{y}(x_{i})$ . We now define the top- $k$  partial loss:

Definition 3 (Top- $k$  partial loss). The top- $k$  partial loss for a given integer  $k \geq 1$  subject to a classifier  $f$ , transition  $\sigma$  and sample  $(x, s)$  is defined as

$$
\ell_ {\sigma} ^ {k} (f (\boldsymbol {x}), s) := \operatorname {S L} \left(\bigvee_ {i = 1} ^ {k} \boldsymbol {y} ^ {(i)}, f (\boldsymbol {x})\right) \tag {5}
$$

where  $\pmb{y}^{(1)},\dots,\pmb{y}^{(k)}\in \mathcal{Y}^M$  are the top-  $k$  maximizers of  $P_{f(\pmb{x})}$  in the preimage of  $s$ , i.e.,  $\{\pmb {y}\in \mathcal{V}^M:\sigma (\pmb {y}) = s\}$ . The top-  $k$  partial classification risk of  $f$  subject to  $\sigma$  is then given by

$$
\mathcal {R} _ {\mathsf {P}} ^ {k} (f; \sigma) := \mathbb {E} _ {(X _ {1}, \dots , X _ {M}, S) \sim \mathcal {D} _ {\mathsf {P}}} \left[ \ell_ {\sigma} ^ {k} \left(f \left(X _ {1}, \dots , X _ {M}\right), S\right) \right] \tag {6}
$$

Remark. The WMC is upper bounded by the sum of probabilities, so we can approximate the SL as  $\ell_{\sigma}^{k}(f(\pmb{x}), s) \geq -\log (\sum_{i=1}^{k} P_{f(\pmb{x})}(\pmb{y}^{(i)}))$ . Furthermore, the special case where  $k = 1$  reduces to the infimum loss [4] and minimal loss [28] in the PLL literature as we show in Appendix B.2.

Let  $\Re_m(\mathcal{F})$  denote the Rademacher complexity [3, 8] of  $\mathcal{F}$  with  $m$  samples as defined in Appendix A. We are now ready to bound the zero-one classification risk with the empirical top- $k$  partial risk.

Theorem 2 (Error bound under unambiguity). Let an integer  $k \geq 1$  and  $\delta \in (0,1)$ . If  $\sigma$  is both 1- and  $M$ -unambiguous, then with probability at least  $1 - \delta$ , we have:

$$
\mathcal {R} ^ {0 1} (f) \leq \Phi \left((k + 1) \left(\widehat {\mathcal {R}} _ {\mathsf {P}} ^ {k} (f; \sigma ; \mathcal {T} _ {\mathsf {P}}) + 2 \sqrt {k} M ^ {3 / 2} \Re_ {M m _ {\mathsf {P}}} (\mathcal {F}) + \sqrt {\frac {\log (1 / \delta)}{2 m _ {\mathsf {P}}}}\right)\right) \tag {7}
$$

where  $\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathsf{P}}^{k}(f;\sigma ;\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{P}}) = \sum_{(\boldsymbol {x},s)\in \mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{P}}}\ell_{\sigma}^{k}(f(\boldsymbol {x}),s) / m_{\mathsf{P}}$  is the empirical counterpart of (6) and  $\Phi$  is an increasing function that satisfies  $\lim_{t\to 0}\Phi (t) / t = 1$

Proof sketch. The proof of Theorem 2 builds upon several results. Firstly, we define a  $\ell^1$  loss for probabilistic predictions that plays a similar role as the ramp loss [27] in margin theory, and show that the  $\ell^1$  loss bridges the gap between the zero-one partial loss and the surrogate semantic loss. Secondly, we prove that the semantic loss is Lipschitz with the output probabilities of the model  $f$ . The final result then follows by applying the standard Rademacher-style error bound with the contraction lemma from [8].

Remark. Theorem 2 suggests that the difficulty of learning increases as  $M$  becomes large. This is intuitive, since it gets harder to disambiguate the gold labels when  $M$  increases, e.g., for 2-SUM  $10^{2}$  possible label vectors need to be considered, while for 4-SUM,  $10^{4}$  ones. Our bound also suggests a tradeoff with the choice of  $k$ : a larger  $k$  decreases the risk, but tends to increase the complexity term.

# 4 Learning Multiple Classifiers under a Known Transition

In this section, we extend the learning problem from Section 3 to jointly learn  $n \geq 1$  different classifiers with shared partial labels. Similarly to Section 3, we aim to propose minimal assumptions for the data distribution to show both results on ERM-learnability (Theorem 3) and a Rademacher-style error bound with the top- $k$  loss (Theorem 4). We formally define the learning setting below.

Problem setting. We aim to learn  $n \geq 1$  classifiers, each of which maps instances from a space  $\mathcal{X}_i$  to the corresponding label space  $\mathcal{Y}_i$  with  $|\mathcal{Y}_i| = c_i$ . For each  $i \in [n]$ , we define a scoring space  $\mathcal{F}_i$ , which contains mappings of the form  $f_i: \mathcal{X}_i \to \Delta_{c_i}$ . Each  $f_i$  is used to classify  $M_i \geq 1$  instances  $(x_{i1},\ldots ,x_{iM_i}) = x_i \in \mathcal{X}_i^{M_i}$  with (hidden) gold labels  $\pmb{y}_i = (y_{i1},\dots,y_{iM_i})$ . We denote the vector of scoring functions as  $\pmb{f} = (f_1,\dots,f_n)$ . Let  $\mathcal{D}_i$  be the joint distribution over elements from  $\mathcal{X}_i \times \mathcal{Y}_i$ . Each training sample given to the learner  $(\pmb{x}_1,\dots,\pmb{x}_n,s)$  is formed by (i) drawing  $M_i$  i.i.d. samples  $(\pmb{x}_i,\pmb{y}_i)$  from  $\mathcal{D}_i$  for each  $i \in [n]$  and then (ii) obtaining its partial label as  $s = \sigma(\pmb{y}_1,\dots,\pmb{y}_n)$ . We override the notation  $\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{P}}$  to denote the distribution of the training samples in this learning setting. The partially labeled dataset  $\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{P}}$  then contains  $m_{\mathbb{P}}$  i.i.d. samples drawn from  $\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{P}}$ . The learner aims to find the classifiers  $f_1,\dots,f_n$  with the minimal zero-one risk defined as  $\mathcal{R}^{01}(\pmb{f}) := \sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{R}^{01}(f_i)$ . As the gold labels are hidden, the learner uses the dataset  $\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{P}}$  to estimate and minimize the zero-one partial risk of  $f_1,\dots,f_n$ , which is defined as  $\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{P}}^{01}(f_1,\dots,f_n;\sigma) := \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{X}_1,\dots,\mathbf{X}_n,S)}\sim_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{P}}}[\ell_{\sigma}^{01}(([f_1](\mathbf{X}_1),\dots,[f_n](\mathbf{X}_n)),S)]$ , where  $[f_i](\mathbf{X}_i)$  is a short for  $([f_i](X_{i1}),\dots,[f_i](X_{iM_i}))$ , for  $1 \leq i \leq M_i$ .

Example 5 (Learning binary operators). Consider a setting where we aim to train a classifier  $f_{1}$  for recognizing MNIST digits and a classifier for recognizing images of addition and multiplication taken from some space  $\mathcal{X}_2$ . The training samples are of the form  $(x_{11}, x_{12}, x_{21}, s)$ , where  $x_{11}$  and  $x_{12}$  are non-zero MNIST digits with  $\mathcal{Y}_{1} = \{1, \dots, 9\}$ ,  $x_{21}$  is an image of an operator with  $\mathcal{Y}_{2} = \{+, \times\}$ , and  $s$  is the result of applying the operator in  $x_{2}$  on the digits in  $x_{11}$  and  $x_{12}$ .

# 4.1 ERM Learnability

Similarly to Section 3, to propose a necessary condition for learnability, we consider the most challenging distribution where each  $\mathcal{D}_i$  is concentrated on a single instance  $x_{i}^{*}\in \mathcal{X}_{i}$ , for  $1\leq i\leq n$ . Then, the only sample that will be observed during learning is  $(\pmb{x}_1^*,\dots ,\pmb{x}_n^*)$  where  $\pmb {x}_i = (x_i^*,\ldots ,x_i^*)$ , for  $1\leq i\leq n$ . Assuming that  $l_{i}^{*}$  is the gold label of  $x_{i}^{*}$ , detecting a classification error is possible only if a misclassification of any  $x_{i}^{*}$ , i.e.,  $[f_i](x_i^*)\neq l_i^*$ , will lead to a prediction vector  $\pmb{y}^{\prime}$  with a different image under  $\sigma$  from the gold label  $\pmb{y}$ , i.e.,  $\sigma (\pmb{y}^{\prime})\neq \sigma (\pmb{y})$ . The above intuition

is captured via the notion of multi-unambiguity, which generalizes the notion of  $M$ -unambiguity. Below, we use  $\pmb{y}_i^{M_i}$ , for  $i \in [n]$ , to denote the  $M_i$ -ary diagonal vector including the  $y_i$  element only.

Definition 4 (Multi-unambiguousity). Transition  $\sigma$  is multi-unambiguous if for any vector  $\mathbf{y} = (\mathbf{y}_1^{M_1},\dots,\mathbf{y}_n^{M_n})$ , and any position  $i\in [n]$ , such that the vector  $\mathbf{y}'$  that results after flipping the labels in  $\mathbf{y}_i^{M_i}$  to some diagonal vector  $(\mathbf{y}_i')^{M_i}\neq \mathbf{y}_i^{M_i}$ , has a different image under  $\sigma$ , i.e.,  $\sigma(\mathbf{y})\neq \sigma(\mathbf{y}')$ .

Multi-unambiguity reduces to  $M$ -unambiguity for  $n = 1$ . An example of multi-unambiguity is below.

Example 6 (Learning binary operator, cont'd). In Example 5, the multi-unambiguity condition is violated since  $2 + 2 = 2 \times 2$ . Namely, if a distribution assigns all its weight to the digit 2, then it is difficult for the model to distinguish the two operators. On the other hand, if instead, the label space is  $\mathcal{Y}_1 = \{3, \ldots, 9\}$ , then the multi-unambiguity condition is satisfied.

Bounded risk assumption. Differently from Section 3, multi-unambiguity alone is not sufficient to ensure learnability. To see this, consider a variant of SUM2 where the digits are classified by two independent classifiers,  $f_{1}$  and  $f_{2}$ . If the distributions of the first and the second image are concentrated on 1 and 7 respectively, but  $f_{1}(x_{1}) = 7$  and  $f_{2}(x_{2}) = 1$ , then these errors cannot be detected. Therefore, we assume there is a constant  $R < 1$ , such that  $\mathcal{R}^{01}(f_i) \leq R$  holds for any  $i \in [n]$  and any  $f \in \mathcal{F}_i$ . We refer to such  $f_{i}$ 's as zero-one risk  $R$ -bounded. This assumption is mild since it only requires the classifiers to be slightly better than being totally wrong. Also, when a small directly labeled dataset is available, one can bound the classification risks away from 1 with high probability in a uniform manner using standard learning theory. See Appendix C for more details. Now, we are ready to state the ERM-learnability result assuming  $\prod_{i=1}^{n} \mathcal{F}_{i}$  is realizable under  $\ell_{\mathrm{P}}^{01}$ .

Theorem 3 (ERM learnability under multi-unambiguity). Assume that there is a constant  $R < 1$  such that for each  $i \in [n]$ , each  $f \in \mathcal{F}_i$  is zero-one risk  $R$ -bounded. Assume also that there exist positive integers  $M_*$ ,  $M^*$  and  $c^*$ , such that  $M_* \leq M_i \leq M^*$  and  $c_i \leq c_0$  hold for any  $i \in [n]$ . Then, if  $\sigma$  is multi-unambiguous, we have:

$$
\mathcal {R} ^ {0 1} (\boldsymbol {f}) \leq \mathcal {O} \left(\left(\mathcal {R} _ {\mathrm {P}} ^ {0 1} (\boldsymbol {f}; \sigma)\right) ^ {1 / M ^ {*}}\right) \quad a s \quad \mathcal {R} _ {\mathrm {P}} ^ {0 1} (\boldsymbol {f}; \sigma) \rightarrow 0 \tag {8}
$$

Furthermore, for any  $\epsilon, \delta \in (0,1)$ , there is a universal constant  $C_3$ , such that with probability at least  $1 - \delta$ , the empirical partial risk minimizer with  $\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathsf{P}}^{01}(f;\sigma) = 0$  has a classification risk  $\mathcal{R}^{01}(f) < \epsilon$  if

$$
m _ {\mathrm {P}} \geq C _ {3} \frac {n c _ {0} ^ {2 M ^ {*} - 2}}{\epsilon^ {M ^ {*}} (1 - R) ^ {M}} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} d _ {[ \mathcal {F} _ {i} ]} \log \left(n c _ {i} M _ {i} d _ {[ \mathcal {F} _ {i} ]}\right) \log \left(\frac {n c _ {0} ^ {2 M ^ {*} - 2}}{\epsilon^ {M ^ {*}} (1 - R) ^ {M}}\right) + \log \left(\frac {1}{\delta}\right)\right) \tag {9}
$$

Remark. This result suggests that the rate of convergence is controlled by  $M^{*}$ . A smaller  $M^{*}$  leads to a more strict unambiguity condition. For example, the case  $M^{*} = 1$  requires  $\sigma$  to be unstable to any 1-position perturbations, while the case  $M^{*} = M$  reduces to  $M$ -unambiguity. This observation confirms the intuition that the supervision power of partial labels depends on the "instability" of  $\sigma$ .

The top- $k$  partial loss  $\ell_{\sigma}^{k}(\pmb{f}(\pmb{x}), s)$  for multiple classifiers subject to  $\pmb{f} \in \mathcal{F}^n$ , transition  $\sigma$  and  $s \in S$  straightforwardly extends the top- $k$  partial loss for the single classifier case, see Definition 3. Similarly, the (empirical) top- $k$  partial classification risk of  $\pmb{f}$  subject to  $\sigma$  straightforwardly extends the one from Section 3.2. Below, we provide a Rademacher-style error bound with the top- $k$  loss.

Theorem 4 (Error bound under multi-unambiguousity with multiple classifiers). Suppose  $\sigma$  is multi-unambiguous and each  $f_{i}$  is zero-one risk  $R$ -bounded, for  $R \in (0,1)$ . Then, for any integer  $k \geq 1$  and any  $\delta \in (0,1)$ , with probability at least  $1 - \delta$ , we have:

$$
\mathcal {R} ^ {0 1} (\pmb {f}) \leq \left(\frac {n c _ {0} ^ {2 M ^ {*} - 2} (k + 1)}{(1 - R) ^ {M}} \left(\widehat {\mathcal {R}} _ {\mathsf {P}} ^ {k} (\pmb {f}; \sigma ; \mathcal {T} _ {\mathsf {P}}) + \sqrt {k M} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} M _ {i} \Re_ {m _ {\mathsf {P}} M _ {i}} (\mathcal {F} _ {i}) + \sqrt {\frac {\log (1 / \delta)}{2 m _ {\mathsf {P}}}}\right)\right) ^ {1 / M ^ {*}}
$$

# 5 Learning under an Unknown Transition

We now explore another direction by dropping the assumption that  $\sigma$  is known. Instead, we assume the learner has access to a transition space  $\mathcal{G}$  that contains mappings of the form  $\mathcal{Y}^{M} \to \mathcal{S}$  including

the transition  $\sigma$  that generates the samples. Notice that learning now becomes particularly challenging, since  $\mathcal{G}$  can be expressive enough to lead to correct predictions for  $s$  from wrong classifications.

Problem setting. To illustrate the core idea, we consider a simple setting where we only learn a single scoring function  $f \in \mathcal{F}$  as in Section 3. However,  $\sigma$  is an unknown mapping in  $\mathcal{G}$ . Given partially labeled samples, the learner aims to minimize the zero-one partial risk of  $f$  subject to  $\mathcal{G}$ ,  $\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{P}}^{01}(f; \mathcal{G})$  that is defined as the minimal partial risk to predict  $s$  with a candidate transition in  $\mathcal{G}$ , i.e.,  $\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{P}}^{01}(f; \mathcal{G}) := \inf_{\sigma^* \in \mathcal{G}} \mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{P}}^{01}(f; \sigma^*)$ . We demonstrate this learning setting with the following example:

Example 7. Let  $\mathcal{G} = \{(y_1, y_2) \mapsto \alpha y_1 + \beta y_2 | (\alpha, \beta) \in \mathbb{R}^2 - \{(0,0)\}\}$ , namely all weighted sums with at least one non-zero weight. We aim to learn an MNIST classifier  $f$ , given training samples of the form  $(x_1, x_2, s)$ , where the  $x_i$ 's are MNIST digits and  $s$  is the result of applying some  $\sigma$  from  $\mathcal{G}$  on  $(y_1, y_2)$ , where  $y_i$  is the prediction of  $f$  on  $x_i$ . The gold  $\sigma$ , i.e., the exact  $\alpha$  and  $\beta$ , is unknown.

# 5.1 ERM Learnability

Similarly to the known transition case, learnability requires us to learn under any data distribution  $\mathcal{D}$ . Differently from Sections 3 and 4, to propose a necessary learning condition, we consider the distribution where the mass in  $\mathcal{D}$  is concentrated to a single label, i.e., all instances  $x_{i}$  have the same gold label  $l^{*}$ , and hence, the training samples are all associated with the gold label vector  $\pmb{y} = \{l^{*}\}^{M}$ . In this case, it will be impossible to learn or to detect the errors, when there is a candidate transition  $\sigma^{\prime} \in \mathcal{G}$  which maps the vector  $\pmb{y}^{\prime}$  that results after one or more misclassifications to the gold observation of  $s$ , i.e.,  $\sigma(\pmb{y}) = \sigma(\pmb{y}^{\prime})$ . Since the true  $\sigma$  is unknown, to ensure learnability, conditions (i) and (ii) must not hold for each transition in  $\mathcal{G}$ . Below, we summarize our learnability condition:

Definition 5 (Unambiguous transition space). Transition space  $\mathcal{G}$  is unambiguous, if for each  $\sigma' \in \mathcal{G}$ , each diagonal label vector  $\pmb{y} = (l_i, \dots, l_i)$ , where  $l_i \in \mathcal{V}$ , and each  $l_j \neq l_i$ , where  $l_j \in \mathcal{V}$ , there exists a vector  $\pmb{y}' \in \{l_i, l_j\}^M$ , such that  $\sigma'(\pmb{y}') \neq \sigma(\pmb{y})$ .

Class  $\mathcal{G}$  in Example 7 is unambiguous: for each transition  $(y_{1},y_{2})\mapsto \alpha^{\prime}y_{1} + \beta^{\prime}y_{2}$  and each two labels  $l^{\prime}\neq l$  , the set  $\{\alpha^{\prime}y_{1} + \beta^{\prime}y_{2}|(y_{1},y_{2})\in \{l,l^{\prime}\}^{2}\}$  is not a singleton, so there must exist a label vector with a different weighted sum that is different than the true sum. A counterexample is below:

Example 8. Let  $\mathcal{G} = \{(y_1, y_2) \mapsto w_1y_1 + w_2y_1^2 + w_3y_2^2 + w_4y_2 | w_i \neq 0 \forall i \in [4]\}$ . Consider the true transition  $\sigma : (y_1, y_2) \mapsto y_1 - y_1^2 + y_2 - y_2^2$  from  $\mathcal{G}$  and a candidate transition  $\sigma' : (y_1, y_2) \mapsto y_1 - y_1^2 - y_2 + y_2^2$ . Then,  $\mathcal{G}$  is not unambiguous, since  $\sigma'(\pmb{y}) = \sigma(\pmb{y}) = 0$  for any  $\pmb{y} \in \{0,1\}^2$ . Namely, the partially labeled data are not informative enough to distinguish the label 0 from 1.

Bounded risk assumption. Similarly to Section 4, this unambiguity condition is necessary but not sufficient to show learnability. To show learnability, we additionally assume there is a constant  $r > 0$  such that  $\mathbb{P}([f](X) = y | Y = y) \geq r$  for each  $y \in \mathcal{V}$  and  $f \in \mathcal{F}$ . We refer to such an  $f$  as  $r$ -bounded. This assumption is slightly stronger than that of Section 4 as it additionally requires the classifiers to be not fully wrong for every possible label. The main learnability result of this section is below:

Theorem 5. If  $\mathcal{G}$  is unambiguous and any  $f\in \mathcal{F}$  is  $r$  -bounded, then we have:

$$
\mathcal {R} ^ {0 1} (f) \leq O \left(\mathcal {R} _ {\mathrm {P}} ^ {0 1} (f; \mathcal {G}) ^ {1 / M}\right) \quad a s \quad \mathcal {R} _ {\mathrm {P}} ^ {0 1} (f; \mathcal {G}) \rightarrow 0 \tag {10}
$$

Furthermore, suppose  $[\mathcal{F}]$  has a finite Natarajan dimension  $d_{[\mathcal{F}]}$  and the function class  $\{(y,s)\mapsto \mathbb{1}\{\sigma^{\prime}(\pmb {y})\neq s\} |\sigma^{\prime}\in \mathcal{G}\}$  has a finite VC-dimension  $d_{\mathcal{G}}$ . Then, for any  $\epsilon ,\delta \in (0,1)$ , there is a universal constant  $C_4$  such that with probability at least  $1 - \delta$ , the empirical partial risk minimizer with  $\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathsf{P}}^{01}(f;\sigma) = 0$  has a classification risk  $\mathcal{R}^{01}(f) < \epsilon$ , if

$$
m _ {\mathbb {P}} \geq C _ {4} \frac {c ^ {2 M - 2}}{r ^ {M} \epsilon^ {M}} \left(\left((d _ {[ \mathcal {F} ]} + d _ {\mathcal {G}}) \log (6 M (d _ {[ \mathcal {F} ]} + d _ {\mathcal {G}})) + d _ {[ \mathcal {F} ]} \log c\right) \log \left(\frac {c ^ {2 M - 2}}{r ^ {M} \epsilon^ {M}}\right) + \log \left(\frac {1}{\delta}\right)\right) \tag {11}
$$

Baselines. Discussion. The results confirm our theory: (i) we can learn classifiers under unknown transitions as per Theorem 5 (see the accuracy for DLog and NLog in Table 1); and (ii) learning gets harder when  $M$  increases as per Eq. (11) (see the accuracy for different  $M$ 's in Table 2). Surprisingly, for  $M = 2$ , weak supervision leads to better accuracy than SL even without pre-training and with fewer training samples: DLog(#4K) and NLog(#4K) lead to mean accuracy  $96\%$  and  $97\%$ , while SL(#4K) leads to mean accuracy  $93\%$ , see Table 1. This result does not invalidate Theorem 5 that requires  $f$  to be  $r$ -bounded: we prove learnability under distributions that concentrate mass on a single label- this assumption is invalidated for our weak training samples. Another observation is that the approximations in DLog-A (first row of Table 2) perform worse than the top-  $k$  one of NLog (last row of Table 2), suggesting that this simpler (w.r.t. to the approximation assumed in Theorem 2 and 3) top-  $k$  approximation can facilitate learning. We leave its theoretical analysis as part of future research. It is also worth exploring why NASP, ABL and ENT led to low accuracy<sup>4</sup>.

Table 2 shows that a key issue is scalability: the accuracy of the learned MNIST classifiers drastically drops for  $M = 4$  even under pre-training. The above suggests that it is critical to develop weakly supervised techniques for learning under unknown weights that can scale.

# 7 Related work

We provide an overview of the literature that closely relates to our work. A more comprehensive review of PLL, latent structural learning, and neuro-symbolic learning, is presented in Appendix F.

PLL. Learnability of PLL is typically shown under the small ambiguity assumption [9, 26, 4]. While it is technically possible to cast our problem to standard PLL by viewing a target  $s$  as a partial label for the hidden labels  $y$ , the small ambiguity condition is violated in our case since a distracting label can occur because  $\sigma$  is deterministic. Therefore, our proposed conditions relax and generalize the small ambiguity by allowing deterministic transitions, see Appendix E.3. Another line of work in weak supervision exploits the invertibility of transition matrices [43] to compute posterior class probabilities [6, 7, 55]. Our learnability condition,  $M$ -unambiguity, and the invertibility condition do not imply each other, see Appendix E.1 and E.2. There, we also show that small ambiguity and the invertibility condition do not imply each other, which might be of independent interest.

Neuro-symbolic and structured learning. We were motivated by frameworks that employ logic for training neural models [29, 48, 11, 53, 41, 30, 22, 25, 21]. We prove learnability under (unknown) transitions that capture different languages and error bounds under the SL [50] and top- $k$  approximations. Notice that [52] also proves consistency of a top- $k$  loss. However, they use a zero-one-style loss for standard supervised learning. Our work is also relevant to structured prediction [40, 34, 33, 31] To our knowledge, no formal learning guarantees have been given for that problem. Complementary to ours is the work in [17, 44, 32] that integrates combinatorial solvers into deep models.

# 8 Conclusions

We formulated multi-instance PLL and showed its connections with latent structural and neuro-symbolic learning. Our work exhibits a greater level of flexibility compared to (single-instance) PLL, as it allows for multiple instances and deterministic transitions. We introduced minimal distribution assumptions that enabled us to establish ERM-learnability and derive error bounds using the top- $k$ -semantics loss. Our findings suggest that the interaction of multiple instances and classifiers to form weak labels can facilitate learning. Although our proposed conditions are minimal and necessary, they can be restrictive, as the data distributions in real scenarios are typically not concentrated or adversarial. Therefore, for future work, we will assume a certain degree of smoothness for the data distributions. Other directions are settings where the hidden labels are non-i.i.d. and structured.
