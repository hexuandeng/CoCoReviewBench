# ABSTRACT

We study the optimisation problem associated with Gaussian process regression using squared loss. The most common approach to this problem is to apply an exact solver, such as conjugate gradient descent, either directly, or to a reduced-order version of the problem. Recently, driven by successes in deep learning, stochastic gradient descent has gained traction as an alternative. In this paper, we show that when done right—by which we mean using specific insights from the optimisation and kernel communities—this approach is highly effective. We thus introduce a particular stochastic dual gradient descent algorithm, that may be implemented with a few lines of code using any deep learning framework. We explain our design decisions by illustrating their advantage against alternatives with ablation studies and show that the new method is highly competitive. Our evaluations on standard regression benchmarks and a Bayesian optimisation task set our approach apart from preconditioned conjugate gradients, variational Gaussian process approximations, and a previous version of stochastic gradient descent for Gaussian processes. On a molecular binding affinity prediction task, our method places Gaussian process regression on par in terms of performance with state-of-the-art graph neural networks.

# 1 INTRODUCTION

Gaussian processes are a probabilistic framework for learning unknown functions. They are the de facto standard model of choice in areas like Bayesian optimisation, where uncertainty-aware decision making is required to gather data in an efficient manner. The main limitation of Gaussian process models is that computing their posterior mean or producing a posterior sample requires solving a large quadratic optimisation problem—minimising the kernel ridge regression objective—which, using direct methods, has a cost cubic in the number of observations. Standard approaches to reduce this cost typically apply approximations which reduce the complexity of the quadratic optimisation problem, such as Nystrom and related variational approximations (Williams and Seeger, 2000; Titsias, 2009; Hensman et al., 2013), or use carefully engineered iterative solvers, such as preconditioned conjugate gradients (Wang et al., 2019), or employ a combination of both (Rudi et al., 2017).

Alternatively, one can approach the problem using stochastic optimisation: Lin et al. (2023) and Dai et al. (2014) show that a variant of stochastic gradient descent outperforms preconditioned conjugate gradients whenever the data set is too large for convergence of the latter. Indeed, Lin et al. (2023) show that while stopping conjugate gradients prior to convergence may yield very poor results, the performance of stochastic gradient methods degrades gracefully, both in terms of the Gaussian process mean predictions and the associated error bar estimates. This makes stochastic optimisation methods attractive in large-scale or low-compute settings.

In this paper, we introduce stochastic dual descent, a stochastic gradient descent method based on insights from the kernel and optimisation communities, where the application of stochastic gradient descent to quadratic problems has been studied in depth. Stochastic dual descent is very simple, and may be implemented in a few lines of code using standard deep learning libraries. It combines (i) a

dual objective in place of the (primal) kernel ridge regression objective, (ii) stochastic approximation entirely via random subsampling of the data instead of random features, (iii) Nesterov's momentum, and (iv) geometric, rather than arithmetic, iterate averaging. Stochastic dual descent can be seen as an adaptation of the stochastic dual coordinate ascent (SDCA) algorithm of Shalev-Shwartz and Zhang (2013) to the large-scale deep-learning-type gradient descent framework, combined with insights on stochastic approximation from the theoretical work of Dieuleveut et al. (2017) and Varre et al. (2021).

As a result of the careful choices behind our method, we significantly improve upon the results of Lin et al. (2023) for stochastic gradient descent. For this, we present the following experimental evidence:

1. On standard UCI regression benchmarks with up to 2 million observations, stochastic dual descent either matches or improves upon the performance of standard Gaussian process baselines, including conjugate gradients and sparse variational Gaussian processes.  
2. On the large-scale Bayesian optimisation task considered by Lin et al. (2023), stochastic dual descent is shown to be superior to their stochastic gradient descent method and other baselines, both against the number of iterations and against wall-clock time.  
3. On a molecular binding affinity prediction task, where Gaussian processes have not previously been shown to be competitive with deep learning approaches, the performance of stochastic dual descent matches that of state-of-the-art graph neural networks.

In short, we show that a simple-but-well-designed stochastic gradient method for Gaussian processes can be very competitive with other approaches for Gaussian processes, and may make Gaussian processes competitive with graph neural networks on tasks on which the latter are state-of-the-art.

# 2 GAUSSIAN PROCESS REGRESSION

We consider Gaussian process regression over a domain  $\mathcal{X} \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ , assuming a Gaussian process prior associated with a continuous, bounded, positive-definite kernel function  $k: \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}$ . The goal is either (i) to produce a sample from the Gaussian process posterior given some observations or (ii) to compute the mean of such a posterior.

To formalise these goals, let some observed inputs  $x_{1},\ldots ,x_{n}\in \mathcal{X}$  be collected in a matrix  $X\in \mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$  and let  $y\in \mathbb{R}^n$  denote the corresponding observed real-valued targets. We say that a random function  $f_{n}\colon \mathcal{X}\to \mathbb{R}$  is a sample from the posterior Gaussian process associated with the kernel  $k$ , data set  $(X,y)$ , and likelihood variance parameter  $\lambda >0$ , if the distribution of any finite set of its marginals is jointly multivariate Gaussian, and the mean and covariance between the marginals of  $f_{n}$  at any  $a$  and  $a^\prime$  in  $\mathcal{X}$  are given by

$$
\begin{array}{l} m _ {n} (a) = \mathbb {E} [ f _ {n} (a) ] = k (a, X) (K + \lambda I) ^ {- 1} y \quad \text {a n d} \\ \operatorname {C o v} \left(f _ {n} (a), f _ {n} \left(a ^ {\prime}\right)\right) = k \left(a, a ^ {\prime}\right) - k (a, X) \left(K + \lambda I\right) ^ {- 1} k \left(X, a ^ {\prime}\right) \\ \end{array}
$$

respectively, where  $K \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$  is the  $n \times n$  matrix whose  $(i,j)$  entry is given by  $k(x_i,x_j)$ ,  $I$  is the  $n \times n$  identity matrix,  $k(a,X)$  is a row vector in  $\mathbb{R}^n$  with entries  $k(a,x_1),\ldots ,k(a,x_n)$  and  $k(X,a')$  is a column vector defined likewise (per the notation of Rasmussen and Williams, 2006).

For two vectors  $a, b \in \mathbb{R}^p$ , with  $p \in \mathbb{N}^+$ , we write  $a^\top b$  for their usual inner product and  $\|a\|$  for the 2-norm of  $a$ . For a symmetric positive semidefinite matrix  $M \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times p}$ , we write  $\|a\|_M$  for the  $M$ -weighted 2-norm of  $a$ , given by  $\|a\|_M^2 = a^\top Ma$ . For a symmetric matrix  $S$ , we write  $\|S\|$  for the operator norm of  $S$ , and  $\lambda_i(S)$  for the  $i$ th largest eigenvalue of  $S$ , such that  $\lambda_1(S) = \|S\|$ .

# 3 STOCHASTIC DUAL DESCENT FOR REGRESSION AND SAMPLING

We show our proposed algorithm, stochastic dual descent, in Algorithm 1. The algorithm can be used for two purposes: regression, where the goal is to produce a good approximation to the posterior mean function  $m_{n}$ , and sampling, that is, drawing a sample from the Gaussian process posterior.

The algorithm takes as input a kernel matrix  $K$ , the entries of which can be computed online using  $k$  and  $x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n}$  as needed, a likelihood variance  $\lambda > 0$ , and a target vector  $b \in \mathbb{R}^n$ . It produces a vector of coefficients  $\overline{\alpha}_{T} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ , which approximates

$$
\alpha^ {\star} (b) = \left(K + \lambda I\right) ^ {- 1} b. \tag {1}
$$

Algorithm 1 Stochastic dual descent for approximating  $\alpha^{\star}(b) = (K + \lambda I)^{-1}b$  
Require: Kernel matrix  $K$  with rows  $K_{1},\ldots ,K_{n}\in \mathbb{R}^{n}$  targets  $b\in \mathbb{R}^n$  likelihood variance  $\lambda >0$  number of steps  $T\in \mathbb{N}^{+}$  , batch size  $B\in \{1,\dots ,n\}$  , step size  $\beta >0$  momentum parameter  $\rho \in [0,1)$  averaging parameter  $r\in (0,1]$  1:  $v_{0} = 0;\alpha_{0} = 0;\overline{\alpha}_{0} = 0$  all in  $\mathbb{R}^n$    
2: for  $t\in \{1,\dots ,T\}$  do Sample  $\mathcal{I}_t = (i_1^t,\dots ,i_B^t)\sim \mathrm{Uniform}(\{1,\dots ,n\})$  independently random coordinates   
4:  $g_{t} = \frac{n}{B}\sum_{i\in \mathcal{I}_{t}}((K_{i} + \lambda e_{i})^{\mathrm{T}}(\alpha_{t - 1} + \rho v_{t - 1}) - b_{i})e_{i}$  gradient estimate   
5:  $v_{t} = \rho v_{t - 1} - \beta g_{t}$  velocity update   
6:  $\alpha_{t} = \alpha_{t - 1} + v_{t}$  parameter update   
7:  $\overline{\alpha}_{t} = r\alpha_{t} + (1 - r)\overline{\alpha}_{t - 1}$  iterate averaging   
8: return  $\overline{\alpha}_T$

Now, given a  $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}^n$ , let

$$
h _ {\alpha} (\cdot) = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \alpha_ {i} k (x _ {i}, \cdot).
$$

Then  $h_{\alpha^{*}(y)}$  gives the mean function  $m_{n}$ . Furthermore, choosing  $f_{0}$  from the Gaussian process prior associated with  $k$  and sampling  $\zeta \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\lambda I)$ , it follows that

$$
f _ {0} + h _ {\alpha^ {*} (y - (f _ {0} (X) + \zeta))}
$$

is a sample from the Gaussian process posterior; this approach is termed pathwise conditioning by Wilson et al. (2020, 2021). To produce  $f_{0}$ , we use random features (as done in Wilson et al., 2020, 2021; Lin et al., 2023).

In the following subsections, we examine and justify each of the choices behind the algorithm design, and illustrate these on the UCI data set POL (Dua and Graff, 2017)—chosen for its small size, which helps us to compare against more alternatives, including those less effective. Since what we present here has appeared in bits and pieces across multiple communities, we delay bibliographical information and comparisons until Section 3.4.

# 3.1 GRADIENT DESCENT: PRIMAL VERSUS DUAL OBJECTIVES

For any  $b \in \mathbb{R}^n$ , computing the vector  $\alpha^{\star}(b)$  of equation (1) directly, using naive Cholesky factorization, takes on the order of  $n^3$  operations. People familiar with regression estimation will, however, recognise that  $\alpha^{\star}(b)$  is the minimiser of the quadratic

$$
L (\alpha) = \frac {1}{2} \| b - K \alpha \| ^ {2} + \frac {\lambda}{2} \| \alpha \| _ {K} ^ {2}
$$

over  $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}^n$ . In the literature,  $L$  is known as the kernel ridge regression objective (Smola and Scholkopf, 1998); we call  $L$  the primal objective in the context of this work.

Consider using gradient descent to minimise  $L(\alpha)$ . This entails constructing a sequence  $(\alpha_{t})$  of elements in  $\mathbb{R}^n$ , which we initialise at the standard but otherwise arbitrary choice  $\alpha_0 = 0$ , and setting

$$
\alpha_ {t + 1} = \alpha_ {t} - \beta \nabla L (\alpha_ {t}),
$$

where  $\beta > 0$  is a step-size and  $\nabla L$  is the gradient function of  $L$ . The primal gradient and Hessian are

$$
\nabla L (\alpha) = K (\lambda \alpha - b + K \alpha) \quad \text {a n d} \quad \nabla^ {2} L (\alpha) = K (K + \lambda I) \tag {2}
$$

respectively. Recall that the speed at which  $\alpha_{t}$  approaches  $\alpha^{\star}(b)$  is determined by the condition number of the Hessian: the larger the condition number, the slower the convergence speed. The intuitive reason for this correspondence is that, to guarantee convergence, the step-size needs to scale inversely with the largest eigenvalue of the Hessian, while progress in the direction of an eigenvector underlying an eigenvalue is governed by the step-size multiplied with the corresponding eigenvalue. For the primal objective, the tight bounds on the relevant eigenvalues are

$$
0 \leq \lambda_ {n} (K (K + \lambda I)) \leq \lambda_ {1} (K (K + \lambda I)) \leq \kappa n (\kappa n + \lambda),
$$

where  $\kappa = \sup_{x\in \mathcal{X}}k(x,x)$  is finite by assumption. These bounds only allow for a step-size  $\beta$  on the order of  $(\kappa n(\kappa n + \lambda))^{-1}$  and, since they do not bound the minimum eigenvalue away from zero, we do not have a priori guarantees for the performance of gradient descent.

Consider, instead, minimising the dual objective

$$
L ^ {*} (\alpha) = \frac {1}{2} \| \alpha \| _ {K + \lambda I} ^ {2} - \alpha^ {\mathsf {T}} b.
$$

The dual  $L^{*}$  has the same unique minimiser as  $L$ , namely  $\alpha^{\star}(b)$ —indeed, we show in Appendix A that  $(L,\lambda L^{*})$  ares, in a certain sense, a dual pair of optimisation objectives; the factor of  $\lambda$  is immaterial. The dual gradient and Hessian are given by

$$
g (\alpha) := \nabla L ^ {*} (\alpha) = \lambda \alpha - b + K \alpha \quad \text {a n d} \quad \nabla^ {2} L ^ {*} (\alpha) = K + \lambda I. \tag {3}
$$

Observe that running gradient descent on the dual objective  $L^{*}$ , that is, using the gradient function  $g$  in place of  $\nabla L$ , we can use a step-size of up to an order of  $\kappa n$  higher than before. Moreover, since the condition number of the dual satisfies  $\mathrm{cond}(K + \lambda I) \leq 1 + \kappa n / \lambda$  and  $\lambda$  is positive, we have faster convergence, and can provide an a priori bound on the number of iterations required for any fixed error level for any length  $n$  sequence of observations.

To illustrate the discussion so far, we compare the progress of dual and the primal gradient descent on the UCI POL regression task, with results shown in Figure 1. There, for the step-sizes we tried, gradient descent with the primal objective was only stable up to  $\beta n = 0.1$ , and diverged for larger step-sizes. In contrast, gradient descent with the dual objective is stable with a step-size as much as  $500 \times$  higher, converges faster and to a better solution. We show this on three evaluation metrics: distance to  $\alpha^{\star}(y)$  measured in  $\| \cdot \|_K^2$ , the  $K$ -norm (squared) and in  $\| \cdot \|_{K^2}^2$ , the  $K^2$ -norm (squared), and test set root mean square error (RMSE). To understand the difference between the two norms, note that the  $K$ -norm error bounds the error of approximating  $h_{\alpha^{\star}(b)}$  uniformly. Indeed, as shown in Appendix A, we have the bound

$$
\left\| h _ {\alpha} - h _ {\alpha^ {*} (b)} \right\| _ {\infty} \leq \sqrt {\kappa} \left\| \alpha - \alpha^ {*} (b) \right\| _ {K} ^ {2}, \tag {4}
$$

where  $\kappa = \sup_{x\in \mathcal{X}}k(x,x)$ . Uniform norm guarantees of this type are crucial for sequential decision making tasks, such as Bayesian optimisation, where test input locations may be arbitrary. The  $K^2$ -norm metric, on the other hand, reflects training error. Examining the gradients, its immediate that the primal gradient optimises for the  $K^2$ -norm, while the dual for the  $K$ -norm. And indeed, we see in Figure 1 that when both methods use  $\beta n = 0.1$ , up to 70k iterations, the dual method is better on the  $K$ -norm metric and the primal on  $K^2$ . Later, the dual gradient method performs better on all metrics. This, too, is to be expected, as the minimum eigenvalue of the Hessian of the dual loss is higher than that of the primal loss.

two ways that use a noisy gradient estimate to reduce this cost to order  $n$  computations. Observe that this needs to be done carefully: when stochasticity is introduced into the gradient, more iterations are needed to average out the resulting noise. This increase in the number of iterations will depend on the magnitude of the noise and how this magnitude changes as  $\alpha$  approaches  $\alpha^{\star}(b)$ .

We show the two types of randomised gradient calculations for the dual gradient  $g(\alpha)$ . For the sake of exposition, we assume that we are in a  $m$ -dimensional (finite) linear model setting, such that  $K$  is of the form  $\sum_{j=1}^{m} z_j z_j^\top$ , where  $z_j \in \mathbb{R}^n$  collects the values of the  $j$ th feature of the  $n$  observations,  $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ . Then,  $K\alpha = \sum_{j=1}^{m} z_j z_j^\top \alpha$ . It follows that

$$
\widetilde {g} (\alpha) = \lambda \alpha - b + m z _ {j} z _ {j} ^ {\intercal} \alpha \quad \text {w h e r e} \quad j \sim \operatorname {U n i f o r m} (\{1, \dots , m \})
$$

gives an unbiased estimate of  $g(\alpha)$ , since  $\mathbb{E}[m z_j z_j^{\mathrm{T}}] = K$ . An alternative is to use

$$
\widehat {g} (\alpha) = n e _ {i} e _ {i} ^ {\mathsf {T}} g (\alpha) = n e _ {i} \left(\lambda \alpha_ {i} - b _ {i} + K _ {i} \alpha\right) \quad \text {w i t h} \quad i \sim \operatorname {U n i f o r m} (\{1, \dots , n \}).
$$

Thanks to  $\mathbb{E}[ne_i e_i^{\top}] = I$ , this is also an unbiased estimate of  $g(\alpha)$ . Note that the cost of calculating either  $\widetilde{g}(\alpha)$  or  $\widehat{g}(\alpha)$  is linear in  $n$ , achieving our goal of reduced computation time. Also, note that while  $\widetilde{g}(\alpha)$ , which we call the random feature estimate, is generally a dense vector,  $\widehat{g}(\alpha)$ , which we call the random coordinate estimate, is sparse. In particular, all but one coordinates of  $\widehat{g}(\alpha)$  are zero.

While so far  $\widetilde{g}$  and  $\widehat{g}$  may appear similarly appealing, the nature of the noise introduced by these, and thus their qualities, are quite different. In particular, one can show that

$$
\left\| \widehat {g} (\alpha) - g (\alpha) \right\| \leq \left\| (M - I) (K + \lambda I) \right\| \left\| \alpha - \alpha^ {\star} (b) \right\|,
$$

where  $M = n e_{i}e_{i}$  is the random coordinate approximation to the identity matrix. As such, the noise introduced by  $\widehat{g}(\alpha)$  is proportional to the distance between the current iterate  $\alpha$ , and the target  $\alpha^{\star}$ . For  $\widetilde{g}(\alpha)$ , letting  $\widetilde{K} = mz_jz_j^\top$ , we have

$$
\left\| \widetilde {g} (\alpha) - g (\alpha) \right\| = \left\| (\widetilde {K} - K) \alpha \right\|.
$$

As such, the error in  $\widetilde{g}(\alpha)$  is not reduced as  $\alpha$  approaches  $\alpha^{\star}(b)$ . In the optimisation literature,  $\widetilde{g}$  would be classed as an additive noise gradient oracle, whereas  $\widehat{g}$  as a multiplicative noise oracle (Dieuleveut et al., 2017). Intuitively, multiplicative noise oracles automatically reduce the amount of noise injected as the iterates get closer to their target. While harder to analyse, multiplicative noise oracles often yield better performance: see, for example, Varre et al. (2021).

When  $m$ , the number of features, is larger than  $n$ , individual features  $z_{j}$  may also be less informative than individual gradient coordinates  $e_i e_i^\top g(\alpha)$ . Thus, picking features uniformly at random, as in  $\widetilde{g}$ , may yield a poorer estimate. While this can be addressed by introducing an importance sampling distribution for the features  $z_{j}$  (per Li et al., 2019), this adds implementation complexity, and could likewise be used to improve the random coordinate estimate.

In Figure 2, we compare variants of stochastic dual descent with either random (Fourier) features or random coordinates. We see that random features, which produce high-variance additive noise, can only be used with very small step-sizes and have poor asymptotic performance. We test two versions of random coordinates:  $\widehat{g} (\alpha)$ , where, as presented, we subsample the whole gradient, and an alternative,  $n e_{i}e_{i}^{\top}(K\alpha) - y - \lambda \alpha$ , where only the  $K\alpha$  term is subsampled. While both are stable with much higher step-sizes than random features, the latter has worse asymptotic performance. This is a kind of Rao-Blackwellisation trap: introducing the known value of  $-y + \lambda \alpha$  in place of its estimate  $n e_{i}e_{i}^{\top}(-y + \lambda \alpha)$  destroys the multiplicative property of the noise, making things worse, not better.

There are many other possibilities for constructing randomised gradient estimates. For example, Dai et al. (2014) applied the random coordinate method on top of the random feature method, in an effort to further reduce the order  $n$  cost per iteration of gradient computation. However, based on the above discussion, we generally recommend estimates that produce multiplicative noise, and our randomised coordinate gradient estimates as the simplest of such estimates.

# 3.3 NESTEROV'S MOMENTUM AND POLYAK-RUPPERT ITERATE AVERAGING

Momentum, or acceleration, is a range of modifications to the usual gradient descent updates that aim to improve the rate of convergence, in particular with respect to its dependence on the curvature of the optimisation problem (Polyak, 1964). We use Nesterov's momentum (Nesterov, 1983), as adapted for deep learning by Sutskever et al. (2013), as this is readily available in standard deep learning libraries. Algorithm 1 gives the precise updates. We use a momentum of  $\rho = 0.9$  throughout. Comparing the plots in Figure 3, we see that momentum is vital on this problem, independently of iterate averaging.

When gradient updates are stochastic with additive noise and step-size is constant, the resulting iterates will bounce around the optimum rather than converging. In this setting, one can recover a convergent algorithm by arithmetically averaging the tail iterates, a procedure called Polyak-Ruppert averaging (Polyak, 1990; Ruppert, 1988; Polyak and Juditsky, 1992). While Polyak-Ruppert averaging is necessary with constant step-size and additive noise, it is not under multiplicative noise (Varre et al., 2021), and indeed can slow convergence. We recommend using geometric averaging instead, where we let  $\overline{\alpha}_0 = \alpha_0$  and, at each step, compute

$$
\bar {\alpha} _ {t} = r \alpha_ {t} + (1 - r) \bar {\alpha} _ {t - 1} \quad \text {f o r a n a v e r a g i n g p a r a m e t e r} \quad r \in (0, 1),
$$

and return  $\overline{\alpha}_T$ . Geometric averaging is an anytime approach, in that it does not rely on fixed averaging-window size, and thus can be used in combination with early stopping, and the value of  $r$  can be tuned adaptively. Here and throughout, we set  $r = 100 / T$ . Figure 3 shows that geometric averaging outperforms both arithmetic averaging, and simply returning the last iterate  $\alpha_T$  without any averaging.

# 3.4 CONNECTIONS TO LITERATURE

The dual formulation for the kernel ridge regression objective was first pointed out in the kernel literature in Saunders et al. (1998). It features in textbooks on the topic (see equations (10.95) and (10.98) in Smola and Scholkopf, 1998), albeit with no mention of its better conditioning. Gradient descent on the dual objective is equivalent to applying the kernel adatron update rule of Frie et al. (1998), stated there for the hinge loss, and to the kernel least-mean-square algorithm of Liu et al. (2008), analysed theoretically in Dieuleveut et al. (2017).

In the optimisation literature, Shalev-Shwartz and Zhang (2013) combine random coordinates with the dual objective within their stochastic dual coordinate ascent (SDCA) method. They show that SDCA reaches an  $\varepsilon$ -optimal solution of the scaled primal objective,  $L / n$ , in order  $n(1 + 1 / \lambda)\log (1 / \varepsilon)$  iterations, up to log-factors in  $n$  and  $1 / \lambda$ . This guarantee holds both for the last iterate and for an arithmetic average of iterates. In that work, the authors perform experiments with data sets of sizes 30k, 523k and 781k. They consider various classification losses, compare with ordinary stochastic gradient descent, and conclude that SDCA performs better. On the other hand, Tu et al. (2016) test SDCA on classification tasks, using a one-versus-all formulation with squared loss and a Gauss-Seidel solver. They conclude that SDCA is not effective in practice. Our method differs by using fixed step-size stochastic gradient descent with momentum in place of their exact line-search.

Within the Gaussian process literature, the most closely related work to ours is that of Lin et al. (2023) on stochastic gradient descent for Gaussian processes, which we henceforth refer to as SGD. Both SGD and our method, SDD, work with a fixed kernel. The comparison between these is as follows:

1. The SGD method optimises the primal objective, not the dual objective that we recommend.  
2. SGD approximates the primal gradient using a combination of random coordinates and random features, as

$$
\nabla L (\alpha) \approx n K _ {i} \left(K _ {i} ^ {\top} \alpha - b _ {i}\right) + \lambda \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {m} z _ {j} z _ {j} ^ {\top} \alpha , \tag {5}
$$

where  $i \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(\{1, \dots, n\})$  is a random index and  $\sum_{j=1}^{m} z_j z_j^{\top}$  is a random Fourier feature approximation of  $K$ . This restricts one to using a stationary kernel, and results in a combination of two additive noise estimates; see Section 3.2 and Figure 2 for details.

3. Like our method, SGD uses Nesterov's momentum and geometric iterate averaging, however SGD also uses gradient clipping. Because we use the better-conditioned dual gradient, we are able to use much larger step-sizes and do not need to clip gradients.

Similar stochastic gradient approaches were considered for kernel regression by Dai et al. (2014), who used the primal objective with random Fourier features, and for large-scale Bayesian linear models by Antorán et al. (2023), who use the dual objective. The dual objective was previously considered by Bo and Sminchisescu (2008), who use analytic updates on coordinates selected by an active learning procedure. Kivinen et al. (2004) use the primal objective with random sampling of training data (random coordinates, in our terminology) for online learning.

# 4.3 MOLECULE-PROTEIN BINDING AFFINITY PREDICTION FOR DRUG DISCOVERY

The binding affinity between a molecule and certain proteins is a widely used preliminary filter in drug discovery (Pinzi and Rastelli, 2019). While binding energy can be approximated using physics-based simulations, these may be expensive, which motivates using machine learning to quickly estimate their output (Yang et al., 2021). In this final experiment, we show that Gaussian processes with SDD are competitive with graph neural networks for binding affinity prediction.

We work with the DOCKSTRING regression benchmark of García-Ortegón et al. (2022), which contains five tasks, each corresponding to a different protein. The inputs are given by the graph structures of 250k candidate molecules, and the targets are real-valued affinity scores from the docking simulator AutoDock Vina (Trott and Olson, 2010). For each protein, we use a standard train-test splits of 210k and 40k molecules, respectively. We represent molecules using Morgan fingerprints of dimension 1024 (Rogers and Hahn, 2010), and use a Gaussian process model based on the Tanimoto kernel of Ralaivola et al. (2005) with the hyperparameters given in Tripp et al. (2023). For more details on our experimental setup, see Appendix B.3.

In Table 2, following García-Ortegón et al. (2022), we report  $R^2$  values. Alongside results for SDD and SGD, we inclue results from García-Ortegón et al. (2022) for XGBoost, and for two graph neural networks, MPNN (Gilmer et al., 2017) and Attentive FP (Xiong et al., 2019), the latter of which is the state-of-the-art for this task. We also include the results for SVGP reported by Tripp et al. (2023). These results show that SDD matches the performance of Attentive FP on the ESR2 and FP2 proteins, and comes close on the others. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time Gaussian processes have been shown to be competitive on a large-scale molecular prediction task.

# 5 CONCLUSION

We introduced stochastic dual descent, a specialised first-order stochastic optimisation algorithm for computing Gaussian process predictions and posterior samples. To design this algorithm, we combined a number of ideas from the optimisation literature with Gaussian-process-specific ablations, arriving at an algorithm which is simultaneously simple and matches or exceeds the performance of relevant baselines. We showed that stochastic dual descent yields strong performance on standard regression benchmarks and on a large-scale Bayesian optimisation benchmark, and matches the performance of state-of-the-art graph neural networks on a molecular binding affinity prediction task.
