# Abstract

While machine learning methods have demonstrated success on graph-structured data, many methods rely on fully-observed node features. This has led to an increase in research on imputing unknown or missing node features. However, in human networks, nodes belonging to a marginalized group have a disproportionate rate of unknown features. Combined with graph structure and known feature biases, this can cause graph feature imputation algorithms to predict values for unknown features that cause the feature values of the marginalized group to be more distinct on average from the feature values of the dominant group than they are in reality. We call this distinction the discrimination risk. We prove that a higher discrimination risk can amplify the unfairness of a machine learning model trained on the imputed data. We then formalize a general graph feature imputation framework called mean aggregation imputation and theoretically and empirically characterize graphs in which applying this framework can yield imputed features with a high discrimination risk. We propose a simple and effective solution to ensure mean aggregation-imputed features provably have a low discrimination risk (while minimally sacrificing utility) and improve the fairness of models. We evaluate the fairness and utility of our solution on synthetic and real-world credit network datasets.

# 1 Introduction

Machine learning methods [1, 2, 3, 4, 5] have been successfully applied to graph-structured data such as social networks, product graphs, and molecules [6, 7] to aid in important problems like content recommendation, product recommendation, and molecular property prediction [8, 9, 6, 10, 11]. However, many methods rely on fully-observed features for each node, which are not available for privacy reasons, as a consequence of exclusionary data collection practices, or due to the high expenses involved in feature annotation [12, 13, 14]. As a result, the development of algorithms to leverage a graph's structure and known node feature values to impute unknown or missing features has emerged as an important research area [15, 16, 17].

In human networks, nodes belonging to a marginalized group (e.g. on the basis of race, gender, disability, etc.) have a disproportionate rate of unknown features compared to the dominant group because marginalized communities are more reluctant to share their data, annotators erase their data, or they are sidelined in data collection [18, 19, 20, 21]. Furthermore, graph structure and node neighborhoods are correlated with group membership [22, 23], especially in homophilic graphs where nodes belonging to the same group have a higher likelihood of being connected [24]. This can be the result of social stratification [25] or the limited collection of inter-links, i.e. edges between nodes belonging to different groups [24, 6]. Moreover, known feature values are proxies for group membership [19, 26]. Hence, even if graph feature imputation algorithms do not have direct access to

the group membership of a node, these algorithms are influenced by unknown feature rate disparities, graph structure, and known feature values. Thus, they can predict values for unknown features that cause the feature values of the marginalized group to be more distinct on average from the feature values of the dominant group than they are in reality.

As an illustrative example, let's consider an automated candidate screening system based on the job applicant network shown in Figure 1, where nodes represent applicants and edges between applicants indicate that they have similar past work experiences. Each node has one feature: the number of years that the applicant has previously worked. Furthermore, each node belongs to one of two groups, the disabled community (group  $Q$ ) or the able-bodied community (group  $R$ ). (In reality, disability is a fluid and complex identity that cannot be easily reduced to a binary [27].) All the nodes in group  $R$  have a known feature value of 5 years, while all the nodes in group  $Q$  have unknown feature values; however, in reality, all the nodes in group  $Q$  also have a feature value of 5 years. Additionally, all the nodes in group  $Q$  are connected to each other but have few links (if any) to nodes in group  $R$  because of systemic barriers including hiring discrimination and a lack of accommodations [28]. Consequently, after applying the graph feature imputation algorithm Feature Propagation [17], because of the disparate rates of unknown features between the groups and structure of the graph, the nodes in  $Q$  will have, on average, feature values that are more distinct from the feature values of the nodes in  $R$  than the ground truth.

Figure 1: A job applicant network. After applying Feature Propagation [17], the nodes in  $Q$  will have feature values that are more distinct from the feature values of the nodes in  $R$  than the ground truth.

We call this distinction in imputed feature values between the marginalized and dominant groups the discrimination risk. In this paper, we present a theoretically-justified formulation of the discrimination risk of imputed features. We further prove that a higher discrimination risk can amplify the unfairness of a machine learning model (regardless of architecture) trained on the imputed data, which is especially dangerous in high-stakes applications like automated candidate screening and loan approval [29, 30, 31]. For instance, in the automated candidate screening example from above, a model trained on the imputed data may more easily learn to identify and reject disabled job applicants because, on average, they appear to have fewer years of work experience than able-bodied applicants; this would reinforce systemic discrimination against the disabled community [28].

We also formalize a general graph feature imputation framework called mean aggregation imputation that encompasses common, diffusion-based graph feature imputation algorithms in the literature. Subsequently, we theoretically and empirically characterize graphs in which applying mean aggregation feature imputation can yield a high discrimination risk, without making any assumptions about underlying distributions or models for unknown feature rates or graph structure. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to study the effect of graph feature imputation on the fairness of models. This is challenging because we must consider biases stemming from graph structure.

Furthermore, we propose a simple and effective solution to ensure mean aggregation-imputed features provably have a low discrimination risk (while minimally sacrificing utility) and improve the fairness of models. We do so by viewing mean aggregation imputation through the lens of gradient descent and projecting imputed features into the feasible space of features with low discrimination risk. We empirically evaluate the fairness and utility of our solution on synthetic and real-world datasets, finding that it improves fairness without significant loss in utility on the synthetic datasets but doesn't improve fairness on the real-world datasets. We close by discussing the limitations of our solution.

# 2 Related work

Feature imputation Feature imputation algorithms utilize known feature values to predict unknown feature values. For example, unknown feature values may be filled as the mean of known values [17]. However, more intricate feature imputation methods have been proposed in the machine

learning, statistics, and epidemiology literature, with popular approaches including matrix completion [32, 33, 34, 35], nearest neighbors [36, 37], multiple imputation via conditional models [38, 39, 40], and causal inference [41, 42]. Notably, while feature imputation may be applied to data with unknown feature values prior to the data being passed to a machine learning model, feature imputation is distinct from label prediction with missing data, wherein models work directly with unknown feature values [43, 44, 45, 46, 47, 48, 49, 50]; we do not consider the latter paradigm in this paper. Works have extended feature imputation approaches for tabular data to incorporate graph structure [51, 52]. Graph feature imputation using deep learning methods is also gaining traction [53, 54, 16]. However, recently, [17] proposed a diffusion-based approach to impute graph features that minimizes the graph's Dirichlet energy (Feature Propagation). In this paper, we focus on a broader class of mean aggregation feature imputation algorithms (formalized in Section 4).

Fairness of missing data and feature imputation Recently, works have theoretically and empirically investigated the impact of missing data [55, 56, 20, 57, 58] and feature imputation on the fairness of machine learning models [56, 59, 60, 61, 31]. These works consistently find that missing data can amplify algorithmic biases, and some show that in practice, feature imputation can yield less unfair (relative to excluding missing data) but nevertheless discriminatory model outcomes. However, these works only study tabular data and do not consider biases that emerge from graph structure [21, 23, 62]. Furthermore, they either do not provide concrete solutions, or they adapt models to directly work with missing data rather than mitigate the unfairness of feature imputation itself. Despite the prevalence of graph feature imputation methods, to the best of our knowledge, there is no research on their influence on the fairness of models that considers graph structure. Some works have explored fairness constraints in semi-supervised settings [63, 64], but they assume that node features are entirely available, which is not the case in feature imputation.

Fair graph machine learning We focus on group fairness, which ensures model predictions exhibit some form of parity between different groups [65, 22, 66, 30, 67, 68]. Works have studied statistical parity, wherein models predict the positive outcome at the same rate for different groups, and predictive parity, in which the accuracy of model predictions is equivalent across different groups [69]. While our theoretical study of discrimination risk is aligned with statistical parity, we empirically explore the effect of mean aggregation imputation with a lower discrimination risk on both the demographic parity and predictive parity of models. Mechanisms for improving group fairness have been categorized into pre-processing [23], training-time [22, 24, 70], and post-processing [71, 72]. Our work is similar to pre-processing, as we seek to lower the discrimination risk of imputed training data towards improving model fairness. Recently, there has been a surge of works on modifying graph structure to mitigate topology-induced biases [72, 73, 23, 70, 74, 75]. However, because graph semantics (especially for large graphs) are difficult to interpret, it is unclear if the solutions that these papers propose preserve the semantics of the original graph. On the other hand, our work does not modify graph structure and instead optimally transforms imputed features to have a low discrimination risk. Furthermore, some of these works theoretically characterize graphs with high structural bias [6, 76], but differ from our paper in that we analyze topology bias in the setting of feature imputation. [77] investigates the fairness of graph neural networks in the presence of limited group membership information but does not consider imputation; in contrast, our work assumes group membership is fully available but that features are not.

# 3 Discrimination risk and model unfairness

As machine learning is increasingly used in consequential applications like loan approval and automated candidate screening [29, 30, 31], ensuring its fairness is critical. To understand how feature imputation could amplify the unfairness of a machine learning model, we present a theoretically-justified metric called the discrimination risk, which applies beyond the setting of graphs and is agnostic to model architecture. We explore discrimination risk in the context of graphs in Section 5.

Suppose we have a data distribution  $\mathcal{D}$ . Furthermore, we have two groups: a marginalized group  $Q$  and a dominant group  $R$ . For any data instance  $(x,y,s) \sim \mathcal{D}$ , let  $x \in \mathcal{X}$  be the  $d$ -dimensional feature values of the instance (where  $x_{i}$  denotes the  $i$ -th entry of  $x$ ), let  $y \in \mathcal{Y}$  be the label of the instance, and let  $s \in \{Q,R\}$  be the group membership of the instance. We will assume that we can observe the group membership of any data instance and that no instance can belong to both the marginalized group and dominant group.

Definition 1 (Discrimination Risk) We define the discrimination risk of  $\mathcal{D}$  as:

$$
\mathcal {R} _ {\mathcal {D}} = \min  _ {i \in [ d ]} \left| \mathbb {E} _ {(x, y, s) \sim \mathcal {D}} [ x _ {i} | s = Q ] - \mathbb {E} _ {(x, y, s) \sim \mathcal {D}} [ x _ {i} | s = R ] \right|, \tag {1}
$$

where  $[d]$  is the set  $\{1,\dots ,d\}$ .

We now explore the relevance of discrimination risk to model unfairness. Let  $\mathcal{D}'$  be the data distribution with the ground-truth features and  $\mathcal{D}$  be the distribution with imputed features. We will show that if  $\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{D}} > \mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{D}'}$ , imputation may amplify model unfairness.

To be concrete, let's again consider the example of an automated candidate screening system. Let  $S \in \{Q, R\}$  be the marginal distribution over the group membership of job applicants. Furthermore, let  $X' \in \mathcal{X}$  be the marginal distribution over the ground-truth feature values of applicants (e.g. number of years previously worked, highest degree, etc.). In contrast, let  $X \in \mathcal{X}$  be the distribution over imputed feature values of applicants. Let  $Y \in \mathcal{Y}$  be the marginal distribution over applicant labels (e.g. whether an applicant should be hired, a screening score for an applicant, etc.). Now, let  $h'$  be a model trained on samples from  $\mathcal{D}'$ , and  $h$  be another model trained on samples from  $\mathcal{D}$ . Additionally, let  $Z' \in \mathcal{Y}$  be the distribution over the predictions of  $h'$  on instances sampled from  $X$ , and let  $Z \in \mathcal{Y}$  be the distribution over predictions of  $h$  on instances sampled from  $X$ .

We have the dependencies  $S \to X'$  and  $S \to X$  because disability affects the number of years previously worked by a job applicant.  $S \to X$  may be additionally necessary because  $S$  can influence the feature imputation algorithm. Furthermore, we assume that  $h'$  and  $h$  have access to the feature values of an applicant, but not the applicant's group membership, and that the association of  $S$  with  $Z'$  through  $Y$  can be fully explained by  $X'$ . Thus,  $Z'$  is conditionally independent of  $S$  given  $X'$ . Similarly, we assume that the association of  $S$  with  $Z$  through  $Y$  can be fully explained by  $X$ , so  $Z$  is conditionally independent of  $S$  given  $X$ .

Because we are interested in how feature imputation may amplify model unfairness, we investigate when the statistical parity or total variation distance  $d_{TV}(Z|S = Q,Z|S = R) > d_{TV}(Z'|S = Q,Z'|S = R)$  [78], where  $Z|S = Q$  is the conditional prediction distribution given group-  $Q$  membership.  $d_{TV}$  measures the distance between two probability distributions  $A$  and  $B$  as  $d_{TV}(A,B) = \sup_{x\in \mathcal{F}}|A(x) - B(x)|$ . Intuitively,  $d_{TV}(Z|S = Q,Z|S = R)$  captures how much the prediction distribution of  $h$  absolutely differs between  $Q$  and  $R$ , and thus it quantifies the (statistical parity) unfairness of  $h$ . We now discuss the relationships of  $d_{TV}(Z'|S = Q,Z'|S = R)$  to  $d_{TV}(X'|S = Q,X'|S = R)$  and  $d_{TV}(Z|S = Q,Z|S = R)$  to  $d_{TV}(X|S = Q,X|S = R)$ . We begin with the following lemma from [79].

Lemma 1 (Corollary 17 from [79]) By the Data Processing Inequality,  $d_{TV}(Z'|S = Q,Z'|S = R) \leq d_{TV}(X'|S = Q,X'|S = R)$  and  $d_{TV}(Z|S = Q,Z|S = R) \leq d_{TV}(X|S = Q,X|S = R)$ .

Please refer to Section A.1 for the proof of Lemma 1. At a high level, Lemma 1 states that the unfairness of any model is bounded above by the statistical parity distance of the features between the groups. We now use Lemma 1 to prove the following theorem that connects  $d_{TV}(Z'|S = Q,Z'|S = R)$  to  $\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{D}'}$  and  $d_{TV}(Z|S = Q,Z|S = R)$  to  $\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{D}}$ .

Theorem 1 Suppose  $X'$  and  $X$  are continuous and bounded on the region  $\mathcal{X} := \times_{i=1}^{d}[a_i - m_i, a + m_i]$ . Further suppose WLOG that  $f_{X'|S = Q}(x) - f_{X'|S = R}(x) \geq 0$  and  $f_{X|S = Q}(x) - f_{X|S = R}(x) \geq 0$  only on the region  $\mathcal{A} := \times_{i=1}^{d}[a_i - m_i, a_i]$ , where  $f_A$  is the density function of  $A$ . We then get the following bounds:

$$
d _ {T V} \left(Z ^ {\prime} \mid S = Q, Z ^ {\prime} \mid S = R\right) \leq \min  _ {i \in [ d ]} \frac {\mathcal {R} _ {\mathcal {D} ^ {\prime}}}{m _ {i}} + 1
$$

$$
d _ {T V} (Z | S = Q, Z | S = R) \leq \min  _ {i \in [ d ]} \frac {\mathcal {R} _ {\mathcal {D}}}{m _ {i}} + 1
$$

Please refer to Section A.2 for the proof of Theorem 1. This result suggests that minimizing  $\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{D}}$  can minimize the unfairness of the model trained on the imputed data. Furthermore, it is possible that  $\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{D}} > \mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{D}'}$ , in which case feature imputation may amplify the unfairness of a model.

While our definition of discrimination risk applies beyond the setting of graphs and is agnostic to the model architecture, in practice, it is important to consider model complexity and the context of

the task. We also add that our definition of discrimination risk bears resemblance to the Average Treatment Effect studied in causality [80]. Furthermore, for clarity, we note that [59] has previously proposed a metric also called discrimination risk which quantifies how much the deviation of imputed feature values from the ground-truth feature values differs across groups; this quantity is related to the predictive parity of a model trained on the imputed data. In contrast, our definition of discrimination risk simply measures how much imputed features differ across groups and is more aligned with the statistical parity of a model. Statistical parity and predictive parity are mutually exclusive, except in degenerate cases [19]. We leave extending our definition of discrimination risk to other notions of fairness for future work [69].

# 4 Overview of graph feature imputation

Prior to investigating the discrimination risk of graph feature imputation, we first present a general framework for mean aggregation feature imputation that encompasses common, diffusion-based graph feature imputation algorithms in the literature.

Suppose we have an undirected, possibly weighted homogeneous graph  $G = (V, E)$ . Each node has  $d$  features, hence the node feature matrix  $X \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$ , where  $N = |V|$ , the cardinality of the set. For simplicity, we will assume that  $d = 1$  in our analyses and extend our results to the case  $d > 1$  afterwards. Feature values are unknown for some nodes in  $G$  (denoted as the set  $U$ ), and known for others (denoted as the set  $K$ ). The feature value of each node is either known or unknown, i.e.  $U \cup K = V$  and  $U \cap K = \emptyset$ . Let  $X_S$  refer to the feature values of the nodes in set  $S$ . Assume without loss of generality that  $X = \begin{bmatrix} X_K \\ X_U \end{bmatrix}$ . Furthermore, let  $A \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$  denote the weighted adjacency matrix of  $G$ , where  $A_{ij}$  is the nonnegative weight corresponding to the edge from node  $j$  to node  $i$ . Additionally, let  $A_{S_1S_2}$  denote the submatrix of  $A$  with rows belonging to the nodes in set  $S_1$  and columns belonging to the nodes in set  $S_2$ . Let  $A := \begin{bmatrix} A_{KK} & A_{KU} \\ A_{UK} & A_{UU} \end{bmatrix}$ .  $D$  is the diagonal degree matrix, i.e.  $D_{ii} = \sum_{j=1}^{N} A_{ij}$  and  $D := \begin{bmatrix} D_K & 0 \\ 0 & D_U \end{bmatrix}$ .

Definition 2 (Mean Aggregation Feature Imputation) Denote the feature values at iteration  $t$  of mean aggregation feature imputation as  $X^{(t)}$ . Furthermore, let  $X_{S}^{(t)}$  refer to the feature values of the nodes in set  $S$  at iteration  $t$ . Then, at each iteration  $t$ :

$$
M X ^ {(t + 1)} := \phi (M X ^ {(t)}) = \left[ \begin{array}{c c} \beta I _ {| K |} & 0 \\ 0 & I _ {| U |} \end{array} \right] T M X ^ {(t)} + \left[ \begin{array}{c c} (1 - \beta) I _ {| K |} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{array} \right] M X ^ {(0)}, \tag {2}
$$

where  $M: \mathbb{R}^N \to \mathbb{R}^N$  is a diagonal invertible map,  $T \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$  is a right-stochastic matrix, and  $\beta \in [0,1]$  is a regularization hyperparameter.

We can see that mean aggregation feature imputation treats channels independently (i.e. does not support channel mixing), and only works with continuous (not discrete) features. Mean aggregation encompasses many common graph-agnostic and graph-aware feature imputation methods, including Global Mean (sets the unknown features to the uniform mean of all the known features), Neighbor Mean (sets the unknown features to the degree-weighted mean of the known features for neighboring nodes), Feature Propagation (predicts the unknown features to minimize the Dirichlet energy of the graph while preserving the known feature values), and Graph Regularization (predicts the unknown features via a smoothness constraint and a fitting constraint for the known features). For proofs, refer to Section A.3. Despite Neighbor Mean, Feature Propagation, and Graph Regularization being intended for homophilic graphs [17], mean aggregation feature imputation encompasses algorithms that could perform well on heterophilic graphs as well, with an appropriate choice of  $T$  [17]. We also note that  $T$  cannot be  $A$ , as this might cause feature values to explode over multiple iterations.

# 5 Discrimination risk of mean aggregation feature imputation

To better understand how mean aggregation feature imputation amplifies the unfairness of a machine learning model, we theoretically characterize graphs in which mean aggregation imputation increases

the discrimination risk, without making any assumptions about underlying distributions or models for unknown feature rates or graph structure.

We begin by defining new notation. In particular, we first focus on the case of a single feature (i.e.  $d = 1$ ), and extend our analysis to the case  $d > 1$  in Section A.6. Let  $X_{v}$  be the feature of a node  $v$ . Let  $Q$  denote the set of nodes that belong to the marginalized group, and  $R$  denote the set of nodes that belong to the dominant group. We will assume that  $Q \cup R = V$  and  $Q \cap R = \emptyset$ . We define the discrimination risk after  $t$  iterations of mean aggregation imputation as:

$$
\mathcal {R} ^ {(t)} := \left| \mathbb {E} _ {q \sim Q} \left[ X _ {q} ^ {(t)} \right] - \mathbb {E} _ {r \sim R} \left[ X _ {r} ^ {(t)} \right] \right| \tag {3}
$$

where the expectations are taken uniformly over the nodes in each set. Now, define  $\tilde{X} \coloneqq MX$ . Then, a modified version of the discrimination risk after  $t$  iterations of mean aggregation imputation is  $\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{(t)} \coloneqq \left|\mathbb{E}_{q \sim Q}\left[\tilde{X}_q^{(t)}\right] - \mathbb{E}_{r \sim R}\left[\tilde{X}_r^{(t)}\right]\right|$ . We now bound the discrimination risk  $\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{(t)}$  of the imputed features with respect to  $\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{(0)}$ . We bound  $\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{(t)}$  rather than  $\mathcal{R}^{(t)}$  for simplicity; however, we empirically validate that the bound properties also hold for  $\mathcal{R}^{(t)}$  in Section 7.

We begin with Theorem 2. Define  $\tilde{\mu}_Q^{(t)}\coloneqq \mathbb{E}_{q\sim Q}[\tilde{X}_q^{(t)}]$  and  $\tilde{\mu}_R^{(t)}\coloneqq \mathbb{E}_{r\sim R}[\tilde{X}_r^{(t)}]$ . Furthermore, let  $\tilde{\sigma}^{(t)}$  denote the maximal deviation of the feature values at iteration  $t$ , i.e.  $\forall q_1\in Q,|\tilde{X}_{q_1}^{(t)} - \tilde{\mu}_Q^{(t)}|\leq \max_{q_2\in Q}|\tilde{X}_{q_1}^{(t)} - \tilde{X}_{q_2}^{(t)}|\leq \tilde{\sigma}^{(t)}$  and  $\forall r_1\in R,|\tilde{X}_{r_1}^{(t)} - \tilde{\mu}_R^{(t)}|\leq \max_{r_2\in R}|\tilde{X}_{r_1}^{(t)} - \tilde{X}_{r_2}^{(t)}|\leq \tilde{\sigma}^{(t)}$ . Additionally, define  $T_{S_1\to S_2}\coloneqq \sum_{b\in S_2}\sum_{a\in S_1}T_{ba}$ .

Theorem 2 Let the contraction coefficient  $\alpha := \left|1 - \frac{T_{R\to Q\cap U} + \beta T_{R\to Q\cap K}}{|Q|} - \frac{T_{Q\to R\cap U} + \beta T_{Q\to R\cap K}}{|R|}\right|$ . Then,  $\alpha \leq 1$ , and:

$$
\max  \left\{\alpha^ {t} \tilde {\mathcal {R}} ^ {(0)} - 2 \left(\sum_ {j = 0} ^ {t - 1} \alpha^ {j}\right) \tilde {\sigma} ^ {(0)}, 0 \right\} \leq \tilde {\mathcal {R}} ^ {(t)} \leq \alpha^ {t} \tilde {\mathcal {R}} ^ {(0)} + 2 \left(\sum_ {j = 0} ^ {t - 1} \alpha^ {j}\right) \tilde {\sigma} ^ {(0)}
$$

$$
\alpha <   1 \Rightarrow \lim  _ {t \rightarrow \infty} \tilde {\mathcal {R}} ^ {(t)} \leq \frac {2 \tilde {\sigma} ^ {(0)}}{1 - \alpha}.
$$

Please refer to Section A.4 for a proof of Theorem 2. Theorem 2 shows that the bounds on the discrimination risk contract more slowly (with more iterations of mean aggregation feature imputation) with a larger  $\alpha$ . Furthermore, the upper bound on the discrimination risk is larger with a larger  $\alpha$  and may depend on the initial unknown node features. Theorem 2 allows for interesting interpretations of how graph properties like the rate of unknown features, group size, and graph structure affect the discrimination risk of mean aggregation feature imputation. In Section A.5, we successively vary each property (holding all other properties constant) and investigate its impact on  $\alpha$ , and in turn the discrimination risk. We find that a low unknown feature rate for both groups or disparate unknown feature rates across the groups and a low inter-link to intra-link ratio can increase  $\alpha$ , and thus the discrimination risk of mean aggregation-imputed features. Additionally, group size alone may not affect  $\alpha$  or the discrimination risk.

# 6 Fair graph feature imputation

We propose a simple and effective solution to ensure mean aggregation feature imputation provably has a low discrimination risk while minimally sacrificing utility. At a high level, we want to constrain the discrimination risk at every iteration  $t$  of imputation to be at most  $\epsilon$ , i.e.  $\forall t \in [0, \infty)$ ,  $\mathcal{R}^{(t)} \leq \epsilon$ . We do this by viewing mean aggregation imputation through a gradient descent lens and projecting imputed features into the feasible space of features with discrimination risk at most  $\epsilon$  at each iteration [81]. We focus on a single feature  $i$ , but our algorithms can be extended to all features by applying them to each feature separately.

We begin with the case where the known feature values remain fixed, i.e.  $\beta = 0$ . Recall the iterative algorithm for mean aggregation feature imputation when  $\beta = 0$ :

$$
M X ^ {(t + 1)} := \phi (M X ^ {(t)}) = \left[ \begin{array}{c c} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & I _ {| U |} \end{array} \right] T M X ^ {(t)} + \left[ \begin{array}{c c} I _ {| K |} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{array} \right] M X ^ {(0)}.
$$

We see that  $\forall t\in [0,\infty),X_K^{(t)} = X_K$ . Furthermore, define  $\Delta \coloneqq I_N - M^{-1}TM$ . In the case of Feature Propagation,  $\Delta = I_N - D^{\frac{1}{2}}(D^{-1}A)D^{-\frac{1}{2}} = I_N - D^{-\frac{1}{2}}AD^{-\frac{1}{2}}$  is the symmetric normalized Laplacian of  $G$  [17]. We see that  $X_U^{(t + 1)} = (I_N - \Delta)_{UU}X_U^{(t)} - \Delta_{UK}X_K$ . As discussed in [17], we can view the update  $X_U^{(t + 1)}\coloneqq X_U^{(t)} - \gamma (\Delta_{UU}X_U^{(t)} + \Delta_{UK}X_K)$  as an iteration of gradient descent (with step size  $\gamma = 1$ ) on the objective function  $\ell (x) = \frac{1}{2} x^{T}\Delta_{UU}x + X_{K}^{T}\Delta_{KU}x + \frac{1}{2} X_{K}^{T}\Delta_{KK}X_{K}$  where  $X_{K}$  is constant. For Feature Propagation,  $\ell$  is the Dirichlet energy of  $G$  [17]. We now present a theorem that shows how to perform mean aggregation feature imputation with a discrimination risk of at most  $\epsilon$  when the known feature values remain fixed.

Theorem 3 ( $\epsilon$ -Fair Imputation,  $\beta = 0$ ) Vanilla mean aggregation feature imputation updates  $X_U^{(t+1)} := X_U^{(t)} - \gamma (\Delta_{UU}X_U^{(t)} + \Delta_{UK}X_K) = Z_U^{(t)}$ , where  $\gamma = 1$ . Let  $\epsilon$ -fair mean aggregation feature imputation instead update  $X_U^{(t+1)} := P_WZ_U^{(t)} + P_B$ , where:

$$
\begin{array}{l} P _ {W} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} I _ {| U |}, & \mathcal {R} _ {K} - \epsilon \leq c ^ {T} Z _ {U} ^ {(t)} \leq \mathcal {R} _ {K} + \epsilon \\ I _ {| U |} - \frac {c c ^ {T}}{c ^ {T} c}, & \text {o t h e r w i s e} \end{array} , P _ {B} = \frac {c c ^ {T}}{c ^ {T} c} \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \mathcal {R} _ {K} - \epsilon , & c ^ {T} Z _ {U} ^ {(t)} <   \mathcal {R} _ {K} - \epsilon \\ \mathcal {R} _ {K} + \epsilon , & c ^ {T} Z _ {U} ^ {(t)} > \mathcal {R} _ {K} + \epsilon \\ 0, & \text {o t h e r w i s e} \end{array} \right. \right. \\ \mathcal {R} _ {K} = \frac {1}{| R |} \sum_ {r \in R \cap K} X _ {r} - \frac {1}{| Q |} \sum_ {q \in Q \cap K} X _ {q} \\ c \in \mathbb {R} ^ {| U |}, c ^ {T} Z _ {U} ^ {(t)} = \frac {1}{| Q |} \sum_ {q \in Q \cap U} Z _ {q} ^ {(t)} - \frac {1}{| R |} \sum_ {r \in R \cap U} Z _ {r} ^ {(t)} \\ \end{array}
$$

Then, assuming  $0 \leq \lambda_{min}(\Delta_{UU}) \leq \lambda_{max}(\Delta_{UU}) < 1$  (where  $\lambda_{min}$  and  $\lambda_{max}$ ) are the minimum and maximum eigenvalues, respectively): 1) a unique optimal (with respect to  $\ell$ ) feasible solution  $X_U^*$  exists; 2) for fixed step size  $\gamma = \frac{1}{\lambda_{max}(\Delta_{UU})}$ ,  $\epsilon$ -fair imputation converges as  $\| X_U^{(t)} - X_U^*\|_2^2 \leq \left(1 - \frac{\lambda_{min}(\Delta_{UU})}{\lambda_{max}(\Delta_{UU})}\right)^t \| X_U^{(0)} - X_U^*\|_2^2$ ; 3) for fixed step size  $\gamma = 1$ ,  $\epsilon$ -fair imputation converges to  $X_U^*$ .

Please refer to Section A.7 for a proof of Theorem 3. The convergence of our solution to the unique optimal (with respect to  $\ell$ ) feasible solution implies that our solution provably has a discrimination risk of at most  $\epsilon$  while minimally sacrificing utility. Furthermore, because our solution simply interleaves projections into the mean aggregation imputation framework, it preserves the framework's speed and scalability [17]. We similarly have a solution when  $\beta > 0$ , i.e. when the known node feature values do not remain fixed, which we present in Section A.8.

# 7 Experimental results and discussion

We empirically evaluate the fairness and utility of our solution on various mean aggregation feature imputation algorithms and synthetic and real-world datasets. We find that while our solution yields improved fairness without significant loss in utility on the synthetic datasets, there is not an improvement in fairness on the real-world datasets.

Datasets We construct undirected two-block synthetic networks (SBM) using StochasticBlockModelDataset from PyTorch Geometric [82] (where one block corresponds to the marginalized group  $Q$  and the other block to the dominant group  $R$ ) with various (relative) group sizes and inter- and intra-link rates (more information in Section B.1). SBM does not have a corresponding task, i.e. the nodes do not have labels. We also use the real-world Credit defaulter and German credit networks from [30]. The Credit defaulter network consists of 30,000 nodes representing individuals, with edges between them indicating similar spending and payment patterns. The corresponding task is to predict whether an individual will default on their credit card payment or not, and the groups are those 25 years old or younger and those above the age of 25 (more information in Section B.1). The German credit network comprises 1,000 nodes representing clients in a German bank who are connected if they have similar credit accounts. The corresponding task is to predict whether a client has good or bad credit risk, and the groups are men and women (more information in Section B.1). (In reality, gender is neither binary nor static, and treating it as such can produce harms [83].) We refrain from using the Recidivism graph from [30] so as not to support the development of carceral technology [84].

Protocols and performance evaluation By default, none of the datasets contain unknown or missing features. To simulate diverse scenarios with unknown features, for each group, we independently at random mark node feature values as unknown with a different probability. We experiment with all 25 combinations of unknown feature rates of  $\{0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9\}$  for the groups. Nodes, even within the same group, may have different unknown features. We choose this scheme to study the effect of disparate unknown feature rates across the groups, which reflects the real world [18, 19, 20, 21]. We encourage future empirical work on other unknown feature schemes, including missingness based on node degree or marking all or none of the features for each node as unknown.

To impute unknown features, we use the regular mean aggregation imputation algorithms overviewed in Section 4: Global Mean (GM), Neighbor Mean (NM), Feature Propagation (FP), and Graph Regularization (GR) (with  $\beta = 0.25$ ), and their  $\epsilon$ -fair counterparts (for  $\epsilon \in \{0.0, 0.025, 0.05\}$ ). For models, we use a simple linear classifier (linear), two-layer MLP (mlx), and Graph Convolutional Network (gcn) [2] (more information in Section B.3). We train all models on the imputed data, but validate and test on fully-observed data. Because there are no previous works (to the best of our knowledge) that directly address the unfairness of graph feature imputation, we do not have baselines against which to compare.

To evaluate the utility of imputed features for SBM, since we don't have labels, we employ relative reconstruction error RE [17]. For the real-world datasets, we consider the test accuracy (Acc) of models trained on the imputed data. To evaluate group fairness, we compute the discrimination risk (DR) of the imputed data. For SBM, we also train models on the imputed data to predict group membership and calculate the test accuracy of the models on identifying group membership (which we refer to as MI) [85, 22]. To evaluate group fairness for the real-world datasets, we use the test statistical parity (SP) of the models, defined as  $|P(Z = 1|S = Q) - P(Z = 1|S = R)|$  [78], and test predictive parity (PP), defined as  $|P(Z = 1|S = Q,Y = 1) - P(Z = 1|S = R,Y = 1)|$  [86]. Please refer to Section B.4 for more details on the metrics. For all metrics, we report the mean and standard error over 5 runs using different random seeds. On each run, a new dataset (in the case of SBM) is generated, new splits are created, and a new model is trained.

Q1. Does the contraction coefficient  $\alpha$  align with the discrimination risk of mean aggregation imputation across graphs with different properties? Figures 2 to 12 in the Appendix show that for SBM, discrimination risk and  $\alpha$  generally have a strong positive association over unknown feature rates, group sizes, and inter-link rate to intra-link rate ratios, which substantiates Theorem 2. This association is weaker for Global Mean and Neighbor Mean. Refer to Section B.5 for more details.

Q2. Does  $\epsilon$ -fair mean aggregation imputation (compared to regular mean aggregation imputation) improve the group fairness of a model trained on the imputed data? Table 1 shows that, for SBM,  $\epsilon$ -fair mean aggregation imputation achieves comparable reconstruction error to its regular counterpart while greatly reducing the discrimination risk and test group membership identification accuracy for all models. As expected, we see that the discrimination risk of  $\epsilon$ -fair imputation is at most  $\epsilon$ , and discrimination risk and test group membership identification accuracy are positively correlated, which substantiates Theorem 1. Furthermore, the reconstruction error for  $\epsilon$ -fair FP and GR (which leverage graph structure and are thus more susceptible to graph structural bias) are much lower than that of the naive  $\epsilon$ -fair GM (which is graph-agnostic), but fair FP and GR reduce the discrimination risk and test group membership identification accuracy for all models to similar levels as fair GM. However, the test group membership identification accuracy generally decreases less as  $\epsilon$  decreases for  $mlp$  and  $gcn$  than it does for linear, which suggests that minimizing the discrimination risk of imputed features is more effective at removing linearly-encoded group membership information than non-linearly encoded information. Furthermore,  $\epsilon$ -fair feature imputation does not guard against group membership information that  $gcn$  learns via graph structure during training. We have similar findings when averaging over different relative group sizes (refer to Table 3) and combinations of inter- and intra-link rates (refer to Table 4).

In contrast, Tables 2 and 6 (in the Appendix) show that, for the real-world datasets, regular mean aggregation imputation and its  $\epsilon$ -fair counterpart yield comparable test accuracy and statistical parity fairness for all models. In the case of Credit defaulter, our solution even appears to exacerbate the unfairness of  $gcn$ . We suspect this is because the datasets already produce models with low fairness, upon which it is difficult for our  $\epsilon$ -fairness solution to improve. We were unable to reproduce similar unfairness results to those in [30], even when all features are known. We find similar results for predictive parity, as shown in Tables 5 and 7.

# 8 Conclusion

We prove that a higher discrimination risk can amplify the unfairness of a machine learning model trained on imputed data. We formalize a general graph feature imputation framework called mean aggregation imputation and theoretically and empirically characterize graphs in which applying the framework can yield a high discrimination risk. We propose a simple and effective solution to ensure mean aggregation-imputed features provably have a low discrimination risk (while minimally sacrificing utility) and improve the fairness of models.

Our analysis and solution, like many fair machine learning algorithms, assume that groups are discrete and that group membership is known and static, which is not true in reality [81, 83, 27]. Furthermore, we don't consider fairness at the intersections of different groups [87, 88], and operationalizations of fairness beyond parity between two groups [89]; we encourage future work in these directions. Furthermore, while fairness is often framed as sufficient for the creation of ethical systems, this is not necessarily the case. For instance,  $\epsilon$ -fair mean aggregation feature imputation may be used to train a more fair model that diversifies news recommendations to social media users [6], but if this model recommends hostile or intolerant news sources to LGBTQIA+ users, this could lead to psychological harm [81]. We remind readers to carefully consider the ethics of downstream model use cases.
