# Abstract

Pre-trained large-scale models provide a transferable embedding, and they show promising performance on diverse downstream tasks. However, the analysis of learned embedding has not been explored well, and the transferability for cross-modal tasks can be improved. This paper provides a perspective to understand multi-modal embedding in terms of uniformity and alignment. We newly find that the representation learned by multi-modal learning models such as CLIP has two separated embedding spaces for each heterogeneous dataset with less alignment. Besides, there are unexplored large intermediate areas between the two modalities with less uniformity. As a result, lack of alignment and uniformity might restrict the robustness and transferability of the representation for the downstream task. To this end, we provide a new end-to-end fine-tuning method for robust representation that encourages better uniformity and alignment score. First, we propose a Geodesic Multi-Modal Mixup that mixes the representation of image and text to generate the hard negative samples on the hyperspherical embedding space. Second, we fine-tune the multi-modal model on hard negative samples as well as normal negatives and positive samples with contrastive loss. Through extensive experiments on retrieval, classification, and structure-awareness task, we demonstrate that our geodesic multi-modal Mixup learns a robust representation and provides improved performance on various downstream tasks.

# 1 Introduction

One of the challenging issues of machine learning is to increase the generalization performance on shifted dataset distribution as well as given dataset distribution. Learning given a limited dataset only may cause overfitting, and it has a limited generalization performance on the test dataset. An alternative method is to pre-train a large-scale model on a large-scale general dataset. Besides large-scale model architectures and datasets, learning methods have been known to be important for improving generalization performance. To this end, there have been many recent works that pre-train the uni-modal model for transferable representation with a contrastive learning approach [1, 2, 3].

A contrastive learning [4, 5] is a widely-used learning algorithm that effectively minimizes the distances between positive pairs while maximizing the dissimilarity between negative pairs. Due to its powerfulness in learning discriminative representation, contrastive learning has been actively adopted to pre-train the model and apply it for many computer vision applications.

It is considered as a de facto standard to demonstrate the generalization ability of a foundation model by fine-tuning and evaluating the model on multiple downstream tasks [7, 8]. However, such exhaustive bottom-up evaluation inherently includes a bias in tasks, which raises the need for metrics that can be quantitatively measured. For this purpose, the measurement of uniformity and alignment have been recently proposed [9, 10]. To be specific, given learned features on  $L^2$ -normalized hypersphere, the uniformity measures how the features are uniformly distributed, and the alignment measures how the features among the positive samples locate in close distances in the latent space [9, 10].

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first paper that analyzes the CLIP embedding and improves the embedding itself as well as the downstream task performance. We find that the learned embedding by CLIP has a bipartite structure with a large unexplored intermediate area between two modalities, while CLIP objectives are designed to explicitly align them together. Separated embedding with the largely unexplored area in CLIP has worse uniformity and alignment, and it might hinder the downstream tasks.

We observe that increasing temperature  $\tau$  in contrastive loss can improve the uniformity and alignment measure, but it simultaneously sacrifices the performance on downstream tasks. Therefore, it

raises an important question that motivates our work: "How can we improve the uniformity and alignment of learned feature distribution without sacrificing the performance on the target downstream task?" In order to answer this question, we propose a geodesic multi-modal-Mixup ( $m^2$ -Mix). As shown in Fig. 1,  $m^2$ -Mix mixes the embedding of the heterogeneous domain, image, and text.  $m^2$ -Mix contributes to robust representation learning in three perspectives. First,  $m^2$ -Mix generates the hard negative samples, and hard negative samples are known to be critical components for robustness [10]. Second,  $m^2$ -Mix interpolates the space between image and text that is not explored by pre-trained CLIP. Our  $m^2$ -Mix interpolates two samples from heterogeneous domains such as image and text to expand the exploration space. To ensure that the mixed dataset lies on the hypersphere,  $m^2$ -Mix interpolates on the geodesic. Our experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of our strategy for improving uniformity and alignment while improving the performance on various target downstream tasks. Third, a  $m^2$ -Mix generates the augmented dataset, and it also improves the indistribution performance as well as the calibration[11]. In summary, our contributions in this work are three-fold:

- We analyze the multi-modal embedding, and find that the embedding space of pre-trained CLIP consists of two separated embedding clusters, text-versa-image, and such representation have worse uniformity and alignment, making downstream tasks more difficult.  
- We propose a new fine-tuning method based on the  $m^2$ -Mix to encourage representation robustness. To the best of our knowledge, this paper is the first to propose the  $m^2$ -Mix that mixes the heterogeneous modality.  
- We empirically validate that  $m^2$ -Mix encourages uniformity and alignment, and it contributes to improving the performance of diverse downstream tasks.

# 2 Related Works

This paper presents a  $m^2$ -Mix, the multi-modal Mixup, for the first time. We verify the superiority of  $m^2$ -Mix on fine-tuning the multi-modal learning model with contrastive loss. In this section, we briefly introduce the previous related works for contrastive learning, Mixup, and multi-modal learning.

# 2.1 Contrastive Learning

Contrastive learning has been widely utilized for unsupervised representation learning or a pretraining on various domains [1, 12, 13, 14]. The goal of contrastive learning is to learn an embedding function that maps data into an embedding space so that semantically similar data have close embeddings under some distance metric. While any distance metric can be used, Cosine distance [1], or Euclidean distance [4] metric is usually used for simplicity.

Traditional contrastive learning adopts  $L^2$  regularization, and it makes the feature representations located onto the hypersphere. There are two key properties to analyze the hyperspherical feature embedding, so-called Uniformity and Alignment. Uniformity indicates the degree of preserving maximal information throughout the whole embedding space, while alignment denotes whether similar inputs have similar features. Better uniformity and alignment are interpreted as representation robustness, and it is related to the downstream task performance. It is known that the hard negative instance for constructing contrastive pair is necessary to increase the robustness of representation learning [15, 16, 10, 17].

Recently, multi-modal learning such as CLIP also incorporates contrastive learning and the  $L^2$  regularization. Different from uni-modal learning, multi-modal learning handles multiple heterogeneous

data such as images and text. However, the analysis for multi-modal learning has not been well studied yet. In this paper, we find that the CLIP embedding has limited uniformity and alignments, and it may harm the downstream tasks.

# 2.2 Data Augmentation and Mixup

There have been many works to improve the generalization performance when the number of data is limited. One of the promising approaches is to generate augmented samples to increase the possible training set. Traditional data augmentation such as rotation and crop [18] explores the vicinity or neighborhood of a given instance. To improve the effectiveness of data augmentation, Mixup [19] makes the new augmented samples by interpolating the data, and it corresponds to the generic vicinal distribution. Empirically, Mixup is helpful for robust representation learning, and it alleviates the overconfident problems and the failure of distribution shift settings as well as the in-distribution accuracy [20]. Besides empirical validation, there are related works to support the Mixup in theoretical viewpoints [21, 22]. Theoretically, cross-entropy loss with Mixup is known to have a term related to the calibration, Jacobian regularization, label smoothing, and input normalization [21].

With the success of Mixup, there are many works to introduce the Mixup for diverse domains such as vision [23, 24, 25, 26, 27], text [28, 29] and graph [30, 31]. Recently, It has been known that contrastive learning with Mixup helps to improving representation learning. Traditional contrastive learning annotates as 1 for positive pairs and 0 for negative pairs.  $i$ -Mix, Un-Mix and Matrix [32, 33, 34] interpolate the images with ratio  $\lambda$ , and they adopt contrastive loss with new pseudo labels according to the mixing ratio  $\lambda$ . Contrary to uni-modal cases, there are very few works on multi-modal cases [35]. STEMM [35] mixes the speech and text features to align them in shared embedding space. However, STEMM can not widely used for multi-modal learning because of its architecture-specific design, and we find that the uni-modal Mixup has limited effects on improving multi-modal learning such as CLIP. Therefore, we propose a new type of Mixup,  $m^2$ -Mix, to handle the multi-modal dataset and improve the robustness of multi-modal representation learning.

# 2.3 Multi-modal Learning

With the rapid development of technology, from various sources, multi-modal data such as images, texts, and videos are flooding the Internet. Thereafter, the universal intelligence system that can process the multi-modal data separately or simultaneously has attracted huge attention from both industry and academy. There are two branches of such works: (1) universal architecture for multimodal data flow [36, 37, 38], (2) representation learning under multi-modal interaction [6, 39, 40]. In this work, we focus on CLIP [6], one of the most popular multi-modal representation learning model.

Based on the learned multi-modal embedding space, CLIP has been widely utilized for image classification [41], video understanding [8, 42, 43], detection [44], and image generation [45]. CLIP also shows significant performance improvements on zero-shot transfer image classification. However, the zero-shot performance of CLIP is still less than the performance of supervised learning methods. Recently, fine-tuning methods to improve the performance of CLIP have been proposed. Wortsman et al. [46] and Kumar et al. [47] provide a robust fine-tuning methods that increase the both in-distribution and out-of-distribution generalization performance. Wortsman et al. provide the weight ensemble methods that combine the pre-trained and fine-tuned parameters with specific ratios. Besides, Kumar et al. [47] adopts a two-step strategy that combines the linear probing step and full fine-tuning step.

The previous works have focused on the fine-tuning on uni-modal dataset only, and the downstream task is also restricted to classification. On the other hand, we provide robust fine-tuning methods for multi-modal datasets as well as uni-modal datasets. Besides, we provide a new perspective to analyze the properties of multi-modal learning in terms of uncertainty and embedding arithmetic. There

is concurrent work that analyzes the CLIP's embedding space with initialization and optimization perspective [48].

# 3 Observations and Problem Definition

Multi-modal learning handles the multi-modal dataset stemming from a heterogeneous domain such as image and text. For given batch  $D = \{x_{i},y_{i}\}_{i = 1}^{n}$  where  $(x_{i},y_{i})$  denotes the  $i$ -th image-text positive pairs, and  $n$  is the number of batch size, the goal of multi-modal learning is to learn the relationship between the pairs of image and text. In other words, CLIP trains the image encoder  $f(\cdot |\theta_1)$  and text encoder  $g(\cdot |\theta_2)$  that  $I_{i} = f(x_{i})$  and  $T_{i} = g(y_{i})$  have similar embeddings as shown in Eq. 1, 2, where  $\theta_{1}$  and  $\theta_{2}$  are learnable parameters. It is noted that  $I_{i}$  and  $T_{i}$  are  $L^2$  normalized unit vectors, and they lie on the hyperspherical embedding space. CLIP adopts the InfoNCE [5] loss  $C(\cdot ,\cdot)$  to encourage the similarity between positive pairs  $(x_{i},y_{i})$  and the dissimilarity between all remain negative pairs  $(x_{i},y_{j})$ .

$$
C (I, T) = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} - \log \frac {\exp \left(\operatorname {s i m} \left(I _ {i} , T _ {i}\right) / \tau\right)}{\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} \exp \left(\operatorname {s i m} \left(I _ {i} , T _ {j}\right) / \tau\right)} \tag {1}
$$

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm {C L I P}} = \frac {1}{2} (C (I, T) + C (T, I)) \tag {2}
$$

$\operatorname{sim}(\cdot)$  calculate the similarity between two given vectors, and CLIP uses simple dot product as a  $\operatorname{sim}(\cdot)$  operation. It is noted that, unlike many previous studies,  $\tau$  is a learnable temperature that scales the magnitude of the calculated similarity.

The representation analysis for uni-modal learning has been widely studied, but the multi-modal representation analysis has not been well explored. We firstly analyze the multi-modal embedding in terms of alignment and uniformity, the well-known properties [9, 10]  $^{1}$ .

Alignment (align.) evaluates the difference between the similarity of positive pairs compared with the hardest negative pairs, while uniformity (unif.) denotes the degree of maximal information. It is noted that greater value of alignment and uniformity denote better representation [9, 10].

$$
\operatorname {a l i g n.} = - \mathbb {E} _ {\left(x _ {i}, y _ {i}\right)} \left[ \| f (x _ {i}) - g (y _ {i}) \| _ {2} ^ {2} - \min  _ {k \neq i} \| f (x _ {i}) - g (y _ {k}) \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right]
$$

$$
\operatorname {u n i f.} = - \log \mathbb {E} _ {\left(x _ {i}, y _ {j}\right)} \left[ \exp \left(- 2 \| f (x _ {i}) - g (y _ {j}) \| _ {2} ^ {2}\right) \right]
$$

We find that the learned embedding from CLIP is separated by two spaces, image embedding space, and text embedding space. Besides, there are wide unexplored regions between the two spaces. The CLIP embedding has worse uniformity and alignment, and these properties might harm the performance of downstream tasks. Fig. 2 denotes the DOSNES visualization [49] for pre-trained CLIP, fine-tuned CLIP, and fine-tuned CLIP with our method on Flickr30k dataset embedding. Fig. 2a and Fig. 2b denotes the CLIP zero-shot embedding, traditional fine-tuned embedding, respectively, and they have worse uniformity and alignments. Fig. 2c shows that fine-tuned embedding with our methods,  $m^2$ -Mix, resolves the issue, and they have better uniformity and alignment.

Our primary goal of this study is to learn robust multi-modal representation learning with  $m^2$ -Mix that generates the hard-negative samples. To validate the robustness, we perform diverse downstream tasks such as retrieval, calibration [11], embedding arithmetic, as well as uniformity and alignment score.

# 4 Methodology

# 4.1  $m^2$ -Mix and Hard Negative Samples

From our findings that the CLIP embedding is not robust enough, we improve the CLIP embedding by fine-tuning the CLIP with contrastive loss and hard negative samples. First, we generate the hard negative samples by mixing the image embedding and text embedding from CLIP,  $m^2$ -Mix. The CLIP embeddings lie on the hypersphere, and the mixed inputs are required to lie on the hypersphere. However, traditional Mixup [19] does not guarantee that the mixed datasets lie on the hypersphere. Therefore, we propose a new type of Mixup, the geodesic Mixup. The geodesic Mixup interpolates on the geodesic, and it guarantees that the mixed inputs lie on the hypersphere. The mixing ratio  $\lambda$  is sampled from the Beta distribution with hyperparameters  $\alpha$  and  $\beta$ .

$$
m i x _ {\lambda} (a, b) = a \frac {\sin (\lambda \theta)}{\sin (\theta)} + b \frac {\sin ((1 - \lambda) \theta)}{\sin (\theta)} \tag {3}
$$

$$
w h e r e \theta = \cos^ {- 1} (a \cdot b) a n d \lambda \sim \operatorname {B e t a} (\alpha , \beta)
$$

Second, we adopt the hard negative samples as a negative instance for contrastive loss. As shown in Eq. 4 and 5, we replace the original negative with our generated hard negative samples. It is noted that we only change the denominator terms and remain the numerator term that compares the similarity between the positive pairs. For the simplicity, we omit the temperature  $\tau$  in all subsequent formulas.

$$
C _ {m ^ {2}} (I, T) = - \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log \frac {\exp \left(I _ {i} \cdot T _ {i}\right)}{\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} \exp \left(I _ {i} \cdot m i x _ {\lambda} \left(I _ {j} , T _ {j}\right)\right)} \tag {4}
$$

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {m ^ {2} - \operatorname {M i x}} = \frac {1}{2} \left(C _ {m ^ {2}} (I, T) + C _ {m ^ {2}} (T, I)\right) \tag {5}
$$

$m^2$ -Mix generates the virtual hard negative samples compared with the original negative samples. From our findings, CLIP has two separated embeddings, and mixing separated embeddings, text, and image, becomes a hard negative sample. Therefore, the similarity between the original image

embedding and the mixed embedding is larger than the similarity between the original image embedding and original text embedding. In other words, when we mix the image and text pair, the mixed embedding becomes hard negative samples for other instances. The existence of hard negative samples is known to be important in uni-modal contrastive learning [9, 10], and we validate it in multi-modal contrastive learning.

We provide the theoretical analysis that  $m^2$ -Mix generates the hard negative samples compared with the original negative samples in Theorem 1. Theorem 1 indicates that the KL-divergence between the pair of a given image and generated hard negative samples is less (i.e., more confused with the positive one) than that of the original negative.

Theorem 1. Let's assume that two random variables  $x_{1}$  and  $x_{2}$  follow the  $M_{d}(\mu_{1},\kappa)$  and  $M_{d}(\mu_{2},\kappa)$ , von Mises-Fisher distribution with mean direction  $\mu_{1},\mu_{2}$  and concentration parameter  $\kappa$  in  $\mathbb{R}^d$ , respectively. Let  $\widetilde{x} = x_{1} + x_{2}$  and  $d = 2$ . Then,  $\mathrm{KL}(p(x_1)||p(\widetilde{x}))\leq \mathrm{KL}(p(x_1)||p(x_2))$  for sufficiently large  $\kappa$ .

Proof. Let  $M_d(\mu, \kappa) = C_d(\kappa)\exp(\kappa\mu^T x)$ , where  $I$  denotes the modified Bessel function, and  $C_d(\kappa) = \frac{\kappa^{d/2-1}}{(2\pi)^{(d/2)}I_{d/2-1}(\kappa)}$ . Let  $x_1 \sim M_2(\mu_1, \kappa)$  and  $x_2 \sim M_2(\mu_1, \kappa)$ . From [51],  $\widetilde{x} \sim M_2(\widetilde{\mu}, \widetilde{\kappa})$  where  $\widetilde{\mu} = \mu_1 + \mu_2$  and  $\widetilde{\kappa} = A^{-1}(A(\kappa)A(\kappa))$ , approximately, where  $A(\kappa) = \frac{I_1(\kappa)}{I_0(\kappa)}$  and  $A^{-1}$  is its inverse. From [52],  $KL(p(x_1)||p(x_2)) = \kappa A(\kappa)(1 - \cos(\mu_1 - \mu_2))$ . Similarly,  $KL(p(x_1)||p(\widetilde{x})) = \log I_0(\widetilde{\kappa}) - \log I_0(\kappa) + \widetilde{\kappa}A(\widetilde{\kappa})(1 - \cos(\mu_1 - \mu_2))$ . From [53] and [54],  $I_0(\kappa) \approx \frac{\exp(\kappa)}{\sqrt{2\pi\kappa}}(1 + \frac{1}{8\kappa})$  and  $I_1(\kappa) \approx \frac{\exp(\kappa)}{\sqrt{2\pi\kappa}}(1 - \frac{3}{8\kappa})$  for sufficiently large  $\kappa$ .

Therefore,  $\mathrm{KL}(p(x_1)||p(\widetilde{x}))\leq \mathrm{KL}(p(x_1)||p(x_2))$  for sufficiently large  $\kappa$ .

Our  $m^2$ -Mix-based contrastive learning has two advantages. First, contrastive learning with hard negative samples encourages maximizing the similarity between positive pairs more strongly. To minimize the contrastive loss, the similarity between positive pairs should be sufficiently larger than that of negative pairs. The harder negative samples make the similarity between negative pairs greater resulting in hard-to-discriminate with positive pairs, so the contrastive learning induces strong alignments between positive pairs.

Second, our  $m^2$ -Mix interpolates the intermediate region of image and text embedding spaces. The intermediate region is not well explored in CLIP embedding, and it might be problematic when CLIP handles the new data located in the intermediate area. On the other hand, CLIP with  $m^2$ -Mix has no unexplored intermediate area, and it makes CLIP perform well on uncertainty estimation as well as retrieval and structure-awareness tasks [55].

# 4.2 Uni-modal Mixup for CLIP

Our proposed method,  $m^2$ -Mix generates the hard negative samples to better aligned and uniformed embedding. Another advantage of Mixup is alleviating the over-confident problems in uni-modal settings. As variants of  $m^2$ -Mix, we propose a uni-modal Mixup, Visual-Mix (V-Mix), Language-Mix (L-Mix), and Visual and Language Mix (VL-Mix) for multi-modal learning. The Fig. 3 denotes the overall structure of our models. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first paper that provides the Mixup for CLIP.

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {\text {u n i - M i x}} = \frac {1}{2} \left(C _ {V} (I, T) + C _ {L} (I, T)\right)

# 4.2.2 VL-Mix

For pair-wise similarity contrast,  $V$ -Mix and  $L$ -Mix only mixes the image embedding and text embedding, respectively. In this section, we propose a  $VL$ -Mix that mixes the image and test embedding simultaneously. It is noted that  $m^2$ -Mix mixes the image and text, while  $VL$ -Mix mixes the images and text independently. Both  $mix_{\lambda}(I_i, I_{i'})$  and  $mix_{\lambda}(T_i, T_{i'})$  has  $i$ -th component and  $i'$ -th component with fraction  $\lambda$  and  $1 - \lambda$  respectively, the pseudo label for the pair  $(mix_{\lambda}(I_i, I_{i'}))$ ,

$mix_{\lambda}(T_i, T_{i'})$  is 1.

$$
C _ {V L} (I, T) = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} - \log \frac {\exp (m i x _ {\lambda} (I _ {i} , I _ {i ^ {\prime}}) \cdot m i x _ {\lambda} (T _ {i} , T _ {i ^ {\prime}}))}{\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} \exp (I _ {i} \cdot T _ {j}))}
$$

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {V L - \mathrm {M i x}} = \frac {1}{2} (C _ {V L} (I, T) + C _ {V L} (T, I))
$$

# 4.2.3  $m^3$ -Mix

We find that our uni-modal Mixup and  $VL$ -Mixup on CLIP alleviate the over-confident problems as well as the  $m^2$ -Mix. We name the combination of  $m^2$ -Mix with uni-Mix and  $VL$ -mix as  $m^3$ -Mix, multiple multi-modal Mixup. The overall objective function of  $m^3$ -Mix is as follows.

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {m ^ {3} - \text {M i x}} = \mathcal {L} _ {\text {C L I P}} + \mathcal {L} _ {m ^ {2} - \text {M i x}} + \mathcal {L} _ {\text {u n i} - \text {M i x}} + \mathcal {L} _ {\text {V L} - \text {M i x}}
$$

# 5 Results

In this study, we find that CLIP embedding has a large gap between image and text embedding. From our findings, we provide a  $m^2$ -Mix that generates the hard negative samples for robust representation learning. Section 5.2. provides the properties of generated samples, and Section 5.3-5.5 provide the extensive experiments including retrieval, calibration, structure-awareness [55], and robustness on modality missing in sentiment analysis (emotion classification) task [56]. # 5.1 Experimental Settings

Model Description For retrieval, calibration, and structure awareness task, we use ViT-B/32 version of OpenAI's<sup>3</sup> CLIP [6] model except for uni-modal pre-training experiment (Section 5.4). Our model uses Vision Transformer with minor modification (additional layer normalization) for image encoder, and Transformer with 63M Parameter 12 layer 512-wide model with 8 attention heads for text encoder. Moreover, CLIP's pre-trained learnable temperature parameter is set to 0.01. We use resize, center crop, and mean-standard Normalization for image preprocessing and 49152 vocabulary size of lowercase Byte Pair Encoding (BPE) for texts. It is noted that this is exactly the same model architecture and preprocessing strategy as the original CLIP paper. We also add "a photo of" prompt in front of each text. For sentiment analysis on video clips with missing modality, we use the same model architectures (such as GRU [57] and Transformer [58]) released<sup>4</sup> by [56].

Dataset To train image-text contrastive model, We use Flickr30k and MS COCO Dataset. Flickr30k dataset contains 30K image-text pair for training data<sup>5</sup>, 1k for validation and test data<sup>6</sup>. For MS COCO Dataset, We use 2017 version of MS COCO Dataset<sup>7</sup>. MS COCO dataset contains 118k image-text pair for training data, 5k for validation, and test data. Also, MS COCO dataset has five captions for each image data. We always select the first caption to create image-text pair. To evaluate the structure awareness task performance of our method, we use SIMAT dataset[55]. SIMAT dataset is created for evaluating text-driven image transformation performance. SIMAT contains 6k images

and 18k "transformation queries" that have pair of (source image, transformation of word, target image). The goal of SIMAT is to retrieve the same image with ground truth target image by (source image)  $+\Delta$  (transformation of word). To evaluate the multi-modal representation learning capability for video classification, we adopt CMU-MOSEI [59], a popular benchmark dataset for multi-modal sentiment analysis. CMU-MOSEI consists of three modalities (T:textual, V:visual and A:audio) and contains 23,453 YouTube video clips about diverse movie reviews, and each clip is annotated with ordinal labels in the range from -3 to 3, where -3 and 3 denote strong negative and strong positive sentiment scores, respectively.

Hyperparameters To fine-tune CLIP, We follow almost the same training setting reported in the original CLIP paper. We use AdamW Optimizer with  $\beta_{1} = 0.9$ ,  $\beta_{2} = 0.98$ ,  $\epsilon = 1\mathrm{e - }6$  and train the model by turning on eval mode, which is known for showing good performance on stabilizing CLIP training in community. Also, We train CLIP model by FP16 precision to accelerate training, reduce memory overhead and use larger batch size. We use one A6000 GPU Machine for each training, and it takes 30 minutes for training on Flickr30k and 50 minutes for training on MS COCO.  $\alpha$  is a parameter of Beta distribution for mixing coefficient  $\lambda \sim \mathrm{Beta}(\alpha ,\alpha)$ . We provide the detailed hyperparameters on Appendix C

# 5.6 Multi-modal Structure-Awareness

We expect that multi-modal embedding represents the structure relationship between instances like word vectors [65]. We validate representation learned by our methods on SIMAT dataset [55].

SIMAT evaluates the text-driven image representation by retrieving the new images which has the highest cosine similarity with the latent vector  $x$ , which is transformed by text delta vectors when we change the word in original text. The formulation is below:

$$
x = I _ {\text {o r i g i n a l}} + \lambda \cdot \left(T _ {\text {n e w}} - T _ {\text {o r i g i n a l}}\right)
$$

It is noted that  $I$  and  $T$  are image and text embedding vector, respectively, and  $\lambda$  is a hyper-parameter for strength of transformation.

# 6 Conclusion

This paper analyzes the representation learned from multi-modal learning with contrastive loss. We find that the CLIP has separated text embedding space and image embedding space with an unexplored large intermediate area. These polarized embedding with the largely unexplored area limits the robustness and transferability of CLIP representation for diverse downstream tasks. From our findings, we propose  $m^2$ -Mix and  $m^3$ -Mix that generates the hard-negative samples by mixing the heterogeneous representation, image, and text. We validate that our  $m^2$ -Mix and  $m^3$ -Mix representation learning induces the robust representation that contributes to improving the diverse downstream task performance while it preserves the rich structured multi-modal relationships.
