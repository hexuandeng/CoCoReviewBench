# Abstract

Given a sample of size  $N$ , it is often useful to select a subsample of smaller size  $n < N$  to be used for statistical estimation or learning. Such a data selection step is useful to reduce the requirements of data labeling and the computational complexity of learning. We assume to be given  $N$  unlabeled samples  $\{\pmb{x}_i\}_{i \leq N}$ , and to be given access to a 'surrogate model' that can predict labels  $y_i$  better than random guessing. Our goal is to select a subset of the samples, to be denoted by  $\{\pmb{x}_i\}_{i \in G}$ , of size  $|G| = n < N$ . We then acquire labels for this set and we use them to train a model via regularized empirical risk minimization.

By using a mixture of numerical experiments on real and synthetic data, and mathematical derivations under low- and high-dimensional asymptotics, we show that: (i) Data selection can be very effective, in particular beating training on the full sample in some cases; (ii) Certain popular choices in data selection methods (e.g. unbiased reweighted subsampling, or influence function-based subsampling) can be substantially suboptimal.

# 1 Introduction

Labeling is a notoriously laborious task in machine learning. A possible approach towards reducing this burden is to identify a small subset of training samples that are most valuable for training. To be concrete, consider the standard supervised learning setting in which the whole data consist of  $N$  feature vector-label pairs  $(\pmb{x}_i, y_i) \in \mathbb{R}^p \times \mathbb{R}$ ,  $i \leq N$ , but now assume that the labels  $y_i$  are not observed. We would like to label only the  $n$  most important vectors in this sample.

More explicitly, data selection-based learning consists of two steps:

1. Data selection. Given feature vectors  $\mathbf{X} \coloneqq (\pmb{x}_i)_{i \leq N}$ , select a subset  $G \subseteq [N]$  of size  $n$  (or close to  $n$ ).  
2. Training. Having acquired labels for the selected subset  $\{y_i\}_{i \in G}$ , train a model  $f(\cdot; \pmb{\theta}) : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}$  (with parameters  $\pmb{\theta}$ ) on the labeled data  $\{(\pmb{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i \in G}$ .

Throughout this paper we will focus on methods with the following structure. In the first step, set  $G$  is generated by selecting each datapoint  $i$  independently with probability  $\pi_i = \pi (\pmb {x}_i)$ . Namely

$$
\mathbb {P} (i \in G | \boldsymbol {y}, \boldsymbol {X}) = \pi_ {i}, \quad \text {i n d e p e n d e n t l y} i \leq N. \tag {1.1}
$$

The second step (training) is carried out by (weighted) empirical risk minimization (ERM) over the selected set  $G$ . Namely, given loss function  $\ell : \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$  and regularizer  $\Omega : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}$ , we solve

$$
\hat {\boldsymbol {\theta}} = \arg \min  _ {\boldsymbol {\theta}} \hat {R} _ {N} (\boldsymbol {\theta}), \tag {1.2}
$$

$$
\hat {R} _ {N} (\boldsymbol {\theta}) := \frac {1}{N} \sum_ {i \in G} w _ {i} \ell \left(y _ {i}, f \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {i}; \boldsymbol {\theta}\right)\right) + \lambda \Omega (\boldsymbol {\theta}). \tag {1.3}
$$

The weights  $w_{i}$  can depend on the feature vectors, and are designed as to reduce the estimation or prediction error. In particular, a popular choice is  $w_{i} = \mathrm{const} / \pi_{i}$  because this implies that  $\hat{R}_N(\pmb{\theta})$  is an unbiased version of the full empirical risk (corresponding to  $\pi_{i} = 1$  for all  $i$ ).

Throughout this paper, we will assume data  $(\pmb{x}_i,y_i)$  to be i.i.d. samples from a common distribution  $\mathbb{P}\in \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^d\times \mathbb{R})$  and will evaluate a data selection procedure via the resulting test error.

$$
R _ {\text {t e s t}} (\boldsymbol {\theta}) = \mathbb {E} \ell_ {\text {t e s t}} \left(y _ {\text {n e w}}; f \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {\text {n e w}}; \boldsymbol {\theta}\right)\right), \tag {1.4}
$$

where expectation is taken with respect to the test sample  $(\pmb{x}_{\mathrm{new}},y_{\mathrm{new}})\sim \mathbb{P}$ . We allow the test loss  $\ell_{\mathrm{test}}$  to be different from the training loss  $\ell$ . We will denote the (training) population risk by  $R(\pmb{\theta}) = \mathbb{E}\ell \big(y;f(\pmb{x};\pmb{\theta})\big)$ .

Given its practical utility, data selection has been extensively studied in a variety of settings, including experimental design, active learning, learning algorithms and data optimization. While some heuristics (e.g., focus on samples that are most difficult to predict) have resurfaced from

different viewpoints, their implementation and effectiveness depends on the problem formulation. In particular, unlike the most common active learning scenario, it is important that we assume to be given a fixed data sample  $\{\pmb{x}_i\}_{i\leq N}$  (without labels) and carry out a single data selection step. (This is sometime referred to as 'pool-based active learning' [Set12].)

Before summarizing our contributions, it is useful to mention some of the existing approaches.

Bayesian methods. Within a Bayesian setting, one can quantify uncertainty about  $\pmb{\theta}$  by using its conditional entropy given the data, and is therefore natural to select the subsample  $G$  as to minimize this conditional entropy. This is a first example of "uncertainty sampling." It was introduced in the seminal work of Lindley [Lin56] and developed in a high-dimensional setting by Seung, Opper, Sompolinsky [SOS92]. We refer to [HHGL11, GIG17] for recent pointers to this line of work, emphasizing that its focus is largely on online active learning. (See [CSZ21] for a recent exception.)

Heuristic approaches. Several groups developed subsampling schemes based on some measure of the impact of each samples on the trained model. Among others: [LG94] measures uncertainty using probabilities predicted by a single current model; [VLM18] selects the samples with largest norm of the gradient  $\nabla_{\pmb{\theta}}\ell (y_i,f(\pmb{x}_i,\pmb{\theta}))$ ; [JWZ+19] selects sample  $i$  with probability increasing in the loss itself  $\ell (y_i,f(\pmb{x}_i,\pmb{\theta}))$  (more precisely, the probability depends on the order statistics of  $\ell (y_i,f(\pmb{x}_i,\pmb{\theta})))$ .

Leverage scores have been used for a long time in statistics as a measure of importance of a data point  $i \in \{1, \dots, N\}$  in linear regression [CH86]. Recall that the leverage of sample  $\boldsymbol{x}_i$  is defined as  $H_{ii} := \boldsymbol{x}_i^{\mathsf{T}}(\boldsymbol{X}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{X})^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}_i$ . Subsampling methods based on leverage scores suggest to sample rows with probabilities  $\pi_i \propto H_{ii}$  and weight them with  $w_i = 1 / \pi_i$  to obtain an unbiased risk estimate [DM06, DMMS11].

These approaches were initially developed from a numerical linear algebra perspective. As a consequence, their objective was to approximate the full sample linear regression solution, rather than to achieve small estimation or generalization error. Statistical analyses of leverage score-based data selection was developed in [MMY14, RM16,  $\mathrm{MCZ}^{+}22$ ]. These works all assume variations of the original scheme of [DM06] possibly replacing  $\pi_i \propto H_{ii}$  by  $\pi_i \propto f(H_{ii})$  for some monotone increasing function  $f$ , and  $w_i = 1 / \pi_i$  for unbiasedness.

Influence functions. Let  $\pmb{\theta}_{*} = \arg \min_{\pmb{\theta}\in \mathbb{R}^{p}}R(\pmb{\theta})$  denote the population parameter values. For a number of estimators (in particular, for M-estimators of the form (1.2), under certain regularity conditions) the following approximate linearity holds as  $n\to \infty$  for  $p$  fixed [vdV00]

$$
\hat {\boldsymbol {\theta}} - \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {*} = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i \in G} w _ {i} \psi \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {i}, y _ {i}\right) + o _ {P} (1 / \sqrt {n}), \tag {1.5}
$$

where  $\psi : \mathbb{R}^p \times \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$  is the so-called influence function. This approximation can be used to select the probabilities  $\pi_i$  and weights  $w_i$ . Several authors have developed this approach in the context of generalized linear models, resulting in the choice  $\pi_i \propto \| \psi(\boldsymbol{x}_i, y_i) \|_2$  [TB18, WZM18, AYZW21]. However this conclusion is derived assuming unbiased subsampling, i.e. for  $w_i = 1 / \pi_i$ . (The recent paper [WZD+20] shows potential for improvement by using biased schemes.)

Further, most of these works [WZM18, WZD $^{+}$ 20, AYZW21] assume a specific asymptotics in which size of the subsample  $n$  diverges after the full sample size  $N$  diverges. In other words, they focus on the regime  $1 \ll n \ll N$ , in which the estimation (or generalization) error achieved after data selection is much larger than the one on the full sample. In many applications, we are most interested in the case in which the two errors (before and after selection) are comparable. In other words, we are practically interested in selecting a constant fraction of the data:  $n = \Theta(N)$ .

Margin-based selection. The recent paper  $\left[\mathrm{SGS}^{+}22\right]$  shows that, in the case of the binary perceptron, under a noiseless teacher-student distribution, selecting samples far from the margin is beneficial in a high-dimensional or data-poor regime. This is in stark contrast with influence-function and related approaches that upsample data points whose labels are more difficult to predict. While our results confirm these findings, measuring uncertainty in terms of distance from the margin is fairly specific to binary classification under certain distributional assumptions<sup>1</sup>.

# 2 Definitions

As mentioned in the introduction, an important application of data selection is to alleviate the need to label data. We will therefore focus on data selection schemes that do not make use of the labels  $(y_{i})_{i\leq N}$ . On the other hand, we will assume to have access to a surrogate model  $\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{su}}(\mathrm{dy}|\boldsymbol {x})$  which is a probability kernel from  $\mathbb{R}^d$  to  $\mathbb{R}$ . We will consider two settings:

- Ideal surrogate. In this case  $\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{su}}(\mathrm{dy}|\pmb{x}) = \mathbb{P}(\mathrm{dy}|\pmb{x})$  is the actual conditional distribution of the labels. Of course this is the very object we want to learn. Since however the overall data selection and learning scheme is constrained, the problem is nevertheless non-trivial.  
- Imperfect surrogates. In this case  $\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{su}}(\mathrm{dy}|\pmb{x})$  is predictive of the actual value of the labels, but does not coincide with the Bayes predictor. We will study the dependence of optimal data-selection on the accuracy of the surrogate model.

The selection probabilities and weights can depend on  $\boldsymbol{x}_i$  and  $\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{su}}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{x}_i)$  (the conditional distribution of  $y_i$  given  $\boldsymbol{x}_i$  under the surrogate). Formally

$$
\pi_ {i} = \pi \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {i}, \mathrm {P} _ {\mathrm {s u}} (\cdot | \boldsymbol {x} _ {i})\right), \quad w _ {i} = w \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {i}, \mathrm {P} _ {\mathrm {s u}} (\cdot | \boldsymbol {x} _ {i})\right), \tag {2.1}
$$

for some functions  $\pi(\cdots), w(\cdots)$ . In order to simplify notations, we will omit the dependence on the surrogate predictions, unless needed.

It is convenient to encode the selection process into random variables  $S_{i}(\pmb{x}_{i},\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{su}}(\cdot |\pmb{x}_{i})) \geq 0$  which depend on  $\pmb{x}_i, \mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{su}}(\cdot |\pmb{x}_i)$ , and also on additional randomness independent across different samples<sup>2</sup>. Again, we will omit the dependence on  $\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{su}}(\cdot |\pmb{x}_i)$ . We recover the original formulation by setting

$$
S _ {i} (\boldsymbol {x} _ {i}) = w \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {i}\right) \mathbf {1} _ {i \in G}, \quad \mathbb {P} (i \in G | \boldsymbol {X}, \boldsymbol {y}) = \pi \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {i}\right). \tag {2.2}
$$

We can thus rewrite the empirical risk (1.3) as:

$$
\hat {R} _ {N} (\boldsymbol {\theta}) = \frac {1}{N} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} S _ {i} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {i}\right) L \left(\boldsymbol {\theta}; y _ {i}, \boldsymbol {x} _ {i}\right) + \lambda \Omega (\boldsymbol {\theta}), \tag {2.3}
$$

$$
L (\boldsymbol {\theta}; y, \boldsymbol {x}) := \ell (y, f (\boldsymbol {x}; \boldsymbol {\theta})). \tag {2.4}
$$

(Analogously, we introduce the notation  $L_{\mathrm{test}}(\pmb {\theta};y,\pmb {x})\coloneqq \ell_{\mathrm{test}}(y,f(\pmb {x};\pmb {\theta}))$  for the test loss.) We also recall the notation for full sample (not reweighted) population risk and its minimizer

$$
R (\boldsymbol {\theta}) := \mathbb {E} \left\{L (\boldsymbol {\theta}; y, \boldsymbol {x}) \right\}, \quad \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {*} := \arg \min  _ {\boldsymbol {\theta}} R (\boldsymbol {\theta}). \tag {2.5}
$$

We will enforce that the target sample size  $n$  is achieved in expectation, namely

$$
n = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \mathbb {E} \pi \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {i}\right), \quad \pi \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {i}\right) := \mathbb {P} \left(S _ {i} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {i}\right) > 0 | \boldsymbol {X}, \boldsymbol {y}\right). \tag {2.6}
$$

A special role is played of course by unbiased schemes.

Definition 2.1. We denote the set of data selection schemes defined above (namely, randomized functions  $(\pmb{x},\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{su}}(\cdot |\pmb{x}))\mapsto S(\pmb{x},\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{su}}(\cdot |\pmb{x})))$  by  $\mathcal{A}$ .

A data selection scheme is unbiased if  $\mathbb{E}\{S(\pmb{x})|\pmb{X},\pmb{y}\} = 1$ . We denote the set of unbiased data selection schemes by  $\mathcal{U}$ .

Throughout, we consider  $n,N\to \infty$  , with converging ratio

$$
\frac {n}{N} \rightarrow \gamma \in (0, 1). \tag {2.7}
$$

We refer to  $\gamma$  as to the 'subsampling fraction.' For the sake of simplicity, we will think of sequences of instances indexed by  $N\to \infty$  and it will be understood that  $n(N)\rightarrow \infty$  as well. As mentioned above, we are interested in  $\gamma$  bounded away from 0 because we want to keep the accuracy close to the full sample accuracy.

Unbiased subsampling can be suboptimal. As mentioned in the introduction, the vast majority of (non-Bayesian) theoretical studies assumes unbiased subsampling schemes, whereby  $w_{i} \propto 1 / \pi_{i}$ . We show both theoretically and empirically that this can lead to potentially unbounded loss in accuracy with respect to biased schemes. Low-dimensional asymptotics provide a particularly compelling explanation of this phenomenon.

In the low-dimensional regime, the estimation error is—roughly speaking—inversely proportional to the curvature of the expected risk. Unbiased methods do not change the curvature, while biased methods can increase the curvature. In fact, we will prove that unbiased methods are suboptimal under a broad set of conditions conditions.

Optimal selection depends on the subsampling fraction. Popular data selection methods are independent of subsampling fraction in the sense that the selection probabilities  $\pi_{i}$  can be computed without knowing the target sampling fraction  $n / N$ , except from the overall normalization that enforces the constraint (2.6). In particular, both leverage score and influence function methods compute scores that depend on the data, but not on the target sample size. In contrast, we will see that optimal schemes depend in a non-trivial way on  $n / N$ . This is intuitively very natural. Consider, for instance the leverage score  $\pmb{x}_i^{\mathsf{T}}(\pmb{X}^{\mathsf{T}}\pmb{X})^{-1}\pmb{x}_i$ . This measures how much datapoint  $\pmb{x}_i$  differs from the other data. However, it makes more sense to measure the difference from other data in the selected set  $G$ . This suggests the use of  $\pmb{x}_i^{\mathsf{T}}(\pmb{X}_G^{\mathsf{T}}\pmb{X}_G)^{-1}\pmb{x}_i$  to select a new data point to add to  $G$ . This intuition will be reflected in the optimal methods derived below.

Plugin use of the surrogate can be suboptimal. How to construct selection schemes in absence of the labels  $y_{i}$ ? A natural idea would be to start from selection probabilities that use the labels,  $\pi_0(y_i,\pmb {x}_i)$ , and then take a conditional expectation with respect to  $y_{i}$ , to get  $\pi (\pmb {x}_i) = \mathbb{E}\{\pi_0(y_i,\pmb {x}_i)|\pmb {x}_i\}$ . We can then replace this conditional expectation by conditional expectation with respect to the surrogate:  $\pi_{\mathrm{su}}(\pmb {x}_i) = \mathsf{E}_{\mathrm{su}}\{\pi (y_i,\pmb {x}_i)|\pmb {x}_i\}$ .

We will show that this is not always the best option, and in particular better surrogate models

do not always lead to better data selection. This is (partially) illustrated by Figure 1, which reports test error achieved by selecting data on the basis of surrogate models of different accuracy. We observe that the test error is insensitive to the difference of surrogate models.

Uncertainty-based subsampling is effective. The simplest rule of thumb emerging from our work broadly confirms earlier research: subsampling schemes based on the sample 'hardness,' i.e. on how uncertain surrogate predictions are, perform well in a broad array of settings. One prominent example is provided by influence function-based selection, which is biased towards most uncertain samples.

While influence-function based subsampling is often a good starting point, it is not generally optimal. First: in the low-dimensional asymptotics, stronger bias towards hard examples is beneficial, cf. Section 4.3. Second: in certain high-dimensional cases, this bias must be reversed, and "easier" examples should be selected, see Section 6. This is in agreement with the results of  $\left[\mathrm{SGS}^{+}22\right]$ . Third, balancing the previous point, selecting the 'hardest' or 'easiest' even in high dimension, is not always optimal. For instance, in some cases biasing towards hard samples can be beneficial in high-dimension, but selecting the hardest ones, e.g. those that have largest value of influence, can lead to significant failures, see e.g. Section 7.

Data subsampling can improve generalization. As stated several times, our objective is to select a fraction  $\gamma \in (0,1)$  of the data such that, training a model on the selected subset yields test error close to the one achieved by training on the whole subset. To our surprise, we discover that good data selection, not only can keep test error close to the original one down to  $\gamma$  as small as 0.4, but can actually reduce the test error below the one obtained from the full sample. This is illustrated by Figure 1.

The reader might wonder whether this happens because information is being passed from the surrogate model to the trained model via data selection. However, things are more interesting than this. Indeed, we achieve a reduction in test error even when the surrogate model is trained on significantly than  $(1 - \gamma)N$  random samples from the same population (so that overall, we are not using the entire dataset).

In Section 4.4, we will construct explicit examples for which we prove that learning after data selection achieves smaller test error than learning on the full sample.

# 4 Low-dimensional asymptotics: Ideal surrogate

In this section we study the classical asymptotics in which  $p$  is fixed as  $n, N \to \infty$ . This setting is simpler and allows for weaker assumptions on the data distribution.

# 4.1 General asymptotics

The asymptotics of  $\hat{\pmb{\theta}}$  depends on the population risk associated to sampling scheme  $S$  (that is the expectation of the empirical risk (2.3)):

$$
R _ {S} (\boldsymbol {\theta}) := \mathbb {E} \left\{S (\boldsymbol {x}) L (\boldsymbol {\theta}; y, \boldsymbol {x}) \right\}. \tag {4.1}
$$

(In this notation, the argument  $S$  indicates the dependence on the function that defines the subsampling procedure.) The conditional gradient covariance, and conditional Hessian will play an

important role, and are defined below:

$$
\boldsymbol {G} (\boldsymbol {x}) := \mathbb {E} \left\{\nabla_ {\boldsymbol {\theta}} L \left(\boldsymbol {\theta} _ {*}; y, \boldsymbol {x}\right) \nabla_ {\boldsymbol {\theta}} L \left(\boldsymbol {\theta} _ {*}; y, \boldsymbol {x}\right) ^ {\top} | \boldsymbol {x} \right\}, \quad \boldsymbol {H} (\boldsymbol {x}) := \mathbb {E} \left\{\nabla_ {\boldsymbol {\theta}} ^ {2} L \left(\boldsymbol {\theta} _ {*}; y, \boldsymbol {x}\right) | \boldsymbol {x} \right\}. \tag {4.2}
$$

Our first result characterizes the error under weighted quadratic losses  $\| \hat{\pmb{\theta}} -\pmb {\theta}_*\|_{\pmb{Q}}^2 \coloneqq \langle \hat{\pmb{\theta}} -\pmb {\theta}_*,\pmb {Q}(\hat{\pmb{\theta}} -$ $\pmb {\theta}_{*})\rangle$ , for  $Q\in \mathbb{R}^{p\times p}$ . This covers both the standard  $\ell_2$  estimation error (by setting  $\pmb {Q} = \pmb{I}$ ) and, under smoothness conditions, the test error  $R_{\mathrm{test}}(\hat{\pmb{\theta}})$

We define the asymptotic error coefficient via

$$
\rho (S; \boldsymbol {Q}) := \lim  _ {M \rightarrow \infty} \lim  _ {N \rightarrow \infty} \mathbb {E} \left\{\left(N \| \hat {\boldsymbol {\theta}} - \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {*} \| _ {\boldsymbol {Q}} ^ {2}\right) \wedge M \right\}, \tag {4.3}
$$

whenever the limit exists. The next result is an application of textbook asymptotic statistics, as detailed in Appendix A.

Proposition 4.1. Assume the following:

A1.  $R_{S}(\pmb{\theta})$  is uniquely minimized at  $\pmb{\theta} = \pmb{\theta}_{*}$  
A2.  $\pmb{\theta} \mapsto L(\pmb{\theta}; y, \pmb{x})$  is non-negative, lower semicontinuous. Further, for every  $\pmb{u} \in \mathbb{S}^{p-1}$ , define  $L_{\infty}(\pmb{u}; y, \pmb{x}) \in [0, \infty]$

$$
L _ {\infty} (\boldsymbol {u}; y, \boldsymbol {x}) = \lim  _ {\substack {\boldsymbol {\theta} \rightarrow + \infty\\\boldsymbol {\theta} / \| \boldsymbol {\theta} \| \rightarrow \boldsymbol {u}}} \inf  _ {L (\boldsymbol {\theta}; y, \boldsymbol {x})}, \tag{4.4}
$$

and assume  $\inf_{\pmb{u} \in \mathbb{S}^{p-1}} \mathbb{E}\{S(\pmb{x}) L_{\infty}(\pmb{u}; y, \pmb{x})\} > R_S(\pmb{\theta}_*)$ .

A3.  $\pmb{\theta} \mapsto L(\pmb{\theta}; y, \pmb{x})$  is differentiable at  $\pmb{\theta}_{*}$  for  $\mathbb{P}$ -almost every  $(y, \pmb{x})$ . Further, for  $B$  a neighborhood of  $\pmb{\theta}_{*}$ ,

$$
\mathbb {E} \sup _ {\pmb {\theta} _ {1} \neq \pmb {\theta} _ {2} \in B} \left\{S (\pmb {x}) ^ {2} \frac {| L (\pmb {\theta} _ {1} ; y , \pmb {x}) - L (\pmb {\theta} _ {2} ; y , \pmb {x}) | ^ {2}}{\| \pmb {\theta} _ {1} - \pmb {\theta} _ {2} \| _ {2} ^ {2}} \right\} <   \infty
$$

A4.  $\pmb{\theta} \mapsto R_{S}(\pmb{\theta})$  is twice differentiable at  $\pmb{\theta}_{*}$ , with  $\nabla^{2}R_{S}(\pmb{\theta}_{*}) = \mathbb{E}\{S(\pmb{x})\pmb{H}(\pmb{x})\} \succ \mathbf{0}$ .

Then, for any  $\mathbf{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times p}$  symmetric, the limit of Eq. (4.3) exists and is given by

$$
\rho (S; \boldsymbol {Q}) = \frac {\mathbb {E} \left\{S (\boldsymbol {x}) ^ {2} \right\}}{\mathbb {E} \left\{S (\boldsymbol {x}) \right\} ^ {2}} \operatorname {T r} \left(\boldsymbol {G} _ {S} \boldsymbol {H} _ {S} ^ {- 1} \boldsymbol {Q} \boldsymbol {H} _ {S} ^ {- 1}\right), \tag {4.5}
$$

where

$$
\boldsymbol {G} _ {S} := \frac {\mathbb {E} \left\{S (\boldsymbol {x}) ^ {2} \boldsymbol {G} (\boldsymbol {x}) \right\}}{\mathbb {E} \left\{S (\boldsymbol {x}) ^ {2} \right\}}, \quad \boldsymbol {H} _ {S} := \frac {\mathbb {E} \left\{S (\boldsymbol {x}) \boldsymbol {H} (\boldsymbol {x}) \right\}}{\mathbb {E} \left\{S (\boldsymbol {x}) \right\}}. \tag {4.6}
$$

Further,  $\lim_{M\to \infty}\lim_{N\to \infty}\mathbb{P}\big\{N\| \hat{\pmb{\theta}} -\pmb{\theta}_{*}\|_{\pmb{Q}}^{2} > M\big\} = 0.$

In particular, if  $\pmb{\theta} \mapsto R_{\mathrm{test}}(\pmb{\theta})$  is twice continuously differentiable at  $\pmb{\theta}_{*}$ , with  $\nabla R_{\mathrm{test}}(\pmb{\theta}_{*}) = \mathbf{0}$ , then

$$
R _ {\text {t e s t}} (\hat {\boldsymbol {\theta}}) = R _ {\text {t e s t}} \left(\boldsymbol {\theta} _ {*}\right) + \frac {1}{N} \cdot \rho (S; \boldsymbol {Q} _ {\text {t e s t}}) \cdot Y _ {S} + o _ {P} (1 / N), \tag {4.7}
$$

where  $\pmb{Q}_{\mathrm{test}} := \frac{1}{2}\nabla^2 R_{\mathrm{test}}(\pmb{\theta}_*)$ , and  $\mathbb{E}Y_S = 1$ . (More explicitly,  $Y_S = \| \pmb{g}_S\|_{\pmb{Q}_{\mathrm{test}}}^2 / \mathbb{E}\{\| \pmb{g}_S\|_{\pmb{Q}_{\mathrm{test}}}^2\}$ , where  $\pmb{g}$  is a centered Gaussian vector with covariance  $\pmb{H}_S^{-1}\pmb{G}_S\pmb{H}_S^{-1}$ .)

Remark 4.1. Key for Proposition 4.1 to hold is condition A1, which requires the minimizer of the subsampled population risk  $R_{S}$  to coincide with the minimizer of the original risk  $R$ . This amounts to say that the data selection scheme is not so biased as to make empirical risk minimization inconsistent. If it does not hold, then the resulting error  $\| \hat{\pmb{\theta}} - \pmb{\theta}_{*} \|_{\pmb{Q}}^{2}$  will be, in general, of order one.

# 4.2 Unbiased data selection

In this section and the next one, we discuss optimal choices of the subsampling procedure under the low-dimensional asymptotics.

We begin by considering unbiased schemes, i.e. schemes satisfying Definition 2.1. While this case is covered by earlier work [TB18, WZM18, AYZW21], it is somewhat simpler than the biased case and provides useful background for the development in the next sections.

The key simplification is that, in the unbiased case, the matrix  $\mathbf{H}_S$  does not depend on  $S$ , namely  $\mathbf{H}_S = \mathbf{H}$ , where

$$
\boldsymbol {H} = \nabla^ {2} R \left(\boldsymbol {\theta} _ {*}\right), \quad R (\boldsymbol {\theta}) := \mathbb {E} L (\boldsymbol {\theta}; y, \boldsymbol {x}). \tag {4.8}
$$

(In general  $R$  can be different from the test error  $R_{\mathrm{test}}(\pmb{\theta})$  because of the different loss.) We thus recover the standard subsampling scheme based on influence functions. (For the reader's convenience, we present a proof of this specific formulation in Appendix B.)

Proposition 4.2. Under the assumptions of Proposition 4.1, further assume  $Q\succeq 0$ ,  $H\succeq 0$ . Then  $\rho (S;Q)$  is minimized among unbiased data selection schemes by a scheme of the form

$$
S _ {\text {u n b}} (\boldsymbol {x}) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \pi_ {\text {u n b}} (\boldsymbol {x}) ^ {- 1} & \text {w i t h p r o b a b i l i t y} \pi_ {\text {u n b}} (\boldsymbol {x}), \\ 0 & \text {o t h e r w i s e}, \end{array} \right. \tag {4.9}
$$

Further, an optimal choice of  $\pi (\pmb {x})$  is given by

$$
\pi_ {\mathrm {u n b}} (\boldsymbol {x}) = \min  \left(1; c (\gamma) Z (\boldsymbol {x}) ^ {1 / 2}\right), \tag {4.10}
$$

$$
Z (\boldsymbol {x}) := \operatorname {T r} \bigl (G (\boldsymbol {x}) H ^ {- 1} Q H ^ {- 1} \bigr) = \mathbb {E} \big \{\left\| \nabla_ {\theta} L (\boldsymbol {\theta}; y, \boldsymbol {x}) \right\| _ {H ^ {- 1} Q H ^ {- 1}} ^ {2} | \boldsymbol {x} \big \}.
$$

Here the constant  $c(\gamma)$  is defined so that  $\mathbb{E}\pi_{\mathrm{unb}}(\pmb {x}) = \gamma$

Finally the optimal coefficient  $\rho_{\mathrm{unb}}(\pmb {Q})\coloneqq \inf_{S\in \mathcal{U}}\rho (S;\pmb {Q}) = \rho (S_{\mathrm{unb}};\pmb {Q})$  is given by

$$
\begin{array}{l} \rho_ {\text {u n b}} (\boldsymbol {Q}) = \inf  _ {\mathbb {E} \pi (\boldsymbol {x}) = \gamma} \mathbb {E} \left\{\frac {Z (\boldsymbol {x})}{\pi (\boldsymbol {x})} \right\} \tag {4.11} \\ = \mathbb {E} \max  \left(\frac {1}{c (\gamma)} Z (\boldsymbol {x}) ^ {1 / 2}; Z (\boldsymbol {x})\right). \\ \end{array}
$$

Denote by  $S_{\mathrm{rand}}$  random subsampling, namely  $S_{\mathrm{rand}}(\pmb{x}) = 1 / \gamma$  with probability  $\gamma$ , and  $S_{\mathrm{rand}}(\pmb{x}) = 0$  otherwise (which is of course unbiased). The next proposition establishes some basic properties of  $\rho_{\mathrm{unb}}(\pmb{Q}; \gamma)$ .

Proposition 4.3. Write  $\rho_{\mathrm{unb}}(\mathbf{Q};\gamma)$  for the optimal unbiased coefficient when the subsampling fraction equals  $\gamma$ . Then: (1)  $\rho_{\mathrm{unb}}(\mathbf{Q}) \leq \rho_{\mathrm{rand}}(\mathbf{Q})$  with the inequality being strict unless  $Z(\mathbf{x})$  is almost surely constant or  $\gamma = 1$ ; (2)  $\gamma \mapsto \rho_{\mathrm{unb}}(\mathbf{Q};\gamma)$  is monotone non-increasing (for  $\mathbf{Q} \succeq \mathbf{0}$ ).

Proof. For claim (1), it is easy to compute  $\rho_{\mathrm{rand}}(\mathbf{Q}) = \gamma^{-1}\mathbb{E}(Z)$ . Therefore, the gain derived from optimal subsampling can be written as

$$
\frac {\rho_ {\mathrm {u n b}} (\boldsymbol {Q})}{\rho_ {\mathrm {r a n d}} (\boldsymbol {Q})} = \frac {\mathbb {E} (Z / \pi_ {\mathrm {u n b}}) \mathbb {E} (\pi_ {\mathrm {u n b}})}{\mathbb {E} ((Z / \pi_ {\mathrm {u n b}}) \cdot \pi_ {\mathrm {u n b}})} \leq 1. \tag {4.12}
$$

The inequality above is due to the fact that  $\pi_{\mathrm{unb}}$  and  $Z / \pi_{\mathrm{unb}}$  are both non-decreasing functions of  $Z$ . It is strict unless the following happens with probability one, for i.i.d. copies  $Z_{1}, Z_{2}$  of  $Z$ :  $\min(1; cZ_{1}^{1/2}) = \min(1; cZ_{2}^{1/2})$  or  $\max(Z_{1}; Z_{1}^{1/2}/c) = \max(Z_{2}; Z_{2}^{1/2}/c)$ . However, this happens only if either  $Z$  is almost surely a constant or if  $cZ \leq 1$  almost surely. The latter implies  $\gamma = 1$ .

To prove claim (2), note that the mapping  $\pi \mapsto \mathbb{E}\{Z(\pmb{x}) / \pi(\pmb{x})\}$  appearing in Eq. (4.11) is monotone with respect to the partial ordering of pointwise domination. Therefore, we can replace the constraint  $\mathbb{E}\pi(\pmb{x}) = \gamma$  by  $\mathbb{E}\pi(\pmb{x}) \leq \gamma$ , whence monotonicity trivially follows.

While monotonicity may appear intuitively obvious, we will see in the Section 4.4 that it does not hold for biased subsampling.

Remark 4.2. Besides computing expectations with respect to the conditional distribution of  $y$  given  $\pmb{x}$  (a problem which will be addressed in the next section), evaluating the score requires to be able to approximate  $\pmb{H} = \mathbb{E}\{\pmb{H}(\pmb{x})\}$ . However, this can be consistently estimated e.g. using the empirical mean  $\hat{H} \coloneqq N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\pmb{H}(\pmb{x}_i)$ . We will neglect errors involved in such approximations.

Remark 4.3 (Connection with influence functions). Under the assumptions of Proposition 4.2 the influence function is given by

$$
\psi (\boldsymbol {x}, y) = - \boldsymbol {H} ^ {- 1} \nabla_ {\boldsymbol {\theta}} L \left(\boldsymbol {\theta} _ {*}; y, \boldsymbol {x}\right), \tag {4.13}
$$

and therefore we can rewrite (omitting for simplicity the capping at  $\pi = 1$ , which is only needed at large  $\gamma$ ),

$$
\pi \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {i}\right) \propto \mathbb {E} \left\{\left\| \psi \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {i}, y _ {i}\right) \right\| _ {\boldsymbol {Q}} ^ {2} \mid \boldsymbol {x} _ {i} \right\} ^ {1 / 2}. \tag {4.14}
$$

We recovered influence function-based subsampling [TB18, WZM18, AYZW21] (with the choice of the norm depending on the estimation loss). Note that the fact that we do not use the label  $y_{i}$  results in the appearance of a conditional expectation of  $y_{i}$  given  $\boldsymbol{x}_{i}$ . Interestingly, the optimal way to deal with unknown  $y_{i}$  is to take conditional expectation of the square of the sampling probability. Namely, we compute (4.14) instead of  $\mathbb{E}\big\{\big|\big|\psi (\boldsymbol{x}_i,y_i)\big|\big|_Q\big|\boldsymbol{x}_i\big\}$ .

Example 4.4 (Linear regression). In this case we take  $L(\pmb{\theta}; y, \pmb{x}) = L_{\mathrm{test}}(\pmb{\theta}; y, \pmb{x}) = (y - \langle \pmb{\theta}, \pmb{x} \rangle)^2 / 2$ . With little loss of generality, we assume  $\pmb{x}$  to be centered i.e.  $\mathbb{E}(\pmb{x}) = \mathbf{0}$ , with finite covariance  $\mathbb{E}\{\pmb{x}\pmb{x}^{\top}\} = \pmb{\Sigma}$ . We then have  $\nabla^2 R(\pmb{\theta}) = \pmb{\Sigma}$  and  $\nabla_{\pmb{\theta}}L(\pmb{\theta}_*; y_i, \pmb{x}_i) = -\varepsilon_i \pmb{x}_i$  where  $\varepsilon_i = y_i - \langle \pmb{\theta}_*, \pmb{x}_i \rangle$  is the 'noise' for observation  $i$ .

If we further assume  $\mathbb{E}(\varepsilon_i^2 | \boldsymbol{x}_i) = \tau^2$  independent of  $\boldsymbol{x}_i$ , we finally get (dropping a constant multiplicative factor)

$$
Z (\boldsymbol {x}) = \left\langle \boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {\Sigma} ^ {- 1} \boldsymbol {Q} \boldsymbol {\Sigma} ^ {- 1} \boldsymbol {x} \right\rangle . \tag {4.15}
$$

In the special case of prediction error,  $\pmb{Q} = \pmb{\Sigma}$  and therefore  $Z(\pmb{x}) = \langle \pmb{x}, \pmb{\Sigma}^{-1}\pmb{x} \rangle$  and we recover a population version of the leverage score. Note that the optimal sampling probabilities are proportional to the square root of the score (capped at 1).

Example 4.5 (Generalized linear models). Again, we assume  $\pmb{x}_i$  to be a centered vector and

$$
\mathbb {P} \left(\mathrm {d} y _ {i} \mid \boldsymbol {x} _ {i}\right) = \exp \left\{y _ {i} \langle \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {*}, \boldsymbol {x} _ {i} \rangle - \phi \left(\langle \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {*}, \boldsymbol {x} _ {i} \rangle\right) \right\} v _ {0} (\mathrm {d} y _ {i}). \tag {4.16}
$$

Here  $\nu_{0}$  is the reference measure, e.g.  $\nu_{0} = \delta_{+1} + \delta_{-1}$  for logistic regression. We fit a model of the same type and train via the loss  $L(\pmb {\theta};y,\pmb {x}) = -y\langle \pmb {\theta},\pmb {x}\rangle +\phi (\langle \pmb {\theta},\pmb {x}\rangle)$ . Hence  $\nabla_{\pmb{\theta}}L(\pmb {\theta};y,\pmb {x}) = -(y - \phi^{\prime}(\langle \pmb {\theta},\pmb {x}\rangle))\pmb{x}^{\mathsf{T}}$  and  $\nabla^2 R(\pmb {\theta}_*) = \mathbb{E}\{\phi ''(\langle \pmb {\theta},\pmb {x}\rangle)\pmb {x}\pmb{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\} \coloneqq \pmb{H}$ .

A simple calculation yields:

$$
Z (\boldsymbol {x}) = \phi^ {\prime \prime} \left(\left\langle \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {*}, \boldsymbol {x} \right\rangle\right) \cdot \left\langle \boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {H} ^ {- 1} \boldsymbol {Q} \boldsymbol {H} ^ {- 1} \boldsymbol {x} \right\rangle . \tag {4.17}
$$

# 4.3 Biased data selection

We now consider biased subsampling schemes  $S$ . Among these, a special role is played by those schemes in which the only randomness is the choice of whether or not to select a certain sample  $i$ . We refer to these schemes as 'simple.'

Definition 4.4 (Simple data selection schemes). A scheme is simple if there exist functions  $w, \pi$  such that

$$
S (\boldsymbol {x}) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} w (\boldsymbol {x}) & \text {w i t h p r o b a b i l i t y} \pi (\boldsymbol {x}), \\ 0 & \text {o t h e r w i s e}, \end{array} \right. \tag {4.18}
$$

We denote the set of simple sampling schemes by  $\mathcal{S} \subseteq \mathcal{A}$  (recall that  $\mathcal{A}$  is the set of all data selection schemes).

We can identify any simple data selection scheme with the pair  $(\pi, w)$ , hence we will occasionally abuse notation and write  $\rho(\pi, w; \mathbf{Q})$  for  $\rho(S; \mathbf{Q})$ .

Lemma 4.5. For  $Q\succeq 0$  , we have

$$
\inf  _ {S \in \mathscr {A}} \rho (S; Q) = \inf  _ {S \in \mathscr {P}} \rho (S; Q). \tag {4.19}
$$

We can therefore restrict ourselves to simple schemes without loss (See Appendix C for the proof). Among these, we focus on 'non-reweighting schemes' that are defined by the additional condition  $w = 1$  and denote them by  $\mathcal{N} \subseteq \mathcal{S}$ . Non-reweighting schemes are practically convenient since they do not require to modify the training procedure.

It turns out that optimal non-reweighting schemes have a simple structure, as stated below. (See Appendix D for a proof.)

Proposition 4.6. Under the assumptions of Proposition 4.1, further assume  $Q\succeq \mathbf{0}$ ,  $H\succeq \mathbf{0}$ ,  $\mathbb{E}\{\nabla_{\pmb{\theta}}L(\pmb{\theta}_{*};y,\pmb{x})|\pmb{x}\} = \mathbf{0}$ , and  $\pmb{x}\mapsto G(\pmb{x})$ ,  $H(\pmb{x})$  to be continuous. Further define

$$
\boldsymbol {G} _ {\pi} := \mathbb {E} _ {\pi} \boldsymbol {G} (\boldsymbol {x}), \quad \boldsymbol {H} _ {\pi} := \mathbb {E} _ {\pi} \boldsymbol {H} (\boldsymbol {x}), \quad \text {w h e r e} \quad \mathbb {E} _ {\pi} f (\boldsymbol {x}) := \frac {\mathbb {E} \left\{f (\boldsymbol {x}) \pi (\boldsymbol {x}) \right\}}{\mathbb {E} \left\{\pi (\boldsymbol {x}) \right\}} \tag {4.20}
$$

Finally define the function

$$
Z (\boldsymbol {x}; \pi) := - \operatorname {T r} \left\{\boldsymbol {G} (\boldsymbol {x}) \boldsymbol {H} _ {\pi} ^ {- 1} \boldsymbol {Q} \boldsymbol {H} _ {\pi} ^ {- 1} \right\} + 2 \operatorname {T r} \left\{\boldsymbol {H} (\boldsymbol {x}) \boldsymbol {H} _ {\pi} ^ {- 1} \boldsymbol {Q} \boldsymbol {H} _ {\pi} ^ {- 1} \boldsymbol {G} _ {\pi} \boldsymbol {H} _ {\pi} ^ {- 1} \right\}. \tag {4.21}
$$

Then there exists  $\pi_{\mathrm{nr}}$  achieving the minimum of the asymptotic error over non-reweighting schemes  $\rho (\pi_{\mathrm{nr}},1;Q) = \inf_{S\in \mathcal{N}}\rho (S;Q)$ . Further, if  $\pmb{H}_{\pi_{\mathrm{nr}}} > \mathbf{0}$  strictly, then  $\pi_{\mathrm{nr}}$  takes the form

$$
\pi_ {\mathrm {n r}} (\boldsymbol {x}) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 1 & \text {i f} Z (\boldsymbol {x}; \pi_ {\mathrm {n r}}) > \lambda , \\ 0 & \text {i f} Z (\boldsymbol {x}; \pi_ {\mathrm {n r}}) <   \lambda , \\ b (\boldsymbol {x}) \in [ 0, 1 ] & \text {i f} Z (\boldsymbol {x}; \pi_ {\mathrm {n r}}) = \lambda . \end{array} \right. \tag {4.22}
$$

where  $\lambda$  and  $b(\pmb{x})$  are chosen so that  $\mathbb{E}\pi_{\mathrm{nr}}(\pmb{x}) = \gamma$ . The resulting optimal asymptotic error  $\rho_{\mathrm{nr}}(\pmb{Q}) = \rho(\pi_{\mathrm{nr}}, 1; \pmb{Q})$  is

$$
\begin{array}{l} \rho_ {\mathrm {n r}} (\boldsymbol {Q}) = \frac {1}{\gamma} \operatorname {T r} \left(\boldsymbol {G} _ {\pi_ {\mathrm {n r}}} \boldsymbol {H} _ {\pi_ {\mathrm {n r}}} ^ {- 1} \boldsymbol {Q} \boldsymbol {H} _ {\pi_ {\mathrm {n r}}} ^ {- 1}\right) (4.23) \\ = \frac {1}{\gamma} \inf  _ {\pi : \mathbb {E} \pi = \gamma} \operatorname {T r} \left(\boldsymbol {G} _ {\pi} \boldsymbol {H} _ {\pi} ^ {- 1} \boldsymbol {Q} \boldsymbol {H} _ {\pi} ^ {- 1}\right). (4.24) \\ \end{array}
$$

In many cases of interest, the set  $\{\pmb{x}: Z(\pmb{x}; \pi_{*}) = \lambda\}$  has zero measure (for instance, this is typically the case if the distribution of  $\pmb{x}$  is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue). In this case, the optimal  $\pi$  selects the samples deterministically to be those satisfying  $Z(\pmb{x}_{i}; \pi_{*}) > \lambda$ .

In some cases, we can interpret  $Z(\pmb{x}_i; \pi_*)$  as a score measuring the uncertainty in predicting the label of sample  $\pmb{x}_i$  (see examples below). Then this proposition establishes that, under the non-reweighing scheme (and in the low-dimensional asymptotics) we should select the 'hardest'  $n$  examples.

Remark 4.6. The condition  $\mathbb{E}\{\nabla_{\pmb{\theta}}L(\pmb{\theta}_{*};y,\pmb{x})|\pmb{x}\} = \mathbf{0}$  (almost surely) could be eliminated at the cost of enforcing the constraint  $\mathbb{E}\{\nabla_{\pmb{\theta}}L(\pmb{\theta}_{*};y,\pmb{x})\pi (\pmb{x})\} = \mathbf{0}$ . This would result in a more complicated expression for the data selection rule: we will not pursue this generalization.

In the next two subsections, we will specialize non-reweighting subsampling to linear models and generalized linear models. In particular, the examples presented in Section 4.3.1 prove that biased data selection can beat unbiased selection by an arbitrarily large factor, as stated formally below.

Theorem 1. Consider least-square regression under the model  $y_{i} = \langle \pmb{\theta}_{*},\pmb{x}_{i}\rangle +\varepsilon_{i}$ , with  $\mathbb{E}(\varepsilon_i|\pmb{x}_i) = 0$ ,  $\mathbb{E}(\varepsilon_i^2 |\pmb{x}_i) = \tau^2$ . For any  $C$ , there is a distribution of the  $\pmb{x}_i$ 's and a  $\gamma \in (0,1)$  such that  $\rho_{\mathrm{unb}}(\pmb{\Sigma};\gamma) / \rho_{\mathrm{nr}}(\pmb{\Sigma};\gamma) > C$ .

# 4.3.1 Linear regression (Proof of Theorem 1)

Continuing from Example 4.4, note that the condition  $\mathbb{E}\{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}L(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*};y,\boldsymbol{x})|\boldsymbol{x}\} = \mathbf{0}$  holds. We now have  $\pmb {G}(\pmb {x}) = \tau^{2}\pmb {x}\pmb{x}^{\top},\pmb {H}(\pmb {x}) = \pmb {x}\pmb{x}^{\top}$ . Since rescaling  $\pmb{G}$  does not change the selection rule, we can redefine  $\pmb {G}(\pmb {x}) = \pmb {x}\pmb{x}^{\top}$ , whence  $\pmb {H}_{\pi} = \pmb{G}_{\pi} = \pmb{\Sigma}_{\pi}$ , where  $\pmb{\Sigma}_{\pi}$  is the population covariance of the subsampled feature vectors.

A simple calculation shows

$$
Z (\boldsymbol {x}; \pi) = \left\langle \boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {\Sigma} _ {\pi} ^ {- 1} \boldsymbol {Q} \boldsymbol {\Sigma} _ {\pi} ^ {- 1} \boldsymbol {x} \right\rangle , \tag {4.25}
$$

$$
\pi_ {\mathrm {n r}} (\boldsymbol {x}) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 1 & \text {i f} \langle \boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {\Sigma} _ {\pi_ {\mathrm {n r}}} ^ {- 1} \boldsymbol {Q} \boldsymbol {\Sigma} _ {\pi_ {\mathrm {n r}}} ^ {- 1} \boldsymbol {x} \rangle > \lambda , \\ 0 & \text {i f} \langle \boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {\Sigma} _ {\pi_ {\mathrm {n r}}} ^ {- 1} \boldsymbol {Q} \boldsymbol {\Sigma} _ {\pi_ {\mathrm {n r}}} ^ {- 1} \boldsymbol {x} \rangle <   \lambda . \end{array} \right. \tag {4.26}
$$

In other words, this scheme selects all data that lay outside a certain ellipsoid. The shape of the ellipsoid is determined self-consistently by the covariance  $\pmb{\Sigma}_{\pi_{\mathrm{nr}}}$  of points outside the ellipsoid.

Let emphasize two differences with respect to the standard leverage-score approach of Eq. (4.15):

(i) As for general non-reweighing schemes, selection is essentially deterministic,  $\pi_{\mathrm{nr}}(\pmb{x}) \in \{0,1\}$  (in particular, if the distribution of  $\pmb{x}$  has a density, then  $\pi_{\mathrm{nr}}(\pmb{x}) \in \{0,1\}$  with probability one).  
(ii) The original covariance  $\pmb{\Sigma}$  is replaced by the covariance of selected data  $\pmb{\Sigma}_{\pi}$ . As anticipated in Section 3, the selected set depends on  $\gamma$  in a nontrivial way.

Given that the leverage score of datapoint  $\pmb{x}_i$  measures how different is  $\pmb{x}_i$  from the other data, the modified score of Eq. (4.25) can be interpreted as measuring how different is  $\pmb{x}_i$  from selected data.

Example 4.7 (One-dimensional covariates). In order to get a more concrete understanding of the difference with respect to unbiased data selection, consider one-dimensional covariates  $x_{i} \sim \mathsf{P}_{X}$  with  $\mathsf{P}_X$  of mean zero. We study prediction error, i.e.  $Q = \Sigma$ . Let  $X_{M}$  be the supremum of the support of  $\mathsf{P}_{|X|}$ , which we assume finite, and  $X$  a sample from  $\mathsf{P}_X$ . In this case  $Z(x) = \tau^2 x^2 / \Sigma$ . Provided  $\gamma \leq \mathbb{E}|X| / X_M$ , we have  $c(\gamma) = \gamma \mathbb{E}(X^2)^{1/2} / (\tau \mathbb{E}|X|)$  and the optimal asymptotic error for unbiased subsampling is

$$
\rho_ {\mathrm {u n b}} (\Sigma) = \frac {\tau^ {2}}{\gamma} \cdot \frac {(\mathbb {E} | X |) ^ {2}}{\mathbb {E} \left(X ^ {2}\right)}. \tag {4.27}
$$

On the other hand, assuming  $\mathsf{P}_X$  has a density, the optimal non-reweighting rule takes the form  $\pi_*(x) = \mathbf{1}(|x| \geq r(\gamma))$ . The coefficient  $r = r(\gamma)$  is fixed by  $\gamma = \mathbb{P}(|X| \geq r)$ . The optimal asymptotic error for non-reweighting subsampling is

$$
\rho_ {\mathrm {n r}} (\Sigma) = \tau^ {2} \cdot \frac {\mathbb {E} \left(X ^ {2}\right)}{\mathbb {E} \left(X ^ {2} \mathbf {1} _ {| X | \geq r}\right)}. \tag {4.28}
$$

The ratio of biased to unbiased error then takes the form

$$
\frac {\rho_ {\mathrm {n r}} (\Sigma)}{\rho_ {\mathrm {u n b}} (\Sigma)} = \frac {\mathbb {E} \left(X ^ {2}\right) ^ {2} \mathbb {P} (| X | \geq r)}{(\mathbb {E} | X |) ^ {2} \mathbb {E} \left(X ^ {2} \mathbf {1} _ {| X | \geq r}\right)}, \quad \gamma \leq \frac {\mathbb {E} | X |}{X _ {M}}. \tag {4.29}
$$

It is easy to come up with examples in which this ratio is smaller than one. For instance if  $\mathsf{P}_X = \mathsf{Unif}([-X_M, X_M])$ , then

$$
\frac {\rho_ {\mathrm {n r}} (\Sigma)}{\rho_ {\mathrm {u n b}} (\Sigma)} = \frac {4 \gamma}{3 (1 - (1 - \gamma) ^ {3})}, \quad \gamma \leq \frac {1}{2}. \tag {4.30}
$$

More generally, as  $\gamma \to 0$ , we get

$$
\frac {\rho_ {\mathrm {n r}} (\Sigma)}{\rho_ {\mathrm {u n b}} (\Sigma)} = \frac {\mathbb {E} \left(X ^ {2}\right) ^ {2}}{(\mathbb {E} | X |) ^ {2} X _ {M} ^ {2}} + o (1), \quad \gamma \rightarrow 0, \tag {4.31}
$$

Notice that this ratio is always smaller than one (by Hölder's inequality) and can be arbitrarily small. For instance  $\mathsf{P}_X(\mathrm{d}x) = C_{M,\alpha}|x|^{-\alpha}\mathbf{1}_{1\leq |x|\leq X_M},\alpha >3$  then the above ratio is

$$
\frac {\rho_ {\mathrm {n r}} (\Sigma)}{\rho_ {\mathrm {u n b}} (\Sigma)} = \left(\frac {\alpha - 2}{\alpha - 3} \cdot \frac {1 - X _ {M} ^ {- \alpha + 3}}{1 - X _ {M} ^ {- \alpha + 2}}\right) ^ {2} \frac {1}{X _ {M} ^ {2}} + o (1). \tag {4.32}
$$

To be concrete, for  $\alpha = 4$ ,  $X_M = 10$ , unbiased is suboptimal by a factor larger than 30. My choosing  $X_M$  large, we can make this ratio arbitrarily small, hence proving Theorem 1.

Example 4.8 (Elliptical covariates). The one-dimensional example above is easily generalized to higher dimensions. Consider  $\boldsymbol{x}_i = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{z}_i$  where  $\boldsymbol{z}_i$  are spherically symmetric, namely  $\boldsymbol{z}_i = r_i\boldsymbol{u}_i$  with  $(r_i,\boldsymbol{u}_i)\sim \mathsf{P}_R\otimes \mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d - 1}(\sqrt{d}))$ . If we consider test error (and therefore  $Q = \Sigma$ ), then it is a symmetry argument shows that, for optimal  $\pi$  the modified leverage score (4.25) coincides with the original one

$$
Z (\boldsymbol {x}; \pi_ {*}) = \left\langle \boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {\Sigma} ^ {- 1} \boldsymbol {x} \right\rangle = \| \boldsymbol {z} \| _ {2} ^ {2} = r ^ {2}. \tag {4.33}
$$

We therefore recover the one-dimensional case with  $\mathsf{P}_{|X|}$  replaced by  $\mathsf{P}_R$ .

# 4.3.2 Generalized linear models

Continuing from Example 4.5, we note that  $\mathbb{E}\{y|\pmb{x}\} = \phi'(\langle \pmb{\theta}_*,\pmb{x}\rangle)$ . Therefore  $\mathbb{E}\{\nabla_{\pmb{\theta}}L(\pmb{\theta}_*;y,\pmb{x})|\pmb{x}\} = \mathbf{0}$ . Further

$$
\boldsymbol {G} (\boldsymbol {x}) = \mathbb {E} \left\{\left(y - \phi^ {\prime} \left(\left\langle \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {*}, \boldsymbol {x} \right\rangle\right)\right) ^ {2} \mid \boldsymbol {x} \right\} \cdot \boldsymbol {x x} ^ {\top} = \phi^ {\prime \prime} \left(\left\langle \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {*}, \boldsymbol {x} \right\rangle\right) \cdot \boldsymbol {x x} ^ {\top} = \boldsymbol {H} (\boldsymbol {x}). \tag {4.34}
$$

whence we have the following generalization of the score (4.25):

$$
Z (\boldsymbol {x}; \pi) = \phi^ {\prime \prime} \left(\left\langle \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {*}, \boldsymbol {x} \right\rangle\right) \cdot \left\langle \boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {H} _ {\pi} ^ {- 1} \boldsymbol {Q} \boldsymbol {H} _ {\pi} ^ {- 1} \boldsymbol {x} \right\rangle . \tag {4.35}
$$

Figure 4: Cartoon of the monotonicity properties of various data-selection schemes, with the selected sample size.

Note that this is similar to the score derived in the unbiased case, cf. Eq. (4.17), with two important differences that we already encountered for linear regression: (i) The selection process is essentially deterministic: a datapoint is selected if  $Z(\pmb{x}_i; \pi) > \lambda$  and not selected if  $Z(\pmb{x}_i; \pi) < \lambda$ ; (ii) The score is computed with respect to the selected data, namely  $H$  is replaced by  $H_{\pi}$ . The effect of replacing  $H$  by  $H_{\pi}$  is illustrated on a toy data distribution in Figure 3.

We observe that at high  $\gamma$ , the two selection procedures are very similar. In contrast, at low  $\gamma$ , selection based on  $\pmb{H}$  is always biased towards selecting "hard samples" (in directions roughly orthogonal to  $\pmb{\theta}_{*}$ ), whereas selection based on  $\pmb{H}_{\pi}$  takes into account geometry of selected subset and keeps samples in a more diverse set of directions.

We also note that, for GLMs, the optimal asymptotic error coefficient (4.23) takes the particularly simple form

$$
\rho_ {\mathrm {n r}} (\boldsymbol {Q}) = \frac {1}{\gamma} \inf  _ {\pi : \mathbb {E} \pi = \gamma} \operatorname {T r} \left(\boldsymbol {H} _ {\pi} ^ {- 1} \boldsymbol {Q}\right). \tag {4.36}
$$

In the case of well-specified GLMs, biased data selection cannot improve over full sample estimation. Indeed,  $\rho_{\mathrm{nr}}(Q) = \operatorname{Tr}(\mathbb{E}\{H(\pmb{x})\pi_{\mathrm{nr}}(\pmb{x})\}^{-1}Q)$ , and  $\mathbb{E}\{H(\pmb{x})\pi_{\mathrm{nr}}(\pmb{x})\} \preceq \mathbb{E}\{H(\pmb{x})\}$ . On the other hand, it is clear that  $\rho_{\mathrm{nr}}(Q) \leq \rho_{\mathrm{rand}}(Q)$  (because  $\rho_{\mathrm{rand}}(Q)$  corresponds to a special choice of  $\pi$  on the right-hand side of Eq. (4.36)). Further, the inequality is strict (namely,  $\rho_{\mathrm{nr}}(Q) < \rho_{\mathrm{rand}}(Q)$ ) except (possibly) on a degenerate case in which  $Z(\pmb{x};\pi)$  is constant on a set of positive measure.

# 4.4 Data selection can beat full-sample estimation

One striking phenomenon illustrated in Figure 1 is that data selection can reduce generalization error. Namely, running empirical risk minimization (ERM) with respect to a selected subset of  $n < N$  samples out of  $N$  total produces a better model than running ERM on the full dataset. This is the case even when data selection was based on a relatively weak surrogate, e.g. one trained on  $n_{\mathrm{su}}$  random samples, so that  $n_{\mathrm{su}} + n$  is significantly below  $N$ .

Is this non-monotonic behavior compatible with theoretical expectations? The answer depends on the data selection scheme (see Figure 4 for a cartoon illustration):

- For unbiased (reweighted) data selection, we saw in Section 4.2 that the asymptotic test error is always monotone in the size of the subsample  $n$  (at least within the low-dimensional setting studied here). In particular, full sample data ERM cannot have worse test error than ERM on selected data.

- Consider then the optimal data selection scheme with reweighting. We claim that this is also monotone. Indeed given target sample sizes  $n_1 < n_2$ , one can simulate data selection at sample size  $n_1$  by first selecting  $n_2$  samples and then setting to 0 the weights of  $n_2 - n_1$  samples.

However, for  $n = N$ , this scheme does not reduce to unweighted ERM, but to optimally weighted ERM. As a consequence, this monotonicity property does not imply that original unweighted ERM on the full sample has better test error than weighted ERM on a data-selected subsample.

- The main result of this section will be a proof that non-monotonicity is possible in a neighborhood of  $\gamma = 1$  for non-reweighting schemes.

Theorem 2. Under the setting of Proposition 4.6, further assume  $H \succ 0$ ,  $\mathbb{E}\{\| G(\pmb{x})\|_{\mathrm{op}}^4\} < \infty$ ,  $\mathbb{E}\{\| H(\pmb{x})\|_{\mathrm{op}}^4\} < \infty$ . Then, there exists a constant  $C$  such that, for any  $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$  such that  $\mathbb{P}(Z_Q(\pmb{x};1) < \lambda) > 0$ , we have

$$

$$

Further

# 4.5 Is unbiased subsampling ever optimal?

In the Section 4.3, we presented examples in which unbiased subsampling is inferior to a special type of biased subsampling: non-reweighting subsampling. Given this, it is natural to ask when is unbiased subsampling optimal. We will next prove that, in a natural setting, unbiased subsampling is always suboptimal below a certain subsampling ratio.

Theorem 4. Consider the setting of Proposition 4.1, and consider the prediction error under test loss equal to training loss (i.e.  $\pmb{Q} = \pmb{H} \coloneqq \nabla^{2} R(\pmb{\theta}_{*})$ ). Further assume

A1. For  $G(\pmb{x})$  and  $H(\pmb{x})$  defined as in Eqs (4.2), we have  $G(\pmb{x}) = H(\pmb{x})$ . (This is for instance the case in maximum likelihood).  
A2.  $G(\pmb {x})$  is almost surely non-zero has an almost sure upper bound3.  
A3. The distribution of  $G(\pmb{x}) / \mathrm{Tr}(G(\pmb{x})H^{-1})^{1/2}$  is not not supported on any strict affine subspace of the set of  $d \times d$  symmetric matrices.  
A4.  $\mathbb{E}\{\nabla_{\pmb{\theta}}L(\pmb{\theta}_{*};y,\pmb{x})|\pmb{x}\} = \mathbf{0}$

Then there exists  $\gamma_0 > 0$  such that, for any  $\gamma \in (0,\gamma_0)$  there is a biased subsampling scheme asymptotically outperforming the best unbiased scheme. Namely, there exists a subsampling scheme  $S_{b}$  such that the following inequality holds strictly

$$
\rho \left(S _ {b}; \boldsymbol {H}\right) <   \inf  _ {S \in \mathcal {U}} \rho (S; \boldsymbol {H}). \tag {4.41}
$$

The proof of this theorem is deferred to Appendix F. We construct a perturbation of the unbiased scheme  $w(\pmb{x}) = (1 + \varepsilon \varphi(\pmb{x})) / \pi_{\mathrm{unb}}(\pmb{x})$ , and show that it leads to an improvement of the test error for small  $\varepsilon$ . Let us emphasize that, as a consequence, we do not prove that a non-reweighing scheme necessarily beats unbiased subsampling.

Remark 4.9. The restriction  $\gamma \in (0,\gamma_0)$  in Theorem 4 is a proof artifact. Under the theorem's assumptions, it implies that the unbiased scheme (4.10) take the simpler form  $\pi_{\mathrm{unb}}(\pmb {x}) = c(\gamma)Z(\pmb {x})^{1 / 2}$ , which is more amenable to analysis.

Example 4.10 (Generalized linear models). Consider again the GLM model of Example 4.5 (log-likelihood loss). Recall that in this case

$$
\boldsymbol {G} (\boldsymbol {x}) = \boldsymbol {H} (\boldsymbol {x}) = \phi^ {\prime \prime} (\langle \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {*}, \boldsymbol {x} \rangle) \boldsymbol {x} \boldsymbol {x} ^ {\top}.
$$

Hence condition A1 is satisfied, and A4 is also always satisfied. If  $\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{d}\pmb{x})$  is supported on  $\| \pmb{x}\| _2\leq M$  and  $\mathbb{P}(\pmb {x} = 0) = 0$  then condition A2 is also satisfied. (Note that  $\phi ''(t) > 0$  for any  $t > 0$  unless  $\nu_{0} = \delta_{c}$  is a point mass, which is a degenerate case.)

Finally, for condition A3 note that

$$
\frac {\boldsymbol {G} (\boldsymbol {x})}{\operatorname {T r} (\boldsymbol {G} (\boldsymbol {x}) \boldsymbol {H} ^ {- 1}) ^ {1 / 2}} = \frac {\boldsymbol {x} \boldsymbol {x} ^ {\intercal}}{\langle \boldsymbol {x} , \boldsymbol {H} \boldsymbol {x} \rangle}.
$$

Therefore, a sufficient condition for A3 is that the support of  $\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{d}\pmb{x})$  contains an arbitrarily small ball  $\mathsf{B}^d (\pmb{x}_0;\varepsilon)\subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ ,  $\varepsilon >0$ .

# 5 Low-dimensional asymptotics: Imperfect surrogates

In this section we consider the more realistic setting in which the surrogate model  $\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{su}}(\mathrm{dy}|\boldsymbol{x})$  does not coincide with the actual conditional distribution of  $y_{i}$  given  $\boldsymbol{x}_i$ .

We will model this situation using a minimax point of view. Namely, we will assume that the actual conditional distribution is in a neighborhood of the surrogate model, and will study the worst case error in this neighborhood. The minimax theorem then implies that we should use data selection schemes that are optimal for the worst data distribution in this neighborhood. We will not pursue the construction of minimax optimal data selection schemes in this paper. However, we point out that the broad conclusion is consistent with some of our empirical findings in Section 8. Namely, in certain cases using a less accurate surrogate model yields better data selection.

Throughout, we will use the notation  $\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{su}}\{F(y,\pmb {x})|\pmb {x}\} = \int F(y,\pmb {x})\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{su}}(\mathrm{d}y|\pmb {x})$ , and similarly for  $\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{su}}(\cdot |\pmb {x})$ .

# 5.1 Plugin schemes

The simplest approach to utilize an imperfect surrogate proceeds as follows: (i) Choose a data selection scheme under the assumption of ideal surrogate; (ii) Replace the conditional expectations  $\mathbb{E}\{F(y,\pmb {x})|\pmb {x}\}$  in that scheme by expectations with respect to the surrogate model  $\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{su}}\{F(y,\pmb {x})|\pmb {x}\}$ ; (iii) Replace expectations over  $\pmb{x}$  by expectation over the data sample.

In particular, revisiting the schemes of Sections 4.2 and 4.3, we obtain the following:

Plugin unbiased data selection. We form

$$
\boldsymbol {G} _ {\mathrm {s u}} (\boldsymbol {x}) := \mathbb {E} _ {\mathrm {s u}} \left\{\nabla_ {\boldsymbol {\theta}} L \left(\hat {\boldsymbol {\theta}} ^ {\mathrm {s u}}; y, \boldsymbol {x}\right) \nabla_ {\boldsymbol {\theta}} L \left(\hat {\boldsymbol {\theta}} ^ {\mathrm {s u}}; y, \boldsymbol {x}\right) ^ {\top} | \boldsymbol {x} \right\}, \tag {5.1}
$$

$$
\boldsymbol {H} _ {\mathrm {s u}} (\boldsymbol {x}) := \mathbb {E} _ {\mathrm {s u}} \left\{\nabla_ {\boldsymbol {\theta}} ^ {2} L \left(\hat {\boldsymbol {\theta}} ^ {\mathrm {s u}}; y, \boldsymbol {x}\right) | \boldsymbol {x} \right\}, \tag {5.2}
$$

and subsample according to

$$
\pi (\boldsymbol {x}) = \min  \left(1; c (\gamma) Z _ {\mathrm {s u}} (\boldsymbol {x}) ^ {1 / 2}\right), \tag {5.3}
$$

$$
Z _ {\mathrm {s u}} (\boldsymbol {x}) := \operatorname {T r} \left(\boldsymbol {G} _ {\mathrm {s u}} (\boldsymbol {x}) \boldsymbol {H} _ {1, \mathrm {s u}} ^ {- 1} \boldsymbol {Q} \boldsymbol {H} _ {1, \mathrm {s u}} ^ {- 1}\right), \tag {5.4}
$$

$$
\boldsymbol {H} _ {1, \mathrm {s u}} := \mathbb {E} \left\{\boldsymbol {H} _ {\mathrm {s u}} (\boldsymbol {x}) \right\}. \tag {5.5}
$$

We then reweight each selected sample proportionally to  $1 / \pi (\pmb {x})$ . Note that:

- The 'true' parameters vector  $\pmb{\theta}_{*}$  appearing in  $\nabla_{\pmb{\theta}}L(\cdot ;y,\pmb{x})$ ,  $\nabla_{\pmb{\theta}}^{2}L(\cdot ;y,\pmb{x})$  was replaced by an estimate obtained from the surrogate model. In certain applications  $\hat{\pmb{\theta}}^{\mathrm{su}}$  can be 'read off' the surrogate model itself. In general, we can define it via  $\hat{\pmb{\theta}}^{\mathrm{su}}\coloneqq \arg \min \sum_{i = 1}^{N}L(\pmb {\theta};y_i^{\mathrm{su}},\pmb {x}_i)$ , where  $(y_i^{\mathrm{su}})_{i\leq N}$  are drawn independently according to  $y_{i}\sim \mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{su}}(\cdot |\pmb{x}_{i})$ .  
- The matrix  $\mathbf{H}_{1,\mathrm{su}}$  can be replaced its empirical version  $\hat{\mathbf{H}}_{1,\mathrm{su}} := N^{-1} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{su}}(\mathbf{x}_i)$ .

Plugin non-reweighting data selection. In this case we select samples such that  $Z_{\mathrm{su}}(\pmb{x}; \pi) > \lambda$ , cf. Eq. (4.22), where

$$
Z (\boldsymbol {x}; \pi) := - \operatorname {T r} \left\{\boldsymbol {G} _ {\mathrm {s u}} (\boldsymbol {x}) \boldsymbol {H} _ {\mathrm {s u}, \pi} ^ {- 1} \boldsymbol {Q} \boldsymbol {H} _ {\mathrm {s u}, \pi} ^ {- 1} \right\} + 2 \operatorname {T r} \left\{\boldsymbol {H} _ {\mathrm {s u}} (\boldsymbol {x}) \boldsymbol {H} _ {\mathrm {s u}, \pi} ^ {- 1} \boldsymbol {Q} \boldsymbol {H} _ {\mathrm {s u}, \pi} ^ {- 1} \boldsymbol {G} _ {\mathrm {s u}, \pi} \boldsymbol {H} _ {\mathrm {s u}, \pi} ^ {- 1} \right\}, \tag {5.6}
$$

and  $\pmb{H}_{\mathrm{su},\pi} := \mathbb{E}\{\pmb{H}_{\mathrm{su}}(\pmb{x})\pi(\pmb{x})\} / \mathbb{E}\{\pi(\pmb{x})\}$  and similarly for  $\pmb{G}_{\mathrm{su},\pi}$ . Again, expectations over  $\pmb{x}$  are replaced by averages over the  $N$  samples.

Plugin approaches are natural and easy to define, and in fact we will use them in our simulations. However, we will show that they can be suboptimal. Before doing that, we need to make more explicit the notion of optimality that is relevant here.

# 5.2 Minimax formulation

We want formalize the idea that we do not know the conditional distribution of  $y$  given  $\pmb{x}$ , but we have some information about it coming from the surrogate model  $\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{su}}$ . With this in mind, we introduce a set of probability kernels

$$
\mathcal {K} _ {d} \subseteq \mathcal {K} _ {d} ^ {0} := \left\{\mathsf {P}: \mathcal {B} _ {\mathbb {R}} \times \mathbb {R} ^ {d} \rightarrow [ 0, 1 ] \mathrm {p r o b a b i l i t y k e r n e l} \right\}. \tag {5.7}
$$

Informally,  $\mathcal{K}_d$  is a neighborhood of the surrogate model  $\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{su}}$ , and captures our uncertainty about the actual conditional distribution: we know that  $\mathbb{P}_{y|\boldsymbol{x}} \in \mathcal{K}_d$ . For instance, we could consider, for some  $r \in [0,1)$ ,

$$
\mathcal {K} _ {d} \left(\mathrm {P} _ {\mathrm {s u}}; r\right) := \left\{\mathrm {P}: \mathbb {E} _ {\boldsymbol {x}} \| \mathrm {P} (\cdot | \boldsymbol {x}) - \mathrm {P} _ {\mathrm {s u}} (\cdot | \boldsymbol {x}) \| _ {\mathrm {T V}} \leq r \right\}. \tag {5.8}
$$

We will assume  $\mathcal{H}_d$  (and its variant  $\mathcal{H}_{N,d}$  introduced below) to be convex. Namely, for all  $\lambda \in [0,1]$ ,

$$
\mathrm {P} _ {0}, \mathrm {P} _ {1} \in \mathcal {H} _ {d} \Rightarrow \mathrm {P} _ {\lambda} (\mathrm {d} y | \boldsymbol {x}) = (1 - \lambda) \mathrm {P} _ {0} (\mathrm {d} y | \boldsymbol {x}) + \lambda \mathrm {P} _ {1} (\mathrm {d} y | \boldsymbol {x}) \in \mathcal {K} _ {d}. \tag {5.9}
$$

We are interested in a data selection scheme that works well uniformly over the uncertainty encoded in  $\mathcal{H}_d$ .

Given a probability kernel  $\mathsf{P}(\mathrm{d}y|\pmb {x})$  (i.e.  $\mathsf{P}:\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{R}}\times \mathbb{R}^d\to [0,1])$ , we write  $\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{P})$  for the data distribution (on  $\mathbb{R}^d\times \mathbb{R}$ ) induced by  $\mathsf{P}$ . Namely  $\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{P})}F(y,\pmb {x}):= \mathbb{E}_{\pmb{x}}\{\int F(y,\pmb {x})\mathsf{P}(\mathrm{d}y|\pmb {x})\}$ .

Let  $R_{\mathrm{test}}(\pmb{\theta}) = \mathbb{E}L_{\mathrm{test}}(\pmb{\theta};y,\pmb{x})$  be the test error with respect to a certain target distribution  $\mathbb{P}$ . Given an estimator  $\hat{\pmb{\theta}}$ , we let  $\hat{\pmb{\theta}}_S(\pmb{y},\pmb{X})$  denote its output when applied to data  $\pmb{y},\pmb{X}$  in conjunction with data selection scheme  $S$ . For clarity of notation, we define

$$
R _ {\#} (S; \boldsymbol {y}, \boldsymbol {X}) := R _ {\text {t e s t}} \left(\hat {\boldsymbol {\theta}} _ {S} (\boldsymbol {y}, \boldsymbol {X})\right). \tag {5.10}
$$

We define the minimax risk  $R_{\mathrm{MM}}(\mathcal{H}_d)$  by (here we recall that  $\mathcal{A}$  is the set of all data selection methods)

$$
R _ {*} (S; \mathcal {K} _ {d}) := \sup  _ {\mathsf {P} \in \mathcal {K} _ {d}} \mathbb {E} _ {\boldsymbol {y}, \boldsymbol {X} \sim \mathbb {P} (\mathsf {P})} R _ {\#} (S; \boldsymbol {y}, \boldsymbol {X}), \tag {5.11}
$$

$$
R _ {\mathrm {M M}} \left(\mathcal {H} _ {d}\right) := \inf  _ {S \in \mathscr {A}} R _ {*} (S; \mathcal {H} _ {d}). \tag {5.12}
$$

We seek near optimal data selection schemes  $S$ , namely schemes such that  $R_{*}(S;\mathcal{H}_{d})\approx R_{\mathrm{MM}}(\mathcal{H}_{d})$ . We note that the expectation in Eq. (5.11) includes expectation over the randomness in  $S$ .

Remark 5.1. The set  $\mathcal{H}_d$  provides information about  $\theta_*$ . This information can be exploited in other ways than via data selection. For instance, we could restrict the empirical risk minimization of Eq. (1.2) to a set that is "compatible" with  $\mathcal{H}_d$ . However, we are only interested in procedures that follow the general data selection framework defined in the previous sections and hence are not necessarily optimal against this broader set.

# 5.3 Duality and its consequences

We can apply Sion's minimax theorem to a relaxation of  $R_{\mathrm{MM}}(\mathcal{K}_d)$ . Namely,

- We replace  $\mathcal{H}_d$  by a set of probability kernels  $\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}$  to  $\mathbb{R}^N$ :

$$
\mathcal {K} _ {N, d} \subseteq \mathcal {K} _ {N, d} ^ {0} := \left\{\mathrm {P}: \mathcal {B} _ {\mathbb {R} ^ {N}} \times \mathbb {R} ^ {N \times d} \rightarrow [ 0, 1 ] \text {p r o b a b i l i t y k e r n e l} \right\}. \tag {5.13}
$$

such that each marginal of  $\mathsf{P} \in \mathcal{H}_{N,s}$  is in  $\mathcal{H}_d$ . In other words, we allow for entries of  $\pmb{y}$  to be conditionally dependent, given  $\pmb{X}$ . Generalizing the notations above,  $\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{P}_N)$  denotes the induced distribution on  $\pmb{y}, \pmb{X}$ .

- We replace the space of data selection schemes  $\mathcal{A}$  by a set  $\overline{\mathcal{A}}$  of probability kernels  $\mathbb{Q}$  such that, for any  $A \subseteq [N]$ , and any  $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$ ,  $\mathbb{Q}(A|\mathbf{X})$  is the conditional probability of selecting data in the set  $A$  given covariate vectors  $\mathbf{X}$ . In other words, we consider more general data-selection schemes in which the selected set is allowed to depend on all the data points.

The following result is an application of the standard minimax theorem in statistical decision theory, see e.g. [LM08, Section 3.7].

Theorem 5. Assume that any  $\mathsf{P}_N\in \mathcal{K}_{d,N}$  is supported on  $\| \pmb {y}\| \leq M$  , and that  $(\pmb {y},\pmb {X})\mapsto$ $R(\hat{\theta}_A(\pmb {y},\pmb {X}))$  is continuous for any  $A$  . Define

$$
\bar {R} _ {\mathrm {M M}} \left(\mathcal {K} _ {d}\right) := \inf  _ {S \in \bar {\mathcal {A}}} \bar {R} _ {*} (S; \mathcal {K} _ {d}) := \inf  _ {S \in \bar {\mathcal {A}}} \sup  _ {\mathsf {P} _ {N} \in \mathcal {K} _ {d, N}} \mathbb {E} _ {\boldsymbol {y}, \boldsymbol {X} \sim \mathbb {P} \left(\mathsf {P} _ {N}\right)} R _ {\#} (S; \boldsymbol {y}, \boldsymbol {X}). \tag {5.14}
$$

Then we have

$$
\bar {R} _ {\mathrm {M M}} \left(\mathcal {K} _ {d}\right) = \sup  _ {\mathsf {P} _ {N} \in \mathcal {K} _ {d, N}} \inf  _ {S \in \overline {{\mathcal {A}}}} \mathbb {E} _ {\boldsymbol {y}, \boldsymbol {X} \sim \mathbb {P} \left(\mathsf {P} _ {N}\right)} R _ {\#} (S; \boldsymbol {y}, \boldsymbol {X}). \tag {5.15}
$$

Further, assume  $\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{MM}}$  achieves the supremum over  $\mathcal{H}_d$  above. Then any

$$
S _ {\mathrm {M M}} \in \underset {S \in \overline {{\mathcal {A}}}} {\arg \min } \mathbb {E} _ {\boldsymbol {y}, \boldsymbol {X} \sim \mathbb {P} (\mathrm {P} _ {\mathrm {M M}})} R _ {\#} (S; \boldsymbol {y}, \boldsymbol {X}) \tag {5.16}
$$

achieves the minimax error.

As is common in estimation theory, the minimax theorem can be difficult to apply since computing the supremum over  $\mathsf{P} \in \mathcal{H}_d$  is in general very difficult. Nevertheless, the theorem implies the following important insight. We should not perform data selection by plugging in the surrogate conditional model for  $y$  given  $\pmb{x}$  for the actual one. Instead, we should optimize data selection as if labels were distributed according to the 'worst' conditional model in a neighborhood of the surrogate.

Below we work out a toy case to illustrate this insight. Instead of studying the minimax problem for the finite-sample risk  $R$ , we will consider its asymptotics defined in Proposition 4.1. We define the asymptotic minimax coefficients  $\rho_{*}(S;\mathcal{H}_{d})$  and  $\rho_{\mathrm{MM}}(\mathcal{H}_d)$  in analogy with Eqs. (5.11) and (5.12).

Example 5.2 (Discrete covariates). Consider  $x$  taking values in  $[k] = \{1, \dots, k\}$ , with probabilities  $p_{\ell} = \mathbb{P}(x = \ell)$ , and  $y$  taking values in  $\{0, 1\}$ , with  $\mathbb{P}(y = 1|x) = \theta_x^*$ , whereby  $\pmb{\theta}^{*} = (\theta_{1}^{*}, \dots, \theta_{k}^{*})$  is a vector of unknown parameters. We estimate  $\pmb{\theta}^{*}$  using empirical risk minimization with respect to log-loss

$$
L (\boldsymbol {\theta}; y, x) = - y \log \theta_ {x} - (1 - y) \log \left(1 - \theta_ {x}\right). \tag {5.17}
$$

We are interested in the quadratic estimation error  $\| \hat{\pmb{\theta}} -\pmb{\theta}^{*}\|_{\pmb{Q}}$  with  $\pmb {Q} = \mathrm{diag}(q_1,q_2,\dots ,q_k)$ . We consider a non-reweighting subsampling scheme whereby a sample with covariate  $x$  is retained independently with probability  $\pi_{x}$ . Either applying Proposition 4.1, or by a straightforward calculation, we obtain:

$$
\mathbb {E} \left\{\| \hat {\boldsymbol {\theta}} ^ {S} - \boldsymbol {\theta} ^ {*} \| _ {\boldsymbol {Q}} ^ {2} \right\} = \frac {1}{N} \rho (\pi ; \boldsymbol {\theta} ^ {*}, \boldsymbol {Q}) + o (1 / N), \tag {5.18}
$$

$$
\rho (\pi ; \boldsymbol {\theta} ^ {*}, \boldsymbol {Q}) = \sum_ {x = 1} ^ {k} \frac {\theta_ {x} ^ {*} (1 - \theta_ {x} ^ {*})}{\pi_ {x} p _ {x}} q _ {x}. \tag {5.19}
$$

(Here we made explicit the dependence on  $\pmb{\theta}^{*}$ .) For  $\mathcal{H} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^k$  a convex set, we then define

$$
\rho_ {*} (\pi ; \mathscr {K}, Q) := \sup  _ {\boldsymbol {\theta} ^ {*} \in \mathscr {K}} \rho (\pi ; \boldsymbol {\theta} ^ {*}, Q), \quad \rho_ {\mathrm {M M}} (\mathscr {K}, Q) := \inf  _ {\pi} \rho_ {*} (\pi ; \mathscr {K}, Q). \tag {5.20}
$$

We will assume that  $\mathcal{K}$  is closed (hence compact) and convex. We can apply the minimax theorem to Eq. (5.20):

$$
\rho_ {\mathrm {M M}} (\mathcal {K}, \boldsymbol {Q}) := \max  _ {\boldsymbol {\theta} ^ {*} \in \mathcal {K}} \min  _ {\pi} \rho (\pi ; \boldsymbol {\theta} ^ {*}, \boldsymbol {Q}). \tag {5.21}
$$

Hence, the minimax optimal data selection strategy is obtained by selecting the optimum  $\pi$  for the worst case  $\pmb{\theta}$ , to be denoted by  $\pmb{\theta}^{\mathrm{MM}}$ . A simple calculation yields

$$
\pi_ {x} ^ {\mathrm {M M}} = \min  \left(\frac {c (\gamma)}{p _ {x}} \sqrt {q _ {x} \theta_ {x} ^ {\mathrm {M M}} \left(1 - \theta_ {x} ^ {\mathrm {M M}}\right)}; 1\right), \tag {5.22}
$$

$$
\boldsymbol {\theta} _ {s} ^ {\mathrm {M M}} = \arg \max  _ {\boldsymbol {\theta} \in \mathcal {K}} \sum_ {x = 1} ^ {k} \max  \left(\frac {1}{c (\gamma)} \sqrt {q _ {x} \theta_ {x} (1 - \theta_ {x})}; \frac {q _ {x}}{p _ {x}} \theta_ {x} (1 - \theta_ {x})\right), \tag {5.23}
$$

where  $c(\gamma)$  is obtained by solving

$$
\sum_ {x = 1} ^ {k} \min  \left(c (\gamma) \sqrt {q _ {x} \theta_ {x} ^ {\mathrm {M M}} \left(1 - \theta_ {x} ^ {\mathrm {M M}}\right)}; p _ {x}\right) = \gamma . \tag {5.24}
$$

The above formulas have a clear interpretation (for simplicity we neglect the factor  $q_{x}$ ). Note that  $\theta_{x}^{\mathrm{MM}}(1 - \theta_{x}^{\mathrm{MM}})$  can be interpreted as measure of uncertainty in predicting  $y_{\mathrm{new}}$  at a test point  $x_{\mathrm{new}} = x$  under the minimax model  $\pmb{\theta}^{\mathrm{MM}}$ . We select data so that the fraction of samples with  $x_{i} = x$  is proportional to the square root of this uncertainty. Assuming that the inner maximum

in Eq. (5.23) is achieved on the first argument, the minimax model itself is the one that maximize total uncertainty within the set  $\mathcal{H}$ .

For instance, if  $\mathcal{H} = [\theta_{\mathrm{su},1} - \varepsilon, \theta_{\mathrm{su},1} + \varepsilon] \times \dots \times [\theta_{\mathrm{su},k} - \varepsilon, \theta_{\mathrm{su},k} + \varepsilon] \subseteq [0,1/2]^k$ , then  $\theta_x^{\mathrm{MM}} = \theta_{\mathrm{su},x} + \varepsilon$  for all  $x$ . We then obtain

$$
\pi_ {x} ^ {\mathrm {M M}} = \min \left(\frac {c (\gamma)}{p _ {x}} \sqrt {q _ {x} (\theta_ {\mathrm {s u} , x} + \varepsilon) (1 - \theta_ {\mathrm {s u} , x} - \varepsilon)}; 1\right) \tag {5.25}
$$

In other words, we should not use the uncertainties computed within the surrogate model, but within the most uncertain model in its neighborhood.

# 6 High-dimensional asymptotics: Generalized linear models

In this section we study data selection within a high dimensional setting whereby the number of samples  $N$  and the dimension  $p$  diverge simultaneously, while their ratio converges to some  $\delta_0\in (0,\infty)$ . We are therefore studying the limit  $n,N,p\to \infty$  with

$$
\frac {n}{N} \rightarrow \gamma , \quad \frac {N}{p} \rightarrow \delta_ {0}, \tag {6.1}
$$

with  $\gamma, \delta_0 \in (0, \infty)$ .

We will restrict ourselves to the case of (potentially misspecified) generalized linear models already introduced in Example 4.5 and Section 4.4. In this case, we can derive an asymptotically exact characterization of the risk using a well-established strategy based on Gaussian comparisons inequalities.

In the next section, we will evaluate theoretical predictions derived in this one, and compare them with numerical simulations on synthetic data.

As we will see in this section and the next, the high-dimensional setting allows us to unveil a few interesting phenomena, namely: (i) Biasing data selection towards hard samples (those that are uncertain under the surrogate model) can be suboptimal; (ii) Even when biasing towards hard samples is effective, selecting the top hardest one can lead to poor behavior at small  $\gamma$ ; (iii) A one parameter family of selection probabilities introduced in the next section is broadly effective.

# 6.1 Setting

We want to focus on the optimal use of the surrogate model, and how this changes from low to high-dimension. With this motivation in mind, we consider a model in which the covariates carry little or no information about the value of a sample. Namely, we assume isotropic covariates  $\pmb{x}_i\sim \mathsf{N}(0,\pmb{I}_p)$ , and responses  $y_{i}$  that depend on a one-dimensional projection of the data:

$$
\mathbb {P} \left(y _ {i} \in A \mid \boldsymbol {x} _ {i}\right) = \mathrm {P} \left(A \mid \langle \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {0}, \boldsymbol {x} _ {i} \rangle\right), \tag {6.2}
$$

where, for each  $z$ ,  $\mathsf{P}(\cdot |z)$  is a probability distribution over  $\mathbb{R}$ . Note that this setting includes, as a special case, generalized linear models (see Example 4.5), whereby, for  $z_{i} = \langle \pmb{\theta}_{*},\pmb{x}_{i}\rangle$ :  $\mathsf{P}(\mathrm{d}y_i|z_i) = \exp \left\{y_i z_i - \phi (z_i)\right\} \nu_0(\mathrm{d}y_i)$ . It also includes the special misspecified binary response model of Section 4.4 in which case  $y_{i}\in \{+1, - 1\}$  and  $\mathbb{P}(y_i = +1|\pmb {x}_i) = f(\langle \pmb {\theta}_0,\pmb {x}_i\rangle) = 1 - \mathbb{P}(y_i = -1|\pmb {x}_i)$ .

We specialize the post-data selection empirical risk minimization of Eq. (2.3) to

$$
\hat {R} _ {N} (\boldsymbol {\theta}) := \frac {1}{N} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} S _ {i} \left(\left\langle \hat {\boldsymbol {\theta}} ^ {\mathrm {s u}}, \boldsymbol {x} _ {i} \right\rangle\right) L \left(\left\langle \boldsymbol {\theta}, \boldsymbol {x} _ {i} \right\rangle , y _ {i}\right) + \frac {\lambda}{2} \| \boldsymbol {\theta} \| _ {2} ^ {2}. \tag {6.3}
$$

where  $L: \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$  is a loss function (we abuse notations and replace  $L(\pmb{\theta}; y_i, \pmb{x}_i)$  by  $L(\langle \pmb{\theta}, \pmb{x}_i \rangle, y_i)$ ). This is a special case of Eq. (2.3) in two ways: first, the loss depends on  $\pmb{\theta}$ ,  $\pmb{x}_i$  only through  $\langle \pmb{\theta}, \pmb{x}_i \rangle$  and, second, we focus on a ridge regularizer. Our characterization will require  $L$  to be convex in its first argument (hence  $L(\langle \pmb{\theta}, \pmb{x}_i \rangle, y_i)$  will be convex in  $\pmb{\theta}$ ). As before, it is understood that  $S_i(\langle \hat{\pmb{\theta}}^{\mathrm{su}}, \pmb{x}_i \rangle)$  depends on some additional i.i.d. randomness, namely  $S_i(\langle \hat{\pmb{\theta}}^{\mathrm{su}}, \pmb{x}_i \rangle) = s(\langle \hat{\pmb{\theta}}^{\mathrm{su}}, \pmb{x}_i \rangle, U_i)$ , where  $(U_i)_{i \leq N} \sim_{iid} \mathrm{Unif}([0,1])$ .

Denoting by  $\hat{\pmb{\theta}}_{\lambda} \coloneqq \arg \min_{\pmb{\theta}} \hat{R}_{N}(\pmb{\theta})$ , we will consider a test error of the form

$$
R _ {\mathrm {t e s t}} (\hat {\boldsymbol {\theta}} _ {\lambda}) = \mathbb {E} L _ {\mathrm {t e s t}} \left(\langle \hat {\boldsymbol {\theta}} _ {\lambda}, \boldsymbol {x} _ {\mathrm {n e w}} \rangle , y _ {\mathrm {n e w}}\right). \tag {6.4}
$$

We will also consider the excess error  $R_{\mathrm{exc}}(\hat{\pmb{\theta}}_{\lambda}) \coloneqq R_{\mathrm{test}}(\hat{\pmb{\theta}}_{\lambda}) - \inf_{\pmb{\theta}}R_{\mathrm{test}}(\pmb{\theta})$ .

Note that the data distribution is invariant under rotations in feature space that leave  $\pmb{\theta}_0$  unchanged. In other words, if  $\pmb{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times p}$  is an orthogonal matrix such that  $\pmb{Q}\pmb{\theta}_0 = \pmb{\theta}_0$  (with  $\pmb{Q}$  independent of the data), then  $(y_i, \pmb{Q}\pmb{x}_i)$  is distributed as  $(y_i, \pmb{x}_i)$ . As a consequence, and because of the rotational invariance of the ridge regularizer, the behavior of the empirical risk minimization (6.3) depends on the surrogate model only through the following parameters:

$$
\beta_ {0} := \lim  _ {N, p \rightarrow \infty} \frac {\langle \hat {\boldsymbol {\theta}} ^ {\mathrm {s u}} , \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {0} \rangle}{\| \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {0} \|}, \quad \beta_ {s} := \lim  _ {N, p \rightarrow \infty} \| \boldsymbol {P} _ {0} ^ {\perp} \hat {\boldsymbol {\theta}} ^ {\mathrm {s u}} \| _ {2}, \tag {6.5}
$$

where  $P_0^\perp$  is the projector orthogonal to  $\theta_0$ . In words,  $\beta_0$  is the size of the projection of the surrogate model onto the direction of the true model, while  $\beta_\perp$  is the size of the perpendicular direction.

Remark 6.1. Of course in this setting the population risk minimizer  $\pmb{\theta}_{*}$  does not coincide necessarily with  $\pmb{\theta}_0$ . Instead, we have  $\pmb{\theta}_{*} = c_{*}\pmb{\theta}_{0} / \| \pmb{\theta}_{0}\|$ , where

$$
c _ {*} = \arg \min  _ {c \in \mathbb {R}} \mathbb {E} L (c G _ {0}; Y), \tag {6.6}
$$

and expectation is with respect to  $G_0 \sim \mathsf{N}(0,1)$ ,  $Y \sim \mathsf{P}(\cdot | \| \pmb{\theta}_0 \| G_0)$ .

# 6.2 Asymptotics of the estimation error

The high-dimensional asymptotics of the test error is determined by a saddle point of the following Lagrangian (here and below  $\pmb{\alpha} \coloneqq (\alpha_0, \alpha_s, \alpha_\perp)$ ,  $\pmb{\beta} \coloneqq (\beta_0, \beta_s, 0)$ ):

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathcal {L} (\boldsymbol {\alpha}, \mu , \omega) := \frac {\lambda}{2} \| \boldsymbol {\alpha} \| ^ {2} - \frac {1}{2 \delta_ {0}} \mu \alpha_ {\perp} ^ {2} + \mathbb {E} \Big \{\min  _ {u \in \mathbb {R}} \left[ S (\langle \boldsymbol {\beta}, \boldsymbol {G} \rangle) L (\alpha_ {0} G _ {0} + \alpha_ {s} G _ {s} + u, Y) + \right. \\ \left. \frac {1}{2} \mu \left(\alpha_ {\perp} G _ {\perp} - u\right) ^ {2} \right] \} \tag {6.7} \\ \end{array}
$$

Here expectation is with respect to

$$
\boldsymbol {g} = \left(G _ {0}, G _ {s}, G _ {\perp}\right) \sim \mathrm {N} (\mathbf {0}, \boldsymbol {I} _ {3}), \quad Y \sim \mathrm {P} \left(\cdot \mid \| \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {0} \| _ {2} G _ {0}\right). \tag {6.8}
$$

as well as the randomness in  $S$ .

Theorem 6. Assume  $u \mapsto L(u, y)$  is convex, continuous, with at most quadratic growth, and  $\lambda > 0$ . Further denote by  $\alpha^{*}, \mu^{*}$  the solution of the following minimax problem ( $\alpha^{*}$  is uniquely defined by this condition)

$$
\min  _ {\boldsymbol {\alpha}} \max  _ {\mu \geq 0} \mathcal {L} (\boldsymbol {\alpha}, \mu). \tag {6.9}
$$

Then the following hold in the limit  $N,p\to \infty$  , with  $N / p\rightarrow \delta_0$  ..

(a) If  $(u,z)\mapsto \int L_{\mathrm{test}}(u;y)\mathsf{P}(\mathrm{d}y|z)$  is a continuous function with at most quadratic growth, we have

$$
\underset {N, p \rightarrow \infty} {\operatorname * {p - l i m}} R _ {\text {t e s t}} (\hat {\boldsymbol {\theta}} _ {\lambda}) = \mathbb {E} L _ {\text {t e s t}} \left(\alpha_ {0} ^ {*} G _ {0} + \sqrt {\left(\alpha_ {s} ^ {*}\right) ^ {2} + \left(\alpha_ {\perp} ^ {*}\right) ^ {2}} G; Y\right), \tag {6.10}
$$

where expectation is taken with respect to the joint distribution of Eq. (6.8).

(b) If  $\pmb{\theta}_0 \in \arg \min R_{\mathrm{test}}(\pmb{\theta})$ , then the excess risk is given by

$$
\underset {N, p \rightarrow \infty} {\operatorname * {p - l i m}} R _ {\mathrm {e x c}} (\hat {\boldsymbol {\theta}} _ {\lambda}) = \mathbb {E} L _ {\text {t e s t}} \left(\alpha_ {0} ^ {*} G _ {0} + \sqrt {\left(\alpha_ {s} ^ {*}\right) ^ {2} + \left(\alpha_ {\perp} ^ {*}\right) ^ {2}} G; Y\right) - \mathbb {E} L _ {\text {t e s t}} \left(c _ {*} G _ {0}; Y\right). \tag {6.11}
$$

(See Remark 6.1 for a definition of  $c_{*}$ .)

(c) Letting  $\pmb{P}_0^\perp$  be the projector orthogonal to  $\pmb{\theta}_0$  and  $\pmb{P}^{\perp}$  the projector orthogonal to  $\mathrm{span}(\pmb{\theta}_0, \hat{\pmb{\theta}}^{\mathrm{su}})$ , we have

$$
\underset {N, p \rightarrow \infty} {\mathrm {p - l i m}} \frac {\langle \hat {\boldsymbol {\theta}} _ {\lambda} , \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {0} \rangle}{\| \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {0} \|} = \alpha_ {0} ^ {*}, \quad \underset {N, p \rightarrow \infty} {\mathrm {p - l i m}} \frac {\langle \hat {\boldsymbol {\theta}} _ {\lambda} , \boldsymbol {P} _ {0} ^ {\perp} \hat {\boldsymbol {\theta}} ^ {\mathrm {s u}} \rangle}{\| \boldsymbol {P} _ {0} ^ {\perp} \hat {\boldsymbol {\theta}} ^ {\mathrm {s u}} \|} = \alpha_ {s} ^ {*}, \quad \underset {N, p \rightarrow \infty} {\mathrm {p - l i m}} \| \boldsymbol {P} ^ {\perp} \hat {\boldsymbol {\theta}} _ {\lambda} \| = \alpha_ {\perp} ^ {*}. \tag {6.12}
$$

(d) The asymptotic subsampling fraction is given by

$$
\frac {n}{N} \rightarrow \gamma = \mathbb {P} \left(S \left(\beta_ {0} G _ {0} + \beta_ {s} G _ {s}\right) > 0\right). \tag {6.13}
$$

The proof of Theorem 6 is deferred to Appendix G. We can further specialize the above formulas to the two classes of data selection schemes studied before: unbiased and non-reweighting schemes.

Unbiased data selection. In this case  $S_{i}(\langle \hat{\pmb{\theta}}^{\mathrm{su}},\pmb{x}_{i}\rangle) = 1 / \pi (\langle \hat{\pmb{\theta}}^{\mathrm{su}},\pmb{x}_{i}\rangle)$  with probability  $\pi (\langle \hat{\pmb{\theta}}^{\mathrm{su}},\pmb{x}_i\rangle)$  and  $S_{i}(\langle \hat{\pmb{\theta}}^{\mathrm{su}},\pmb{x}_{i}\rangle) = 0$  otherwise. The Lagrangian reduces to

$$
\mathcal {L} (\boldsymbol {\alpha}, \mu) := \frac {\lambda}{2} \| \boldsymbol {\alpha} \| ^ {2} - \frac {1}{2 \delta_ {0}} \mu \alpha_ {\perp} ^ {2} + \mathbb {E} \Big \{\min  _ {u \in \mathbb {R}} \big [ L (\alpha_ {0} G _ {0} + \alpha_ {s} G _ {s} + u; Y) + \frac {1}{2} \mu \pi (\langle \boldsymbol {\beta}, \boldsymbol {g} \rangle) (u - \alpha_ {\perp} G _ {\perp}) ^ {2} \big ] \Big \}.
$$

Non-reweighting data selection. In this case  $S_{i}(\langle \hat{\pmb{\theta}}^{\mathrm{su}},\pmb{x}_{i}\rangle) = 1$  with probability  $\pi (\langle \hat{\pmb{\theta}}^{\mathrm{su}},\pmb{x}_i\rangle)$  and  $S_{i}(\langle \hat{\pmb{\theta}}^{\mathrm{su}},\pmb{x}_{i}\rangle) = 0$  otherwise. The Lagrangian reduces to

$$
\mathscr {L} (\boldsymbol {\alpha}, \mu) := \frac {\lambda}{2} \| \boldsymbol {\alpha} \| ^ {2} - \frac {1}{2 \delta_ {0}} \mu \alpha_ {\perp} ^ {2} + \mathbb {E} \left\{\pi \left(\langle \boldsymbol {\beta}, \boldsymbol {g} \rangle\right) \min  _ {u \in \mathbb {R}} \left[ L \left(\alpha_ {0} G _ {0} + \alpha_ {s} G _ {s} + u; Y\right) + \frac {1}{2} \mu \left(u - \alpha_ {\perp} G _ {\perp}\right) ^ {2} \right] \right\}. \tag {6.14}
$$

# 6.3 The case of misspecified linear regression

We revisit the case of misspecified linear regression, already studied in Section 4.4. We assume a non-reweighting data-selection scheme, whose asymptotic behavior is characterized by the Lagrangian (6.14).

In the case of square loss, the inner minimization over  $u$  is easily solved and we can then perform the maximization over  $\mu$  in Theorem 6 analytically. This calculation yields the following Lagrangian

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm {l s}} (\boldsymbol {\alpha}) := \frac {1}{2} \left(\sqrt {\mathbb {E} \left\{\pi \left(\langle \boldsymbol {\beta} , \boldsymbol {g} \rangle\right) \left[ Y - \langle \boldsymbol {\alpha} , \boldsymbol {g} \rangle \right] ^ {2} \right\}} - \frac {\alpha_ {\perp}}{\sqrt {\delta_ {0}}}\right) _ {+} ^ {2} + \frac {\lambda}{2} \| \boldsymbol {\alpha} \| _ {2} ^ {2}. \tag {6.15}
$$

We then have the following consequence of Theorem 6.

Corollary 6.1. Assume the misspecified generalized linear model of Section 6.1, and further consider the case of square loss  $L(\pmb{\theta}; y, \pmb{x}) = (y - \langle \pmb{\theta}, \pmb{x} \rangle)^2 / 2$ . Let  $\alpha^* = (\alpha_0^*, \alpha_s^*, \alpha_\perp^*)$  be the unique minimizer of the Lagrangian  $\mathcal{L}_0$ . Then claims (a) to (d) of Theorem 6 hold.

The next statement gives a particularly simple expression in the case of perfect surrogate, and ridgeless limit  $\lambda \to 0$ . For  $n > p$  this is standard least squares, while for  $n \leq p$ , this is minimum  $\ell_2$  norm interpolation. Its proof is deferred to Appendix H.

Proposition 6.2. Assume the misspecified generalized linear model of Section 6.1, and further consider the case of square loss  $L(\pmb{\theta}; y, \pmb{x}) = (y - \langle \pmb{\theta}, \pmb{x} \rangle)^2 / 2$ , and further consider the case of a perfect surrogate which, without loss of generality, we assume normalized:  $\hat{\pmb{\theta}}^{\mathrm{su}} = \pmb{\theta}_0 / \| \pmb{\theta}_0 \|$ . Define the quantities

$$
A _ {\pi} := \frac {1}{\gamma} \mathbb {E} [ G ^ {2} \pi (G) ], \quad B _ {\pi} := \frac {1}{\gamma} \mathbb {E} [ G Y \pi (G) ], \quad C _ {\pi} := \frac {1}{\gamma} \mathbb {E} [ Y ^ {2} \pi (G) ], \tag {6.16}
$$

where expectation is with respect to  $G \sim \mathsf{N}(0,1)$  and  $Y \sim \mathsf{P}(\cdot |G)$ . In particular, we let  $A_{1}, B_{1}, C_{1}$  be the quantities defined above with  $\pi(t) = 1$  identically.

Then the asymptotic excess risk of ridgeless regression is as follows

(here  $\delta \coloneqq \delta_0\gamma = \lim_{n,p\to \infty}(n / p))$

1. For  $\delta > 1$ :

$$
\lim  _ {\lambda \rightarrow 0} \mathrm {p - l i m} _ {N, p \rightarrow \infty} R _ {\mathrm {e x c}} (\hat {\boldsymbol {\theta}} _ {\lambda}) = \left(\frac {B _ {1}}{A _ {1}} - \frac {B _ {\pi}}{A _ {\pi}}\right) ^ {2} + \frac {1}{\delta - 1} \cdot \left(C _ {\pi} - \frac {B _ {\pi} ^ {2}}{A _ {\pi}}\right). \tag {6.17}
$$

2. For  $\delta < 1$ :

$$
\begin{array}{l} \lim  _ {\lambda \rightarrow 0} \mathrm {p - l i m} _ {N, p \rightarrow \infty} R _ {\mathrm {e x c}} (\hat {\boldsymbol {\theta}} _ {\lambda}) = \left(\frac {B _ {1}}{A _ {1}} - \frac {B _ {\pi} \delta}{1 - \delta + A _ {\pi} \delta}\right) ^ {2} + \frac {B _ {\pi} ^ {2}}{A _ {\pi}} \cdot \frac {\delta (1 - \delta)}{(1 - \delta + A _ {\pi} \delta) ^ {2}} \tag {6.18} \\ + \frac {\delta}{1 - \delta} \cdot \left(C _ {\pi} - \frac {B _ {\pi} ^ {2}}{A _ {\pi}}\right). \\ \end{array}
$$

Remark 6.2 (Random data selection). In the case of no data selection,  $\pi (\pmb {x}) = \gamma$ , we have  $A_{\pi} = A_{1} = 1$ ,  $B_{\pi} = B_{1}$ ,  $C_{\pi} = C_{1}$ , and we recover the result of ordinary ridgeless regression [ASS20, HMRT22]

$$
\delta > 1: \quad \lim  _ {\lambda \rightarrow 0} \underset {N, p \rightarrow \infty} {\mathrm {p - l i m}} R _ {\mathrm {e x c}} (\hat {\boldsymbol {\theta}} _ {\lambda}) = \frac {1}{\delta - 1} \cdot \left(C _ {1} - \frac {B _ {1} ^ {2}}{A _ {1}}\right), \tag {6.19}
$$

$$
\delta <   1: \quad \lim _ {\lambda \rightarrow 0} \mathrm {p - l i m} _ {N, p \rightarrow \infty} R _ {\mathrm {e x c}} (\hat {\boldsymbol {\theta}} _ {\lambda}) = B _ {1} ^ {2} (1 - \delta) + \frac {\delta}{\delta - 1} \cdot \left(C _ {1} - \frac {B _ {1} ^ {2}}{A _ {1}}\right). \tag {6.20}
$$

# 7 Numerical results: Synthetic data

In this section we present numerical simulations within the synthetic data model introduced in Section 6.1. We consider the case of binary labels  $y_{i} \in \{+1, -1\}$ . Summarizing, we generate isotropic feature vectors  $\pmb{x}_i \sim \mathsf{N}(0, \pmb{I}_p)$ , and labels with  $\mathbb{P}(y_i = +1 | \pmb{x}_i) = f(\langle \pmb{\theta}_0, \pmb{x}_i \rangle)$ . We then perform data selection, with selection probability

$$
\pi (\boldsymbol {x} _ {i}) = \min  \left(c (\gamma) \phi^ {\prime \prime} \left(\langle \hat {\boldsymbol {\theta}} ^ {\mathrm {s u}}, \boldsymbol {x} _ {i} \rangle\right) ^ {\alpha}; 1\right), \tag {7.1}
$$

where we remind the reader that  $\phi$  is the log-moment generating function. In the binary case  $\phi(t) = \log(2\cosh(t))$  and hence  $\phi''(t) = 1 - \tanh(t)^2$  is a label variance under the logistic model. We fix the constant  $c(\gamma)$ , chosen such that  $\sum_{i \leq N} \pi(\boldsymbol{x}_i) = n$ . We can rewrite the above formula as

$$
\pi (\boldsymbol {x} _ {i}) = \min  \left(c (\gamma) p \left(\langle \hat {\boldsymbol {\theta}} ^ {\mathrm {s u}}, \boldsymbol {x} _ {i} \rangle\right) ^ {\alpha} \left(1 - p \left(\langle \hat {\boldsymbol {\theta}} ^ {\mathrm {s u}}, \boldsymbol {x} _ {i} \rangle\right)\right) ^ {\alpha}; 1\right), \tag {7.2}
$$

where  $p(\langle \hat{\pmb{\theta}}^{\mathrm{su}},\pmb{x}_i\rangle) = (\tanh (\langle \hat{\pmb{\theta}}^{\mathrm{su}},\pmb{x}_i\rangle) + 1) / 2 = (1 + e^{-2\langle \hat{\pmb{\theta}}^{\mathrm{su}},\pmb{x}_i\rangle})^{-1}$  is the probability of  $y_{i} = +1$  under the surrogate model (note: binary labels are defined over  $y_{i}\in \{+1, - 1\}$  and not  $y_{i}\in \{+1,0\}$ ). Hence,  $\alpha >0$  upsamples data points with higher uncertainty under the surrogate model ('difficult' data), while  $\alpha < 0$  upsamples data points with lower uncertainty ('easy' data). We then fit ridge regularized logistic regression to the selected data (cf. Eq. (6.3)) and evaluate misclassification error on a hold-out test set.

It is instructive to compare the above scheme with influence function-based data selection, cf. Example 4.5. Within the present data model, the population Hessian takes the form  $\pmb{H} = a_{+}\pmb{I} + b_{+}\pmb{\theta}_{0}\pmb{\theta}_{0}^{\top} / \|\pmb{\theta}_{0}\|^{2}$  where  $a_{+} = \mathbb{E}\{\phi''(\|\pmb{\theta}_{0}\|G)\}$ ,  $b_{+} = \mathbb{E}\{\phi''(\|\pmb{\theta}_{0}\|G)(G^{2} - 1)\}$ . The score of Example 4.5 (cf. Eq. (4.17)) reads (an overall factor  $da_{-}$  is immaterial and introduced for convenience)

$$
Z (\boldsymbol {x} _ {i}) = \phi^ {\prime \prime} (\langle \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {0}, \boldsymbol {x} _ {i} \rangle) \left\{\frac {\| \boldsymbol {x} _ {i} \| ^ {2}}{d} + b _ {-} \frac {\langle \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {0} , \boldsymbol {x} _ {i} \rangle^ {2}}{d a _ {-} \| \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {0} \| ^ {2}} \right\}, \tag {7.3}
$$

where  $a_{-} = 1 / a_{+}$ ,  $b_{-} = (a_{+} + b_{+})^{-1} - 1 / a_{+}$ . For large dimension  $d$ ,  $\| \pmb{x}_i\|^2 / d = 1 + O_P(1 / \sqrt{d})$ , and  $\langle \pmb{\theta}_0, \pmb{x}_i \rangle = O_P(1)$ . We therefore get

$$
Z (\boldsymbol {x} _ {i}) = \phi^ {\prime \prime} \left(\left\langle \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {0}, \boldsymbol {x} _ {i} \right\rangle\right) \cdot \left(1 + O _ {P} (1 / \sqrt {d})\right). \tag {7.4}
$$

Therefore influence-function based subsampling essentially corresponds to the case  $\alpha = 1/2$  of Eq. (7.1).

Figures 5 and 6 report results of simulations (circles) and theoretical predictions (continuous lines), respectively in a lower-dimensional setting ( $N = 34345$ ,  $p = 932$ ) and in a higher-dimensional setting ( $N = 6870$ ,  $p = 3000$ ). A few remarks are in order:

1. The agreement between theory and simulations is excellent over the whole range of settings we investigated.  
2. Theory correctly predicts that, for a suitable choice of  $\alpha$ , the non-reweighted data selection scheme of Eq. (7.1) achieves nearly identical test error as full data, while using as few as  $40\%$  of the samples.  
3. The optimal choice of  $\alpha$  is highly dependent on the setting, with  $\alpha < 0$  broadly outperforming  $\alpha > 0$  when the number of samples per dimension is smaller (Figure 6).  
4. For small  $\lambda$  and certain choices of  $\delta_0$ ,  $\alpha$  we observe interesting non-monotonicities of the error: smaller sample sizes lead to lower misclassification error. This is of course related to suboptimality of that value of  $\alpha$  and of the scheme (7.1) (see Figure 5). Indeed, as we saw in Section 4.4, the optimal data selection scheme is necessarily monotone in  $n$ .

Finally, while all previous figures use a perfect surrogate, Figure 8 explores the impact of an imperfect surrogate. Namely, we estimate  $\hat{\pmb{\theta}}^{\mathrm{su}}$  by using  $N_{\mathrm{su}}$  independent samples from the same distribution as the training samples. The agreement between theoretical predictions and simulation results is again excellent. Also, we observe behaviors that are qualitatively new with respect to the previous setting that assumes well-specified data and a perfect surrogate. Most notably:

1. Learning after data selection often outperforms learning on the full sample.  
2. Upsampling 'hard' datapoints (i.e. using  $\alpha > 0$ ) is often the optimal strategy. This appears to be more common than in the well-specified case.  
3. As shown in Figure 8, the performance of data selection-based learning degrades gracefully with the quality of the surrogate.  
4. In particular, we observe once more the striking phenomenon of Figure 1, cf. bottom row, rightmost plot of Figure 8. As shown in next section, this effect is even stronger with real data.

# 8 Numerical results: Real data

For our real-data experiments we used an Autonomous Vehicle (AV) dataset, and a binary classification task. As in the synthetic data simulations, we use ridge regularized logistic regression. This model was trained and tested on unsupervised features extracted from image data. Below we first provide details of the dataset and experiments, followed by empirical results.

# 8.1 Dataset

We use a subset of images obtained from the KITTI-360 train set [LXG22]. The KITTI-360 train set comprises  $1408 \times 376$  dimensional 8-bit stereo images, with 2D semantic and instance labels. These images are sourced from 9 distinct continuous driving trajectories. We only consider the left stereo image for our dataset. To adapt this dataset for a binary classification task, we initially center-crop the images to dimensions of  $224 \times 224$  pixels. Subsequently, we assign binary labels, by setting  $y_{i} = +1$  if the count of pixels containing the semantic label in a certain class surpasses

a predefined threshold. We choose 'car' as the label and the pixel cutoff threshold is set at 50, resulting in a chance accuracy of approximately 0.69.

We then extract SwAV embeddings of the images to serve as feature vectors  $\left[\mathrm{CMM}^{+}21\right]$ . We use torchhub.load('facebookresearch/swav:main', 'resnet50') as the base model and we use the 2048 dimensional outputs from the penultimate layer (head) as the SwAV features. Following common practice, we normalize the images before computing feature vectors, using mean and standard deviations of the images calculated on ImageNet. This results in a dataset with a total of  $N = 61,280$  images with  $p = 2048$  dimensional features with binary labels indicating the presence or absence of a car.

# 8.2 Experiments

We randomly partition this dataset into four disjoint sets:  $N_{\mathrm{train}} = 34,345$  images to perform subsampling and train models,  $N_{\mathrm{surr}} = 14,720$  images to train surrogate models,  $N_{\mathrm{val}} = 3665$  images for validation and  $N_{\mathrm{test}} = 8550$  images for reporting the final experiment results. Prior to model training, we center and normalize each of the features using mean and standard deviation calculated from  $N_{\mathrm{train}}$  and  $N_{\mathrm{surr}}$ .

We proceeded by training a ridge-regularized logistic regression model without intercept. The training utilized the L-BFGS optimization algorithm with a cap of 10,000 iterations implemented using the scikit-learn library  $\left[\mathrm{PVG}^{+}11\right]$ . Surrogate models are trained on a fraction  $k$  of the surrogate set  $(N_{\mathrm{sur}})$  where  $k \in \{10\%, 50\%, 100\% \}$  using 5-fold cross validation to choose the regularization parameter  $\lambda$ . Note that, the sample sizes used for these surrogate models correspond to  $\{4.2\%, 21.4\%, 42.8\% \}$  of  $N_{\mathrm{train}}$  (but we use a disjoint set of data).

We use the data selection procedure introduced in the previous section, cf. Eqs. (7.1), (7.2). We show empirical results for  $\alpha \in \{-2, -1, -0.5, 0, 0.5, 1, 2\}$ , where  $\alpha = 0$  corresponds to random subsampling and positive (negative) values of  $\alpha$  correspond to upsampling hard (easy) examples respectively. To ensure numerical stability for negative values of  $\alpha$  modify the definition of  $\pi$  in Eqs. (7.1), by limiting $^{5}$ $\langle \hat{\pmb{\theta}}^{\mathrm{su}}, \pmb{x}_i \rangle$  to be in  $[-10, 10]$ .

We also perform experiments in which we select the  $n$  samples with the largest (or smallest) values of  $p(\langle \hat{\pmb{\theta}}^{\mathrm{su}},\pmb{x}_i\rangle)(1 - p(\langle \hat{\pmb{\theta}}^{\mathrm{su}},\pmb{x}_i\rangle))$ . This corresponds to the limit cases  $\alpha \to \infty$  and  $\alpha \to -\infty$  respectively. We will refer to these limit cases as 'Hard topK' and 'Easy topK', respectively.

# 8.3 Results

All experiments are repeated across five random subsamplings when the data selection scheme is probabilistic and across three different surrogate models when the surrogate model is trained an a strict subset of the  $N_{\mathrm{surr}}$  samples reserved for this.

We vary four different parameters in our experiments:

- The ridge regularization parameter  $\lambda$ . This is either fixed or selected optimally by taking  $\lambda^{*} = \arg \min_{\lambda \in \Lambda} R_{\mathrm{val}}(\hat{\pmb{\theta}}_{\lambda})$ , where  $R_{\mathrm{val}}$  is the risk on the validation set and  $\Lambda := \{0.001, 0.01, 0.03, 0.06, 0.1, 1, 10\}$ .

# 9 Conclusion

Data selection has been studied over the years from several point of views. Both heuristics and mathematically motivated approaches have been put forward, sometimes with conflicting conclusions regarding their effectiveness.

In this paper, we studied data selection within a statistical setting, using both low-dimensional and high-dimensional asymptotics. Our main conclusions are as follows:

1. Restricting to 'unbiased' data selection is unnecessary and sometimes harmful.  
2. Data selection criteria based on the 'uncertainty' associated to the label of a data point are effective. However both upsampling 'hard' and 'easy' datapoints can be beneficial in different cases, and the form of the weights used does matter.  
3. The efficacy of these method is not highly sensitive on the quality of the surrogate.

4. Learning after data selection can outperform learning on the full sample.

5. Finally, we introduce a one-parameter family of data selection schemes, depending on parameter  $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$ , cf. Section 7. By optimizing over  $\alpha$ , we obtain consistently good data selection across a variety of settings.
