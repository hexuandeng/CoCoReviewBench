# Abstract

In the matrix completion problem, one wishes to reconstruct a low-rank matrix based on a revealed set of (possibly noisy) entries. Prior work considers completing the entire matrix, which may be highly inaccurate in the common case where the distribution over entries is non-uniform. We formalize the problem of Partial Matrix Completion where the goal is to complete a large subset of the entries, or equivalently to complete the entire matrix and specify an accurate subset of the entries. Interestingly, even though the distribution is unknown and arbitrarily complex, our efficient algorithm is able to guarantee: (a) high accuracy over all completed entries, and (b) high coverage, meaning that it covers at least as much of the matrix as the distribution of observations.

# 1 Introduction

In the classic matrix-completion problem, a subset of entries of a matrix  $M^{\star} \in [-1, 1]^{n \times m}$  are revealed (with or without noise), and the goal is to reconstruct an approximation  $\hat{M}$ , under some assumptions on  $M^{\star}$ , e.g.  $\text{rank} \leq k$ . For example, the matrix may represent movie ratings with  $M_{ij}^{\star}$  being the rating of user  $i$  on movie  $j$ , as in the classic Netflix challenge. Prior theoretical work considered the goal of completing the entire matrix, which is unrealistic in many cases. In this work, we consider the problem of completing only part of a matrix (or more generally a fractional completion where each entry has a confidence rating in [0, 1]). For simplicity in this introduction, we consider noiseless matrix completion where the observed values are equal to the true matrix, but our analysis covers noise as well.

Of course, the reconstruction accuracy cannot be guaranteed everywhere. As a trivial example, suppose half the users did not watch any movies, so the entire bottom of the matrix is empty and impossible to complete with any accuracy. In this case, our algorithm would not complete that part of the matrix. As a slightly harder example, suppose users and movies come from two countries  $A$  and  $B$ , and each user rates random movies only from their country. Without any cross ratings of users from country  $A$  on movies from country  $B$  or vice versa, it is impossible to accurately complete these entries. The solution here is straightforward: partition the users and movies into their respective groups and then complete only the part of the matrix corresponding to user ratings of movies from their country. But what if we do not know these groups in advance, or even the number of different groups? Our goal in this paper is to complete only those entries in which we have high confidence. We are able to handle arbitrary sampling distributions, which captures scenarios such as overlapping groups or idiosyncratic rating habits.

In the partial matrix completion problem, one is given (possibly noisy) observations from a low-rank matrix, and the goal is to complete a subset  $C$  of the entries of the matrix such that one has low completion error $^2$  over  $C$ , defined as  $\frac{1}{|C|}\sum_{ij\in C}(M_{ij}^{\star} - \hat{M}_{ij})^{2}\leq \varepsilon$ . - Based on samples from an arbitrary unknown sampling distribution, how many entries can be completed with  $\leq \varepsilon$  error?  
- How can we efficiently find the entries that can be accurately completed?

Challenges of partial matrix completion. Key challenges are illustrated by two intuitive but naive approaches. First, consider completing just those matrix entries  $(i,j)$  where all rank- $k$  completions  $\hat{M}$  (with low error on the observed entries) have nearly the same value  $\hat{M}_{ij}$ . Even in a simple case where all revealed entries are 1, as in Figure 1, any single entry could be  $\pm 1$  for some rank 2 completion of the matrix. Based on this, one may fear that one cannot complete any entries at all.

Second, consider the generalization of our previous example where the sampling distribution is uniform over an arbitrary subset  $U$  of  $n \times m$  possible indices. For simplicity, think of  $|U| = mn / 2$  as covering half the matrix. As mentioned above, previous work guarantees that one could accurately complete all of  $U$ . However, in general  $U$  is unknown and arbitrarily complex. The second naive approach would be to try to learn  $U$ , but this requires  $\approx |U| = mn / 2 \neq O(k(m + n))$  observations.

# 1.1 Partial matrix completion algorithms and guarantees

Our main focus on this work is designing algorithms that decide which entries to complete. We can use any of a number of prior matrix completion algorithms (black-box) to find a completion  $\hat{M}$ , that satisfies  $\mathbb{E}_{(i,j)\sim \mu}[(M_{ij}^{\star} - \hat{M}_{ij})^{2}] \leq \varepsilon$  where  $\mu$  is the sampling distribution over  $[n] \times [m]$ . We focus on finding a subset  $C$ , represented now as a matrix itself,  $C \in \{0,1\}^{n \times m}$ , where  $|C| \doteq \sum C_{ij}$  is large and  $\frac{1}{|C|} \sum_{ij} C_{ij}(M_{ij}^{\star} - \hat{M}_{ij})^{2} \leq \varepsilon$ . Although our results are more general, in the introduction we will assume that  $\mu$  is uniform over some arbitrary subset  $U \subseteq [n] \times [m]$ .

We present an inefficient algorithm and an efficient relaxation of it, with strong accuracy and completion guarantees. Both algorithms take a set of observations as well as a target accuracy  $\varepsilon > 0$  as a parameter. However, the value of  $\varepsilon$  can be chosen based on the sample size and bounds from prior work [e.g., 18, 16]. The inefficient algorithm, with high probability, completes  $|C| \geq |U|$  entries and achieves average error  $\leq \varepsilon$  over those entries, using the same number  $\tilde{O}\left((m + n)k / \varepsilon^2\right)$  of observed samples as required in prior work. Thus, an answer to the question of how many entries can be completed is at least  $|U|$ . Moreover, it can be seen that one cannot always accurately complete more than  $|U|$  entries (e.g., if  $U$  covers the left half of the matrix). The guarantee of Corollary 1

(simplification of Thm. 8) applies to arbitrary set sizes  $|U|$  and extends naturally to arbitrary sampling distributions.

Corollary 1 (Inefficient algorithm). Let  $M^{\star} \in [-1,1]^{n \times m}$  be a rank- $k$  matrix and let the sampling distribution  $\mu$  be the uniform over an arbitrary subset  $U \subseteq [m] \times [n]$  of entries. Then, for any  $\varepsilon, \delta > 0$ , with probability  $\geq 1 - \delta$  over  $N \geq c_0 \cdot \frac{k(m + n) + \log(1 / \delta)}{\varepsilon^2}$  (for some constant  $c_0$ ) revealed entries from  $M^{\star}$  drawn from  $\mu$ , Algorithm 1 (Sec. 3) outputs  $\hat{M}$  and  $C \in \{0,1\}^{n \times m}$ , such that  $|C| \geq |U|$  and

$$
\frac {1}{| C |} \sum_ {i j} C _ {i j} (M _ {i j} ^ {\star} - \hat {M} _ {i j}) ^ {2} \leq \varepsilon .
$$

The second algorithm (Alg. 2) requires more samples, up to polynomial factors in  $k, \varepsilon$ . It also outputs a fractional completion with  $C_{ij} \in [0,1]$ , though randomized rounding can convert it to a set. The advantage is that it runs in polynomial time. Otherwise, its guarantees are similar to that of the first algorithm. Corollary 2 below is a simplification of Theorem 10.

Corollary 2 (Efficient algorithm). Let  $M^{\star} \in [-1,1]^{n \times m}$  be a rank- $k$  matrix and sampling distribution  $\mu$  be the uniform over an arbitrary subset  $U \subseteq [m] \times [n]$  of entries. Then, for any  $\varepsilon, \delta > 0$ , with probability  $\geq 1 - \delta$  over  $N \geq c_0 \cdot \frac{k^2}{\varepsilon^4} \cdot (k(m + n) + \log(1 / \delta))$  (for some constant  $c_0$ ) revealed entries from  $M$ , Algorithm 2 (Sec. 4) outputs  $\hat{M}$  and  $C \in [0,1]^{n \times m}$ , such that both  $|C| \geq |U|$  and

$$
\frac {1}{\sum_ {i j} C _ {i j}} \sum_ {i j} C _ {i j} (M _ {i j} ^ {\star} - \hat {M} _ {i j}) ^ {2} \leq \varepsilon .
$$

The results can be extended to the setting where the observed entries are noisy with zero-mean bounded noise. A remarkable feature of our algorithms is that once the full completion  $\hat{M}$  is obtained using existing procedures, they only rely on the locations of observed entries and not the values. Thus, the decisions regarding which entries to complete, i.e., to add to  $C$  is completely agnostic to the actual values of the revealed entries.

# 1.2 Related work

Matrix completion and recommendation systems. The most common approach to collaborative filtering is that of matrix completion with trace norm minimization [17, 1]. It was shown that the trace norm regularization requires  $\Omega((n + m)^{3/2})$  observations to complete the matrix under arbitrary distributions, even when the rank of the underlying matrix is constant, and this is sufficient even in the more challenging agnostic online learning setting [11, 16]. Srebro and Shraibman [18] study matrix completion with the max-norm, which behaves better under arbitrary distributions, namely for low-rank matrices,  $\tilde{O}(n + m)$  observations suffice.

Randomized rounding and semi-definite relaxations. For the efficient algorithm, the main analysis technique we deploy is based on randomized rounding solutions of semi-definite programming, due to the seminal work of Goemans and Williamson [7]. These were originally developed in the context of approximation algorithms for MAX-CUT and other combinatorial problems. In this work we use this analysis technique for analyzing inner products of high dimensional vectors, but in a different context and to argue about a convex relaxation of a continuous optimization problem.

Abstention in classification and regression. Abstaining from prediction has a long history in other learning problems such as binary classification [2]. There are several notable similarities. First, many of the algorithms work as ours does, by first fitting a complete classifier and then deciding afterwards where to abstain [e.g., 12, 8]. Second, numerous abstention models have been considered, including a fixed cost for abstaining [e.g., 2, 13] as well as fixing the error rate and maximizing the coverage [e.g., 5] as in our work, and fixing the coverage rate while minimizing the error [e.g., 6]. Third, there are algorithmic similarities, in particular the observation that worst-case performance guarantees do not depend on knowing the underlying distribution Kalai and Kanade [13]. Online models of abstention have also been considered [e.g., 14]. For detailed survey on selective classification see [3].

# 2 Problem Setup and Preliminaries

For a natural number  $n$ , let  $[n] = \{1, 2, \dots, n\}$ . Consider a fixed set of indices  $\mathcal{X} = [n] \times [m]$  throughout (e.g.,  $n$  users and  $m$  movies). Thus, in this paper  $x \in \mathcal{X}$  is  $x = (i, j)$  where  $i \in [n], j \in [m]$ . The set of  $n \times m$  real-valued matrices is thus written as  $\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}$ . We will thus view a matrix  $M$  over index set  $\mathcal{X}$  as a function from  $\mathcal{X} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ , and for  $x \in \mathcal{X}$ , by slight abuse of notation denote by both  $M_x$  and  $M(x)$  the entry of  $M$  at index  $x$ .

We focus on the squared loss in this paper, though it may be interesting to consider other loss functions in future work. Suppose  $\nu$  is a distribution over  $\mathcal{X}$ , for matrices,  $M, M'$ , we denote by  $\ell(\nu, M, M') \doteq \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{X}}[(M(x) - M'(x))^2]$ ; we will also use the shorthand  $\|M - M'\|_{\nu}^2 \doteq \ell(\nu, M, M')$ . When  $T \in \mathcal{X}^N$  is a sequence of elements of  $\mathcal{X}$  of length  $N$ , we denote by  $\ell(T, M, M') \doteq (1/N)\sum_{t=1}^{N}(M(x_t) - M'(x_t))^2$  and the shorthand  $\|M - M'\|_T^2 \doteq \ell(T, M, M')$ .

# 2.1 Matrix Classes, Version Space and Generalization Bounds

Matrix Norms. Given a matrix  $M$ , we denote by  $\| M \|_{2,\infty}$  the maximum row norm of  $M$ . We denote by  $\| M \|_{\max}$  the max-norm of  $M$  defined as,

$$
\| M \| _ {\max } \doteq \min  _ {U V ^ {\top} = M} \| U \| _ {2, \infty}, \| V \| _ {2, \infty}.
$$

The trace norm of a matrix  $M$ , denoted by  $\| M \|_{\mathrm{tr}}$ , is the sum of its singular values. The Frobenius norm of a matrix  $M$ , denoted by  $\| M \|_{\mathrm{fr}}$ , is the square root of the sum of its squared entries.

We will restrict attention to matrices with entries in  $[-1, 1]$ . The following three classes of matrices with restrictions on respectively the rank, max-norm and trace-norm are of interest in this work. Formally, let  $\tilde{\mathcal{K}} = \{M \in [-1, 1]^{\mathcal{X}}\}$ , and define,

$$
\mathcal {M} _ {k} ^ {\mathrm {r k}} \doteq \{\operatorname {r a n k} (M) \leq k \} \cap \tilde {\mathcal {K}}, \mathcal {M} _ {K} ^ {\max } \doteq \{\| M \| _ {\max } \leq K \} \cap \tilde {\mathcal {K}}, \mathcal {M} _ {\tau} ^ {\mathrm {t r}} \doteq \{\| M \| _ {\mathrm {t r}} \leq \tau \} \cap \tilde {\mathcal {K}}
$$

The standard assumption in matrix completion is that the target matrix is low rank; however, most completion algorithms exploit convex optimization methods and optimize over max-norm or trace-norm bounded matrices. It is known that  $\| M\|_{\max}\leq \sqrt{\mathrm{rank}(M)}$  if  $M\in [-1,1]^{\mathcal{X}}$  (cf. Lemma 4.2 in [15]) and it is easy to see that  $\| M\|_{\mathrm{tr}}\leq \mathrm{rank}(M)\sqrt{mn}$  for  $M\in [-1,1]^{\mathcal{X}}$ . Thus,  $\mathcal{M}_k^{\mathrm{rk}}\subseteq \mathcal{M}_{\sqrt{k}}^{\mathrm{max}}$  and  $\mathcal{M}_k^{\mathrm{rk}}\subseteq \mathcal{M}_{k\sqrt{mn}}^{\mathrm{tr}}$ . Hence, we will work with the latter two classes which satisfy the following lemma which is proved in Appendix B.

Lemma 3. The classes,  $\mathcal{M}_K^{\mathrm{max}}$  and  $\mathcal{M}_{\tau}^{\mathrm{tr}}$ , are closed under negations and are convex.

Version Space. We define the notion of version spaces that are used in our key results. For a sequence  $T \in \mathcal{X}^N$ , for any class of matrices  $\mathcal{M}$ , a matrix  $M \in \mathcal{M}$  and  $\beta > 0$ , we define the version space,

$$
\mathcal {V} (M, \beta , T; \mathcal {M}) = \left\{M ^ {\prime} \in \mathcal {M} \mid \| M - M ^ {\prime} \| _ {T} ^ {2} \leq \beta \right\}. \tag {1}
$$

Intuitively, this notion of version space is set of all matrices that are "close" to a given matrix with respect to a  $T$ .

Generalization Bounds. For a general class of matrices  $\mathcal{M}$ , we define a notion of sample complexity that will guarantee the proximity of empirical and population measures of interest for all matrices in the class; we denote this notion by  $\operatorname{sc}(\varepsilon, \delta, \mathcal{M})$ .

Definition 4 (Sample Complexity). For  $\varepsilon, \delta > 0$  and a class of matrices  $\mathcal{M}$ , denote by  $\operatorname{sc}(\varepsilon, \delta, \mathcal{M})$ , the sample complexity, to be the smallest natural number  $N_0$ , such that for any distribution  $\mu$  over  $\mathcal{X}$ , for  $S \sim \mu^{N_0}$ , and for any fixed  $\hat{M} \in \mathcal{M}$  (possibly depending on  $S$ ), with prob. at least  $1 - \delta$ ,

$$
\sup  _ {M \in \mathcal {M}} \left| \ell (S, M, \hat {M}) - \ell (\mu , M, \hat {M}) \right| \leq \varepsilon .
$$

If no such  $N_0$  exists,  $\mathfrak{sc}(\varepsilon ,\delta ,\mathcal{F})\dot{=} \infty$

Bounds on the sample complexity for matrix completion can be derived in terms of rank, max-norm and trace-norm using standard results in the literature, and the fact that the squared loss is 2-Lipschitz and bounded by 4 when both its arguments take values in  $[-1,1]$ . In the proposition below, the max-norm result follows from Theorem 5 in [18] and the trace-norm result follows from Theorem 4 in [16].

Proposition 5. For the classes,  $\mathcal{M}_K^{\mathrm{max}}$ ,  $\mathcal{M}_{\tau}^{\mathrm{tr}}$ , the following hold,

1.  $\mathfrak{sc}(\varepsilon, \delta, \mathcal{M}_K^{\max}) = O\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^2}\left(K^2(m + n) + \log \frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right)$ .  
2.  $\mathfrak{sc}(\varepsilon, \delta, \mathcal{M}_{\tau}^{\mathrm{tr}}) = O\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\tau \sqrt{m + n} + \log \frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right)$ .

It is worth comparing the two bounds in Proposition 5 above. Consider the matrix  $M^{\star}$  consisting of all 1's - from a matrix completion point of view this is particularly easy as every "user" likes every "movie". Note however that  $\| M^{\star}\|_{\max} = 1$  and  $\| M^{\star}\|_{\mathrm{tr}} = \sqrt{mn}$ . For this example, ignoring the dependence on  $\varepsilon, \delta$ , the sample complexity bound obtained using the trace-norm result would be  $O((m + n)\sqrt{m + n})$ , while that using max-norm would be  $O(m + n)$ . In general, it is always the case that  $\| M\|_{\mathrm{tr}} / \sqrt{mn} \leq \| M\|_{\max}$ , so it may seem that the bounds in terms of trace-norm are weaker. However, there are matrices for which this gap can be large and the sample complexity bound in terms of trace-norm is shown to be tight in general (see [18, 16] for further details).

# 2.2 Full Completion Problem (with Noise)

We now define the matrix completion problem with zero-mean noise, in fact a matrix estimation problem in the noisy case. Let  $\bar{D}$  be a distribution supported on  $\mathcal{X} \times [-1,1]$ ; the results in the paper can all be easily extended when  $D$  is supported on  $\mathcal{X} \times [-B,B]$ , increasing squared error by a  $B^2$  factor. Let  $\mu$  be the marginal distribution of  $D$  over  $\mathcal{X}$ . Let  $S_{XY} = \langle (x_t,y_t)\rangle_{t=1}^N$  be an iid sample drawn from  $D^N$ . Note here that  $x_t = (i_t,j_t)$  denotes the index of the matrix and  $y_t$  the observed value. We let  $S = (x_1,\ldots,x_N)$  denote the sequence of  $x_t$ 's from  $S_{XY}$  (with repetitions allowed); note that  $S$  is distributed as  $\mu^N$ . Let  $M^\star \in [-1,1]^{\mathcal{X}}$  be the matrix where  $M_{ij}^\star = \mathbb{E}_D[Y|X = (i,j)]$ . We say that  $\hat{M}$  is an  $\varepsilon$ -accurate completion of  $M^\star$ , if  $\ell(\mu,M^\star,\hat{M}) \leq \varepsilon$ .

Definition 6 (Full Completion Algorithm). We say that  $\mathrm{FullComp}(S_{XY},\varepsilon ,\delta ,\mathcal{M})$  is a full completion algorithm with sample complexity  $\mathfrak{sc}_{\mathsf{FC}}(\varepsilon ,\delta ,\mathcal{M})$  for  $\mathcal{M}$ , if provided  $S_{XY}\sim D^{N}$  for some  $D$  over  $\mathcal{X}\times [-1,1]$  with  $M^{\star}\in \mathcal{M}$ ,  $N\geq \mathsf{sc}_{\mathsf{FC}}(\varepsilon ,\delta ,\mathcal{M})$ ,  $\mathrm{FullComp}(S_{XY},\varepsilon ,\delta ,\mathcal{M})$  outputs  $\hat{M}\in \mathcal{M}$  that with probability at least  $1 - \delta$  satisfies,  $\ell (\mu ,M^{\star},\hat{M})$ .

The following result follows from [18, 16] (see also Prop. 5).

Proposition 7. There exists polynomial time (in  $mn / \varepsilon$ ) full completion algorithms for  $\mathcal{M}_K^{\max}$  and  $\mathcal{M}_{\tau}^{\mathrm{tr}}$  (e.g. ERM methods) with the following sample complexity bounds:

1. For  $\mathcal{M}_K^{\mathrm{max}}$ ,  $\mathfrak{sc}_{\mathsf{FC}}(\varepsilon, \delta, \mathcal{M}_K^{\mathrm{max}}) = O\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^2}\left(K^2(m + n) + \log \frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right)$ .  
2. For  $\mathcal{M}_{\tau}^{\mathrm{tr}}$ $\mathfrak{sc}_{\mathcal{M}_{\tau}^{\mathrm{tr}}}(\varepsilon, \delta, \mathcal{M}_{\tau}^{\mathrm{tr}}) = O\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\tau \sqrt{m + n} + \log \frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right)$ .

# 2.3 The Partial Matrix Completion Problem: Maximizing Coverage

In this work, we will assume that a full completion matrix  $\hat{M}$  is already obtained. The focus of the present work is finding a completion matrix  $C\in \{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}$  (or  $C\in [0,1]^{\mathcal{X}}$  for fractional coverage) with large coverage, defined as:

$$
| C | \doteq \sum_ {x \in \mathcal {X}} C _ {x},
$$

and a low loss, defined as:

$$
\ell (C, M ^ {\star}, \hat {M}) \doteq \frac {1}{| C |} \sum_ {x \in \mathcal {X}} C _ {x} \left(M _ {x} ^ {\star} - \hat {M} _ {x}\right) ^ {2}.
$$

Note that  $C \in [0,1]^{\mathcal{X}}$  can be viewed as a "fractional" set and we are overloading the notation  $\ell$  to allow that. Such a fractional coverage can be randomly rounded to a set whose size is within 1 of

$|C|$ . In an ideal world, we would have at most  $\varepsilon$  loss measured over the cells we complete as defined above, and large or even full coverage  $|C| = mn$ .

The Partial Matrix Completion Problem:

Given a training sample of observations  $S \sim \mu^{N}$ , a full completion  $\hat{M}$ , find a matrix  $C \in [0,1]^{\mathcal{X}}$  with as large a coverage as possible given a specific loss bound  $\varepsilon$ :

$$
\underset {C \in [ 0, 1 ] ^ {\mathcal {X}}} {\arg \max } | C | \text {s . t .} \ell (C, M ^ {\star}, \hat {M}) \leq \epsilon . \tag {2}
$$

Note that although the requirement above is to guarantee  $\ell(C, M^{\star}, M) \leq \varepsilon$ , as we don't know  $M^{\star}$ , the only guarantee we have by using a full completion algorithm (and generalization bounds) is that (whp)  $M^{\star}$  is in some version space, say  $\mathcal{V}$ , centered at  $\hat{M}$ . So we actually show a stronger guarantee that,  $\sup_{M \in \mathcal{V}} \ell(C, M, \hat{M}) \leq \varepsilon$ . A second observation is that if  $M$  is a version space around  $\hat{M}$ , then by convexity of the matrix classes, the matrix  $(M - \hat{M})/2$ , must be in some version space, say  $\mathcal{V}_0$ , centered around the zero matrix,  $\mathbf{0}_{\mathcal{X}}$ . Thus, we will actually find a  $C$  that guarantees,  $\ell(C, M, \mathbf{0}_{\mathcal{X}}) \leq \varepsilon$  for every  $M \in \mathcal{V}_0$ . This means that for maximizing coverage our algorithm has the remarkable property we only need to know the locations of the revealed entries as indicated by  $S$ .

Section 3 presents a computationally inefficient but statistically superior algorithm for the coverage problem and its consequences for partial matrix completion. Section 4 presents a computationally efficient algorithm at the cost of a slightly worse sample complexity when using the class  $\mathcal{M}_K^{\mathrm{max}}$ .

# 3 Maximizing Coverage: An Inefficient Algorithm

The main novelty in the partial matrix completion problem is that of finding an optimal coverage, defined as the matrix  $C$  in formulation (2). In this section we give an (inefficient) algorithm for finding the optimal coverage, and the generalization error bounds for Partial Matrix Completion that arise from it. In the next section we give an efficient approximation algorithm for doing the same, with slightly worse sample complexity bounds and more complex analysis. This differs substantially from prior work on abstention in classification and regression [13] where the optimal solution can be found in polynomial time.

# 4.1 Proof of Proposition 11

For succinctness let  $\mathcal{V}_0 = \mathcal{V}(\mathbf{0}_{\mathcal{X}},\beta ;S,\mathcal{M}_K^{\max})$ . Let  $\nu$  be an arbitrary distribution over  $\mathcal{X}$  and since we know that  $\mathcal{V}_0$  is closed and bounded, we know that a matrix  $M\in \mathcal{V}_0$  exists which achieves the value of  $\sup_{M\in \mathcal{V}_0}\| M\|_\nu^2$ . We will use the probabilistic method to show that there exists  $\tilde{M}\in \mathcal{V}_0$  such that,

$$
\mathbb {E} _ {x \sim \nu} [ \tilde {M} _ {x} ] \geq \frac {2}{\pi K} \| M \| _ {\nu} ^ {2}.
$$

Since  $M \in \mathcal{M}_K^{\max}$ , there exist  $U, V$  such that  $M = UV^\top$  and that  $\| U \|_{2,\infty} \cdot \| V \|_{2,\infty} \leq K$ .

Denote by  $u_{i}, v_{j}$  respectively the  $i$ -th and  $j$ -th rows of  $U$  and  $V$ . Suppose  $u_{i}, v_{j} \in \mathbb{R}^{D}$  for some finite  $D$ , we know that we can always choose  $D \leq \min\{m, n\}$ . Let  $w$  be a random vector drawn uniformly from the unit sphere in  $\mathbb{R}^{D}$ . For any vector  $v \in \mathbb{R}^{D}$ , let  $\tilde{v} \doteq \operatorname{sign}(v^{\top}w)v$  and obtain the matrices  $\tilde{U}, \tilde{V}$  from  $U$  and  $V$  by applying this transformation to all the rows of  $U$  and  $V$ .

We define  $\tilde{M} = \tilde{U}\tilde{V}^{\top}$ . Note that as the only difference between  $U$  and  $\tilde{U}$  (resp.  $V$  and  $\tilde{V}$ ) is sign changes for some subset of the rows, we have  $\| \tilde{U}\|_{2,\infty} = \| U\|_{2,\infty}$  (resp.  $\| \tilde{V}\|_{2,\infty} = \| V\|_{2,\infty}$ ). Also for any  $i,j$ ,  $|u_i^\top v_j| = |\tilde{u}_i^\top \tilde{v}_j|$ , thus the entries of  $M$  and  $\tilde{M}$  may only differ in sign. Thus,  $\tilde{M} \in \mathcal{M}_K^{\max}$  irrespective of the random choice of the vector  $w$ .

In order to complete the proof by the probabilistic method, it suffices to show that,

$$
\mathbb {E} _ {w} \left[ \mathbb {E} _ {x \sim \nu} [ \tilde {M} _ {x} ] \right] \geq \frac {2}{\pi K} \| M \| _ {\nu} ^ {2}. \tag {5}
$$

For any two vectors  $u, v \in \mathbb{R}^D$ , let  $\theta(u, v)$  denote the angle between them. For a vector  $w$  drawn at random from the unit sphere it can be verified that  $\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{sign}(\tilde{u}^\top \tilde{v}) \neq \mathrm{sign}(u^\top v)] = \frac{\theta(u, v)}{\pi}$ .

For any vectors  $u, v \in \mathbb{R}^D$ , we will show that,

$$
\mathbb {E} _ {w} [ \tilde {u} ^ {\top} \tilde {v} ] \geq \frac {(u ^ {\top} v) ^ {2}}{\pi \| u \| \| v \|}. \tag {6}
$$

It suffices to show the above for the case when  $u^{\top}v \geq 0$  as flipping the sign of  $v$  affects neither  $\tilde{v}$  nor  $(u^{\top}v)^{2}$ . Note that in this case  $\theta(u,v) \in [-\pi/2, \pi/2]$ . To prove Eq. (6), we use:

Lemma 12. For any  $\theta \in [-\pi /2,\pi /2]$  , we have that,  $1 - 2\theta /\pi \geq 2\cos (\theta) / \pi$

Proof. Let  $f:[-\pi /2,\pi /2]\to \mathbb{R}$ , where  $f(\theta) = 1 - 2\theta /\pi -2\cos \theta /\pi$ . Note that since  $|\sin \theta |\leq 1$  for all  $\theta$ ,  $f^{\prime}(\theta)\leq 0$ , so  $f$  is decreasing and  $f(\pi /2) = 0$ .

Now, using the fact that  $\cos (\theta (u,v)) = u^{\top}v / (\| u\| \| v\|)$ , we get,

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathbb {E} _ {w} [ \tilde {u} ^ {\top} \tilde {v} ] = u ^ {\top} v \cdot \left(1 - 2 \mathbb {P} [ \operatorname {s i g n} (\tilde {u} ^ {\top} \tilde {v}) \neq \operatorname {s i g n} (u ^ {\top} v) ]\right) = u ^ {\top} v \cdot \left(1 - \frac {2 \theta (u , v)}{\pi}\right) \\ \geq u ^ {\top} v \cdot \frac {2 \cos (\theta (u , v))}{\pi} = \frac {2 (u ^ {\top} v) ^ {2}}{\pi \| u \| \| v \|}. \quad \text {U s i n g L e m m a 1 2} \\ \end{array}
$$

For any fixed  $x = (i,j)\in \mathcal{X}$  , we have that,

$$
\mathbb {E} _ {w} [ \tilde {M} _ {x} ] = \mathbb {E} _ {w} [ \tilde {u} _ {i} ^ {\top} \tilde {v} _ {i} ] \geq \frac {2 (u _ {i} ^ {\top} v) ^ {2}}{\pi \| u _ {i} \| \| v _ {j} \|} \geq \frac {2 M _ {x} ^ {2}}{\pi K}.
$$

The last inequality follows from the fact that  $\| M\|_{\max} = \| U\|_{2,\infty}\cdot \| V\|_{2,\infty}\leq K$  and that  $M_x = u_i^\top v_j$ . Taking expectation with respect to  $\nu$  and applying Fubini's theorem establishes (5).

# 5 Conclusions and Limitations

In this work, given non-uniform random samples of an  $m \times n$  rank- $k$  matrix, we show how to complete a significant fraction of the matrix (as much as was covered by the underlying distribution), so as to guarantee low error. The number of samples required is only  $O\big(k(m + n)\big)$  out of  $mn$  entries. The only assumptions required are that the rank of the underlying matrix is low and the samples are iid. A primary limitation of our analysis is that it holds under a realizable assumption, that the noise is zero mean. It would be interesting to extend the work to an "agnostic" setting where the matrix is not required to be low rank, and yet one competes with the best low-rank model. Another limitation of this work is that it is entirely theoretical—we have not implemented the algorithm in practice. Evaluating PMC may be difficult on standard matrix completion datasets. In evaluating a matrix completion algorithm, one simply samples the dataset and measures error on the remainder. For PMC, measuring the error on the completed entries may involve collecting additional ratings (e.g., asking users to watch movies). One negative potential impact of PMC is that some users may receive fewer predicted entries than other users, which may be a type of harm. In some situations, though, it may be more beneficial not to complete entries than to offer inaccurate recommendations.
